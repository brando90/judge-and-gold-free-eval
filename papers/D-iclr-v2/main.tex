\title{The Reference Is Not an Answer Key:\\LLM Judges Favor the Correct Answer They Are Shown}

\author{Anonymous authors\\Paper under double-blind review}

\begin{document}
\maketitle
\raggedbottom

\begin{abstract}
LLM judges often grade with a reference answer, including the graders that ship with SimpleQA, BrowseComp, and Humanity's Last Exam, on the premise that it acts as an answer key: a correct response should score the same whichever correct answer is shown.
On questions with several correct answers the premise fails.
We separate three readings of a reference (an answer key, the only evidence for a judge that cannot verify, or an answer the judge rewards for matching) and find that (1) swapping the reference between two correct answers moves the score of identical text by about 3 to 5 points out of 10; (2) correct answers that differ from the reference are rejected far more often, even ones the judge accepts without a reference, so verification failure alone cannot explain the effect; and (3) those benchmarks rarely contain such questions (0\% to 3\% of those we sampled), but run verbatim with their own judge models on 149 factual questions that do, their graders score four models 30 to 67 points below their answer-list accuracy.
The size follows the grader's instruction: asked whether the answer is correct instead of whether it matches, judges fall 0.5 to 17 points short, and given the full answer list the benchmark graders come within a point of it.
We test these predictions across eleven judges on constructed tasks, MACE, and MATH-500, with a complete preregistration ledger.
On such questions, a low score from a reference-guided judge does not reliably mean the answer is wrong.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Ask for a two-digit number that is divisible by 5 and whose digits sum to 8.
Both 35 and 80 are correct.
Qwen2.5-7B, shown a candidate that reasons its way to 80, scores it 10.0 out of 10 with no reference, 2.9 when the reference answer is 35, and 9.3 when the reference is 80 (\cref{fig:concept}b).
The text never changed; only the correct answer the judge was shown did.
This example swaps more than most, but over 120 such tasks the same judge moves correct text by about 5 points in either direction (\cref{fig:concept}c).

Reference-guided judging is how many benchmarks are scored.
SimpleQA's grader marks a response incorrect when ``a factual statement in the answer contradicts the gold target''~\citep{wei2024simpleqa}; the judge used by BrowseComp and Humanity's Last Exam (HLE) decides correctness ``based on the precise and unambiguous [correct\_answer]'' and is told ``do not argue for any answer different than [correct\_answer]''~\citep{wei2025browsecomp,phan2025hle}; MT-Bench's math and reasoning template tells the judge to ``Begin your evaluation by comparing the assistant's answer with the reference answer''~\citep{zheng2023judging}.
Of 43 prominent benchmarks, evaluation libraries, cloud platforms, and RL reward pipelines we checked, 35 grade with an LLM judge given one reference or a closed list, and we found 34 recent research papers that do the same; 27 of the 34 judge prompts we could read define correctness as matching the reference, some call the reference ``ALWAYS correct''~\citep{gao2024omnimath} or tell the judge to ``NEVER QUESTION'' it~\citep{liu2025compassverifier}, and none tells the judge to credit a correct answer outside it (\cref{app:survey}).\ph
The same judges choose among sampled answers and serve as reward signals, which optimization exploits~\citep{gao2022scaling}.
The practice is well-motivated when a question has one correct answer, as on curated math benchmarks: our LLM-labeled audit of 200 questions per dataset found 0\% to 2.5\% with several on MATH-500, GSM8K, and DeepScaleR\ph{} (\cref{app:prevalence}).
Benchmarks written for one answer also rarely have several (0\% to 4\% of sampled SimpleQA, BrowseComp, HLE, and TriviaQA questions after adjudication; up to 15\% by either annotator), but reference-guided graders are also used where answers are not unique: 5.5\% of questions in an RL training set whose verifier sees one reference~\citep{ma2025generalreasoner} and 30\% of NQ-open questions\ph{} admit several, as do benchmarks such as \textsc{RewardBench-2} Ties and \textsc{MACE}~\citep{malik2025rewardbench2,mace2026}.
The failure is not about math: factual answers, agent outputs, informal proofs, and RL rewards on general reasoning data have no formal checker, which is why LLM judges grade them. We use arithmetic tasks only as a controlled testbed, because a checker lists every correct answer, and factual questions because benchmark graders operate there; on code, where correct programs have few distinct outputs, we found no effect (\cref{app:code}). We ask how much the premise matters when it fails, and how to fix graders.

\paragraph{Three readings of a reference.}
If the reference is an \emph{answer key}, the judge checks a candidate against every correct answer, and a correct answer scores the same whichever one is shown.
Under \emph{verification failure}, the judge cannot tell on its own that a different answer is correct, so the reference is its only evidence.
Under \emph{agreement}, the judge favors the correct answer that matches the reference even when it can recognize the others, whether because it rewards agreement, because it assumes one intended answer, or because the prompt tells it to use the reference; we test the last of these only partly (\cref{sec:cost,sec:fix}).
We test three predictions. P1 and P2 rule out the answer key; P3 tests for an agreement component beyond verification failure.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=2pt]
\item[\textbf{P1}] \textbf{The score follows the reference.} Swapping which correct answer is the reference moves the score of unchanged text (\cref{sec:p1}).
\item[\textbf{P2}] \textbf{Correct answers that differ from the reference are graded worse.} With several correct answers, they lose ground against wrong answers; with one, every reading allows the reference to help (\cref{sec:p2}).
\item[\textbf{P3}] \textbf{The effect persists after blind acceptance.} It remains for correct answers the judge already accepts without a reference, which verification failure alone does not predict (\cref{sec:p3}).
\end{itemize}
\Cref{tab:summary} gives what we observed. All three predictions hold; P3 indicates an agreement component beyond verification failure for all six judges with a usable acceptance test, and on factual questions for four of them from three developers. The effect matters in practice: run as their authors configure them, the graders of SimpleQA, BrowseComp, and HLE credit 0\% to 53\% of correct answers that differ from the reference and score four models 30 to 67 points below their answer-list accuracy (\cref{sec:cost}). Much of this comes from the instruction to match: asking GPT-4.1 and Sonnet~5 whether the answer is correct leaves 10 to 17 points, and 0.5 to 6 when they reason first (\cref{sec:cost}). Giving the judge the full answer list removes the shortfall and restores grading (\cref{sec:fix}).

Prior work comes close without isolating this failure.
\citet{fixation2025} name ``solution fixation'' but measure it with flawed solutions rewritten in the reference's style; \citet{judgingref2026} and \citet{krumdick2025nofreelabels} replace the reference with a wrong one; \citet{kapetanovic2026anchoring} show judges anchor on scores they are shown.

\section{A model of the three readings}
\label{sec:model}

Let $A^\star$ be the set of correct answers to a question and $a^\star \in A^\star$ the one shown as the reference. Let $b(c)$ be a judge's blind score for candidate $c$, $s(c; a^\star)$ its referenced score, and $v_c \in [0, 1]$ how reliably the judge recognizes $c$ as correct without a reference. We write
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
s(c; a^\star) \;\approx\;& \beta\, b(c) \;+\; \kappa\, \mathbb{I}[\,\mathrm{ans}(c) \in A^\star\,] \;+\; \lambda(v_c)\, \mathbb{I}[\,\mathrm{ans}(c) = a^\star\,] \\
&+\; \gamma\, \mathbb{I}[\,c \text{ is wrong and contains } a^\star\,] \;+\; \varepsilon .
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:model}
\end{equation}
An answer key checks candidates against the whole set: $\kappa \ge 0$ and $\lambda = \gamma = 0$. The other two readings reward matching the one answer shown. Under verification failure the bonus vanishes once the judge can recognize the answer itself ($\lambda(v) = \lambda_0(1-v)$); under agreement it does not ($\lambda(1) > 0$).
With one correct answer, $A^\star = \{a^\star\}$ and the $\kappa$ and $\lambda$ terms coincide, so every reading allows the reference to help (P2$'$).
With several, both non-key readings predict P1 (swapping $a^\star$ moves fixed text by about $\lambda$) and P2: an answer key ($\kappa > 0$) raises the AUROC of every correct answer against wrong ones, whereas a fall for correct answers that differ from $a^\star$ needs the referenced score to keep only part of the judge's own assessment ($\beta < 1$) or wrong answers containing $a^\star$ to share the bonus ($\gamma > 0$).
The two non-key readings differ where $v_c$ is high, which P3 tests (\cref{sec:p3}).
\Cref{eq:model} organizes the predictions; it is not a fitted model (a fit is in \cref{app:composition}).

\section{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Tasks.}
Our own tasks come from five constraint families (remainder systems, subset sums, Goldbach pairs, divisor counts, digit sums); each stores its full set of correct answers and one member, drawn at random, as the reference.
The \emph{replication pool} (120 tasks) was built to replicate an exploratory observation on \textsc{RewardBench-2} Ties (\cref{app:ties}), and the \emph{$k$-pool} has 75 tasks with exactly $k \in \{1,2,4,8,16\}$ correct answers.
\textsc{MACE} contributes factual questions with 1, 2, 4, or 6 correct answers from its River and Language domains, whose answer lists miss 2.3\% and 8.1\% of correct answers; the reference is drawn at random from each list, and answers are matched to it by normalized string containment (\cref{app:mace}). MATH-500~\citep{hendrycks2021math} supplies unique answers; smaller studies use MathConstraint puzzles and MBPP programs (\cref{app:mathconstraint,app:code}).

\paragraph{Candidates and judges.}
Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct writes 64 samples per task at temperature 1 (Qwen2.5-7B for MATH-500). Each sample is judged \emph{blind}, from the question and the candidate, and \emph{referenced}, with the reference under the heading ``Reference answer (trusted)''.
The core judges are four open models that output only a score, Qwen2.5-7B, phi-4, Qwen2.5-14B, and gemma-2-9b, whose score is the expected value over the score tokens (\cref{app:setup}); Claude Sonnet~5 and Claude Opus~5 through the Anthropic API with thinking disabled, on 16-candidate subsamples of each pool that include wrong answers; and GPT-4.1, whose scores tie too often to rank candidates; GPT-4.1 and o3-mini also run benchmark graders (\cref{sec:cost}).
Reasoning variants (Qwen3 with thinking off or on; Sonnet~5 with thinking) are described in \cref{app:reasoning}.

\paragraph{Reading answers.}
The prompt asks for the final answer inside \texttt{\textbackslash boxed\{\}}, and our registered checkers read it only there, so a correct answer written in prose usually counted as wrong; 38\% to 43\% of replies ignore the format.
On the constructed pools the main text therefore uses \emph{any-format labels}, which credit the final answer a reply states in any format (registered for the API judges, post hoc for the open judges). In a registered audit of 200 unboxed replies, Claude Opus~5's reading agrees with these labels on 91\% but confirms only 86\% of the rule's credits (we had predicted 90\%; \cref{app:grid}). For the four open judges and GPT-4.1, registered, any-format, and model-read labels and boxed replies alone agree on the sign of every grading AUROC change, and boxed replies give the open judges' smallest falls; selection-accuracy losses are less robust to the labeling (\cref{app:grid}).

\paragraph{Measurements and conventions.}
The \emph{swap change} is the change in score of identical text when the reference moves. The \emph{false-rejection rate} is the share of correct answers that differ from the reference scored 3 or below, and the \emph{grading AUROC} is the per-task AUROC for separating those answers from wrong answers (with one correct answer, all correct answers against wrong ones). \emph{Best-of-64 accuracy} is the correctness of the highest-scored candidate, averaged over ties. All changes are referenced minus blind, per task, with task-level bootstrap intervals, in points out of 10 for scores and percentage points (pp) for rates and accuracies. Unless stated otherwise, constructed pools use any-format labels and open judges use an exact expected score (\cref{app:estimator}). We do not correct for multiplicity.
Marks apply to effect estimates: unmarked estimates were pre-specified in a timestamped commit or on OSF, \ph{} marks post hoc estimates, and \reana{} marks pre-specified re-analyses of an effect whose direction was already known. \Cref{app:ledger} is the complete prediction ledger, with registration provenance, failed, split, and untestable predictions, and exploratory analyses.

\section{Prediction 1: the score follows the reference}
\label{sec:p1}

We re-judged the replication pool's byte-identical samples with the reference moved to another correct answer.
Text the reference moves onto gains 5.25 points under Qwen2.5-7B,\reana{} 4.09 under phi-4, and 3.04 under Sonnet~5; text it moves off loses 5.41,\reana{} 3.92, and 2.66; correct text that matches neither reference barely moves (\cref{fig:concept}c). In the terms of \cref{eq:model}, $\lambda$ is 3 to 5 points for these judges (\cref{app:rotation}).
All twelve registered cells (two judges, two rubric wordings, three readouts) went as predicted; a registered ordering of the swap by numeric distance from the reference failed (\cref{app:composition}). On 9 MathConstraint puzzles, swapping the reference between two valid solutions moves a fixed candidate by 3 to 5 points (\cref{app:mathconstraint}).
The swap survives the verbatim MT-Bench template, which drops ``trusted'' and asks for an explanation first (3.59 points for Qwen2.5-7B; \cref{app:rotation}). The pull survives a reference written in words but mostly vanishes when it is written as an expression or masked inside a worked solution (arithmetic only; \cref{app:mechanism,app:frontier}).

\section{Prediction 2: correct answers that differ from the reference are graded worse}
\label{sec:p2}

\label{fig:onevsseveral}

\paragraph{Several correct answers.}
Correct answers that differ from the reference are rejected more often: Qwen2.5-7B scores 28\% of them 3 or below with the reference on the replication pool, against 0.4\% blind (registered labels). Their grading AUROC against wrong answers falls by 0.06 to 0.16 for the five judges we ran on \textsc{MACE},\ph{} most for Sonnet~5 and Opus~5 (GPT-4.1's interval includes zero), and by 0.17 to 0.27 for the four open judges on the constructed pools\ph{} (0.16 to 0.25 with model-read labels, 0.07 to 0.14 on boxed replies only; \cref{fig:onevsseveral}b): the direction holds under every labeling, and only the magnitude changes.

\paragraph{One correct answer.}
With one correct answer the reference helps, as every reading allows. Showing only the final answer raises best-of-64 accuracy on MATH-500 by 28.6 and 30.6~pp for Qwen2.5-7B and 14B,\reana{} as the full solution does, and on \textsc{MACE}'s single-answer questions by 29.4\ph{} and 26.5~pp for Qwen2.5-7B and phi-4 (\cref{fig:onevsseveral}).

\paragraph{Verdict.} P2 holds for Sonnet~5, Opus~5, Qwen2.5-7B, and phi-4 on factual questions, where GPT-4.1 falls by 0.06 with an interval that includes zero, and for the four open judges on the constructed pools. On the constructed pools the API judges fall less, by at most 0.08 (\cref{app:frontier}). With one correct answer the reference helps. Selection among sampled answers is less exposed: on \textsc{MACE}'s multi-answer questions best-of-64 accuracy did not fall ($+2.0$ and $+6.1$~pp, intervals including zero; our registered loss was not confirmed), and $k$-pool losses concentrate where no correct candidate matches the reference\ph{} (\cref{app:estimator}).

\section{Prediction 3: the effect persists after blind acceptance}
\label{sec:p3}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig3_acceptance.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{The reference rejects correct answers the judge already accepts.}
Correct answers the judge accepts blind (a blind 10 for Sonnet~5 and Opus~5; for open judges, the blind score that at most 3\% of wrong answers reach). Filled circles: share of those that differ from the reference scored 3 or below once it is shown; open circles: answers that match it; crosses: a second blind call. Arithmetic rows pool the replication and $k$-pool data ($k$-pool only for Qwen2.5-14B and gemma-2-9b); bins in \cref{app:acceptance}.
Pre-specified: the second blind calls and Opus~5's shares; the rest is post hoc.}
\label{fig:reasoning}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Answers the judge accepts without a reference.}
The API judges rarely give a wrong answer a blind 10, so we take a blind 10 as acceptance; the open judges' expected scores rarely reach 10, so for them we take the blind score that at most 3\% of wrong answers reach, about Sonnet~5's rate for a blind 10. Of the correct factual answers the four judges accept that differ from the reference, between 19\% and 83\% score 3 or below once it is shown, and none of those that match it do (\cref{fig:reasoning}).\ph{} The drop is not score noise: a second blind call through the same interface gives 3 or below to 2 of the 282 accepted differing factual answers of the two API judges, against 78 once the reference is shown.
For three of the four judges verification difficulty may explain part of the effect, but the remainder, among answers the judge already accepts blind, is consistent with an agreement component.

\paragraph{Verdict.} P3 holds for all six judges with a usable acceptance test, and on factual questions for four from three developers: two with a registered same-interface noise control, two at a post hoc threshold. The effect therefore cannot be explained solely by verification failure, although blind acceptance is a proxy for verification, not a direct test of it.

\section{How much it matters, and what sets the size}
\label{sec:cost}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig4_graders.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{Graders told to match under-count correct answers; asking whether the answer is correct shrinks the gap, and the full answer list closes it.}
\textbf{(a)}~Pooled accuracy of four models' answers to 149 \textsc{MACE} questions with several correct answers (396 differing correct, 144 matching correct, 56 wrong), minus their answer-list accuracy (90.6\%). Upper: benchmark graders run verbatim with their configured judges (GPT-4.1; o3-mini, a reasoning model) and with Sonnet~5, and open RL-reward verifiers, given one reference or the full list; our score rubric, blind and with one reference. Lower, zoomed: prompts that ask whether the answer is correct, and the HLE judge with its match clause removed.
\textbf{(b)}~Grading AUROC of correct answers that differ from the reference against wrong answers, change against blind, on the 40-task \textsc{MACE} subsample of \cref{fig:onevsseveral}. 95\% intervals; all values pre-specified.}
\label{fig:graders}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Graders shipped with benchmarks.}
We asked Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, Claude Haiku~4.5, Sonnet~5, and Opus~5 for one answer to each of 149 \textsc{MACE} questions with several correct answers and graded every answer with the SimpleQA grader and the judge shared by BrowseComp and HLE, copied from their repositories and run as configured there (GPT-4.1 for SimpleQA and BrowseComp, o3-mini for HLE), and with Sonnet~5 (\cref{app:r24}). Shown one reference drawn from the answer list, the SimpleQA grader credits 47\% to 53\% of correct answers that differ from it and the BrowseComp/HLE judge 0\% to 2.3\%, so pooled accuracy falls 30 to 34 and 65 to 67 points below the answer lists (\cref{fig:graders}a). \pending{[R26-C: the two open RL-reward verifiers, one reference against the full list.]}

\paragraph{Where it matters.}
On the benchmarks these graders were written for (SimpleQA, BrowseComp, HLE), 0\% to 3\% of sampled questions have several answers after an adjudication that favored a unique answer (up to 15\% on HLE by either annotator; \cref{app:prevalence}), which, with those labels, caps the error on their own leaderboards at about 3 points. The risk is reuse where answers are open: a single-reference verifier rewards an RL training set in which 5.5\% to 18\% of questions admit several answers~\citep{ma2025generalreasoner}, IMO-ProofBench's reference-guided grader is reported to be ``overly punitive'' for unconventional correct proofs~\citep{luong2025imobench}, and a reference program made LLM code evaluators worse~\citep{tong2024codejudge}.

\paragraph{Our rubric and other draws.}
With our score rubric, every one of four judges puts every model below its answer-list accuracy: Sonnet~5, Opus~5, and Haiku~4.5 by 16 to 22 points pooled and GPT-4.1 by 10. Blind, Sonnet~5, Opus~5, and GPT-4.1 stay within 3 points; Haiku~4.5 already under-counts by 9 blind. A second reference drawn from the same lists leaves the shortfall about the same but moves credit for answers that stop or start matching by 23 to 38 points under the Claude graders (about 3 on a same-reference regrade) and by 12.5 and 19 under GPT-4.1 (\cref{app:acceptance}).

\paragraph{What sets the size.}
The harm tracks what the grader is asked (\cref{fig:graders}a, lower panel; \cref{app:r25,app:r26}). Told to ``focus only on whether the answers match'', the HLE judge credits almost no differing correct answer, even with o3-mini. Asked instead whether the response is a correct answer, with no reasoning, GPT-4.1 and Sonnet~5 fall 17 and 10 points short (5 and 2 blind); asked to reason first (chain of thought, verify the answer on your own, or solve the question first), 0.5 to 6 points, within 1 blind. Deleting the match clause from the HLE judge and adding that a different correct answer is correct recovers 35 points for GPT-4.1 and 53 for Sonnet~5, leaving 31 and 13. Most of the gap closes when the grader is asked about correctness instead of matching; written reasoning shrinks it further for GPT-4.1 but, by our registered 5-point test, not for Sonnet~5, and a residual pull remains for Sonnet~5 under every prompt.

\section{How to fix it}
\label{sec:fix}

\paragraph{Give the judge every correct answer.}
An answer key is the reading under which the reference is harmless. Stated as ``any one of the following is a complete correct answer'', the full answer list brings all five benchmark graders within 0.5 points of the answer lists and raises their credit for differing correct answers to 99\% or more (\cref{fig:graders}a). It admits at most 3 of 56 more wrong answers, half of these credits to an alias our string matching misses (``The Democratic Republic of Congo''); the registered bound of 3 points failed for the BrowseComp/HLE judge with GPT-4.1 and o3-mini. With our rubric the list also repairs grading: AUROC is 0.03 to 0.06 above blind for Sonnet~5, Opus~5 (whose interval touches zero), and GPT-4.1, against 0.06 to 0.16 below it with one reference, and false rejections do not rise (\cref{fig:graders}b). A list is still closed; whether it makes judges reject correct answers it omits is untested.

\paragraph{When the answers cannot be listed.}
Remove any instruction to match and ask whether the answer is correct, letting the judge solve the question first (\cref{sec:cost}); on the 40-task subsample, which has many wrong answers, solving first had the smallest point estimates of grading loss (AUROC $-0.04$ and $-0.05$ against blind; false acceptances up by at most 3.2 points) but still rejects 5 to 7 more points of differing correct answers (\cref{app:r26}). An instruction that any correct answer deserves full credit cuts the rise in false rejections on \textsc{MACE} from 36 to 18~pp for Sonnet~5 and to 8~pp for Opus~5, adaptive thinking cuts it to 15~pp for Sonnet~5, and together they left no measurable rise ($-0.2$~pp \ci{-7.2}{+4.6}; one judge, 40 tasks). The full-credit instruction did little for Qwen2.5 judges up to 7B, a registered blind-then-reveal protocol left grading below blind for most open judges, and an exploratory max rule gives back up to 6.7~pp of accuracy when the answer is unique\ph{} (\cref{app:mitigation}).

\paragraph{A recipe.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Answers that can be listed:} give the grader every one, introduced by ``any one of the following is a complete correct answer''.
\item \textbf{A checker exists} (constraints, tests, solvers, proof assistants): grade with it.
\item \textbf{No list and no checker:} do not tell the grader to match; ask whether the response is correct and let it solve the question first (two judges); this shrinks but does not remove the harm. A full-credit sentence and a thinking judge each helped on 40 tasks; their combination with solving first is untested.
\item \textbf{Audit:} score a sample blind as well and read answers accepted blind but rejected with the reference; these include multi-answer questions and blind over-credit, in proportions we did not measure.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Reference-guided judging.}
References are the standard remedy for grading math and reasoning with LLM judges~\citep{zheng2023judging,refguided2024,chen2026rethinking}, and judges without them over-credit wrong answers~\citep{generous2026}; judge models such as JudgeLM and Prometheus and the graders of factuality and hard-exam benchmarks take one gold answer~\citep{zhu2023judgelm,kim2024prometheus,wei2024simpleqa,wei2025browsecomp,phan2025hle}. RL-reward verifiers are evaluated mainly on equivalent forms of one answer~\citep{chen2025xverify,liu2025compassverifier}, and false negatives from rule-based checking slow RL~\citep{xu2025tinyv,huang2025pitfalls}.
Judges depend on the reference being correct~\citep{judgingref2026,krumdick2025nofreelabels}, diverge from humans under reference-based grading~\citep{thakur2024judging}, and favor the reference's solution method~\citep{fixation2025}.

\paragraph{Single references and many correct answers.}
Reference-based metrics in translation reward outputs that resemble the particular reference~\citep{freitag2020bleu,fomicheva2016reference}. Open-domain question answering under-credits correct answers that are missing from the gold list or phrased differently~\citep{min2020ambigqa,si2021whatsinaname,bulian2022tomayto,kamalloo2023evaluating}; in our setting the list is complete and the judge still favors the one answer it is shown.

\paragraph{Judge biases and optimization.}
LLM judges favor the first response~\citep{wang2024fair}, longer responses~\citep{dubois2024lengthcontrolled}, their own outputs~\citep{panickssery2024llm}, and scores shown to them~\citep{kapetanovic2026anchoring}; catalogues of judge biases~\citep{koo2024benchmarking,ye2025justice} do not vary which correct answer is shown. The agreement reading resembles sycophancy toward a suggested answer~\citep{sharma2023sycophancy,turpin2023unfaithful}; here the suggestion is correct, so deference and correctness conflict only when another answer is also correct. Optimizing against an imperfect evaluator inflates its scores~\citep{gao2022scaling,rafailov2024scaling}.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Labels.} Any-format labels were checked by a frontier model's reading, not by humans (it rejects 14\% of the unboxed replies the rule credits), and boxed replies alone give conservative estimates. \textsc{MACE}'s lists miss 2.3\% to 8.1\% of correct answers. Prevalence labels come from LLM annotators with low agreement ($\kappa \le 0.35$) and an adjudication that favored a unique answer.
\item \textbf{Tasks.} Constructed pools come from five arithmetic families and one generator, natural data from two \textsc{MACE} domains. The benchmark study uses one answer per model on multi-answer questions; three graders grade their own answers, and intervals resample questions, not grader calls. A full answer list helps only as far as it is complete. Open-domain QA and open-ended tasks (summarization, writing, agent trajectories) are untested.
\item \textbf{Judges and prompts.} Open judges have at most 14B parameters. Our rubric calls the reference trusted; the other wordings we tested do not exhaust reference prompts. Thinking with the full-credit instruction was tested for one judge on 40 tasks, and the correctness prompts for two judges. P3 on factual questions has a same-interface noise control only for the two API judges.
\item \textbf{Mechanism and registration.} No experiment manipulates verification directly; the max rule was chosen post hoc; most registrations are timestamped commits, and the verdict rule was applied retroactively (\cref{app:ledger}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

On the unique-answer tasks we study, a reference answer is a useful check.
When a question has several correct answers, LLM judges favor the one they are shown: swapping it moves the score of unchanged text by 2.7 to 5.4 points, and correct answers that differ from it are rejected far more often, even ones the judge accepts without a reference.
Run as configured on such questions, the graders of SimpleQA, BrowseComp, and HLE score four models 30 to 67 points below their answer-list accuracy, largely because of what they are asked: deleting the HLE judge's match clause recovers 35 and 53 of those points for GPT-4.1 and Sonnet~5.
Where the correct answers can be listed, grade against all of them; otherwise, ask whether the answer is correct, let the judge solve the question first, and audit disagreements between blind and referenced scores.

\subsection*{AI use statement}
Generative AI models were the experimental subjects: open and frontier LLMs generated candidate answers and served as the judges under study (\cref{sec:setup}, \cref{app:setup}); three Claude models answered the benchmark questions of \cref{sec:cost}, which Claude models, GPT-4.1, and o3-mini graded, and Claude Opus~5 read replies for the label audit. One pilot (\cref{app:mathconstraint}) used a Claude model to generate candidate solutions, each verified by exact checks, and Qwen2.5-14B read unboxed replies for one labeling. We used generative AI tools to help write analysis code, draft and edit text, produce figures, search and verify related work, audit the preregistration ledger, and label samples of dataset questions for two prevalence audits, the first with two passes from one model family and the second with Claude Sonnet~5 and GPT-5-mini adjudicated by Claude Opus~5; no label was checked by human annotators. Every number in the paper is produced by committed analysis scripts from stored model outputs, and we checked every quoted number against those outputs. We take responsibility for the final content of this work.

\subsection*{Ethics statement}
This work studies a failure of automated evaluation that can make models look worse than they are on questions with several correct answers. We release no new model and no attack on a deployed system. The findings raise a concern for pipelines that use reference-guided judges as reward signals on such questions, which may reward matching the reference rather than being correct; our three training runs were inconclusive and did not show a policy learning to copy the reference (\cref{app:dpo}). We pair the failure with measurements of when it matters. No human subjects were involved.

\subsection*{Reproducibility statement}
\Cref{app:setup} specifies every pool, judge, prompt, and scorer, and \cref{app:ledger} lists each prediction with its registration type and outcome. The anonymized supplementary material contains task generators and checkers, candidate pools, the answer labelings, all judge score files, the analysis scripts that produce every number, table, and figure, and the registration documents with commit hashes replaced by stable tokens.
