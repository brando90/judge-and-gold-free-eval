\title{The Reference Is Not an Answer Key:\\LLM Judges Favor the Correct Answer They Are Shown}

\author{Anonymous authors\\Paper under double-blind review}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
LLM judges often grade with a reference answer, on the premise that it acts as an answer key: a correct response should score the same whichever correct answer is shown.
On questions with several correct answers the premise fails.
We separate three readings of a reference (an answer key, the only evidence for a judge that cannot verify, or an answer the judge rewards for matching) and find that (1) swapping the reference between two correct answers moves the score of identical text by about 3 to 5 points out of 10; (2) correct answers that differ from the reference are rejected far more often, even ones the judge accepts without a reference, so verification failure alone cannot explain the effect; and (3) thinking nearly removes the effect on arithmetic, while on factual questions thinking or a full-credit instruction only reduces it.
On up to 149 factual benchmark questions, Claude Sonnet~5 and Opus~5, when shown a reference, score four models 7 to 40 points below their answer-list accuracy, and within 3 points of it when grading blind.
Redrawing the reference leaves this shortfall about the same but moves credit for answers whose match changes by 23 to 38 points, against about 3 when the same reference is re-graded.
We test these predictions across ten judges, constructed tasks, MACE, MATH-500, and smaller puzzle and code studies (MathConstraint, MBPP), with a complete preregistration ledger.
On such questions, a low score from a reference-guided judge does not reliably mean the answer is wrong.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Ask for a two-digit number that is divisible by 5 and whose digits sum to 8.
Both 35 and 80 are correct.
Qwen2.5-7B, shown a candidate that reasons its way to 80, scores it 10.0 out of 10 with no reference, 2.9 when the reference answer is 35, and 9.3 when the reference is 80 (\cref{fig:concept}b).
The text never changed; only the correct answer the judge was shown did.
This example swaps more than most, but over 120 such tasks the same judge moves correct text by about 5 points in either direction (\cref{fig:concept}c).

Reference-guided judging is standard practice.
MT-Bench's math and reasoning template tells the judge to begin ``by comparing the assistant's answer with the reference answer''~\citep{zheng2023judging}, references raise agreement with human labels~\citep{refguided2024}, judges without one are too generous~\citep{generous2026}, and comparison against a reference is the most reliable single-pass strategy for grading math~\citep{chen2026rethinking}.
The same judges grade benchmarks, choose among sampled answers, and serve as reward signals, which optimization exploits~\citep{gao2022scaling}.
The practice is well-motivated when a question has one correct answer, as on curated math benchmarks: our LLM-labeled audit of 200 questions per dataset (not checked by humans) found 0\% to 2.5\% with several on MATH-500, GSM8K, and DeepScaleR (\cref{app:prevalence}).
Whether it holds up where answers are not unique has not, to our knowledge, been tested directly for LLM judges, although such questions are common: 30\% of NQ-open questions in the same audit, benchmarks built around several valid answers such as \textsc{RewardBench-2} Ties and the factual benchmark \textsc{MACE}~\citep{malik2025rewardbench2,mace2026}, and tasks that ask for an example or a construction.

\paragraph{Three readings of a reference.}
If the reference is an \emph{answer key}, the judge checks a candidate against every correct answer, and a correct answer scores the same whichever one is shown.
Under \emph{verification failure}, the judge cannot tell on its own that a different answer is correct, so the reference is its only evidence.
Under \emph{agreement}, the judge favors the correct answer that matches the reference even when it can recognize the others, whether because it rewards agreement, because it assumes one intended answer, or because the prompt tells it to use the reference; we test the last of these only partly (\cref{sec:p3}).
We test three predictions. P1 and P2 rule out the answer key; P3 tests for an agreement component beyond verification failure.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=2pt]
\item[\textbf{P1}] \textbf{The score follows the reference.} Swapping which correct answer is the reference moves the score of unchanged text (\cref{sec:p1}).
\item[\textbf{P2}] \textbf{Correct answers that differ from the reference are graded worse.} With several correct answers, they lose ground against wrong answers; with one, every reading allows the reference to help (\cref{sec:p2}).
\item[\textbf{P3}] \textbf{The effect persists after blind acceptance.} It remains for correct answers the judge already accepts without a reference, which verification failure alone does not predict (\cref{sec:p3}).
\end{itemize}
\Cref{tab:summary} gives what we observed. All three predictions hold; P3 indicates an agreement component beyond verification failure for all six judges with a usable acceptance test, and on factual questions for four of them from three developers. A thinking mode nearly removes the effect on arithmetic but only about halves it on factual questions, and an instruction that any correct answer deserves full credit also reduces it there without removing it (\cref{sec:p3}). The effect lowers benchmark scores under both of two reference draws, and which answer is drawn decides which correct answers get credit; an exploratory safeguard that keeps the higher of the blind and referenced scores protects grading at a cost when the answer is unique (\cref{sec:cost}).

Prior work comes close without isolating this failure.
\citet{fixation2025} name ``solution fixation'' but measure it with flawed solutions rewritten in the reference's style; \citet{judgingref2026} and \citet{krumdick2025nofreelabels} replace the reference with a wrong one; \citet{kapetanovic2026anchoring} show judges anchor on scores they are shown; and human raters shown different reference translations favor candidates close to the one they see~\citep{fomicheva2016reference}.

\section{A model of the three readings}
\label{sec:model}

Let $A^\star$ be the set of correct answers to a question and $a^\star \in A^\star$ the one shown as the reference. Let $b(c)$ be a judge's blind score for candidate $c$, $s(c; a^\star)$ its referenced score, and $v_c \in [0, 1]$ how reliably the judge recognizes $c$ as correct without a reference. We write
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
s(c; a^\star) \;\approx\;& \beta\, b(c) \;+\; \kappa\, \mathbb{I}[\,\mathrm{ans}(c) \in A^\star\,] \;+\; \lambda(v_c)\, \mathbb{I}[\,\mathrm{ans}(c) = a^\star\,] \\
&+\; \gamma\, \mathbb{I}[\,c \text{ is wrong and contains } a^\star\,] \;+\; \varepsilon .
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:model}
\end{equation}
An answer key checks candidates against the whole set: $\kappa \ge 0$ and $\lambda = \gamma = 0$. The other two readings reward matching the one answer shown. Under verification failure the bonus vanishes once the judge can recognize the answer itself ($\lambda(v) = \lambda_0(1-v)$); under agreement it does not ($\lambda(1) > 0$).
With one correct answer, $A^\star = \{a^\star\}$ and the $\kappa$ and $\lambda$ terms coincide, so every reading allows the reference to help (P2$'$).
With several, both non-key readings predict P1: swapping $a^\star$ moves fixed text by about $\lambda$. P2 separates them from the answer key in the opposite direction: under an answer key, $\kappa > 0$ raises the AUROC of every correct answer against wrong ones, while P2 predicts that it falls for correct answers that differ from $a^\star$, which needs the referenced score to keep only part of the judge's own assessment ($\beta < 1$) or wrong answers that contain $a^\star$ to collect part of the bonus ($\gamma > 0$).
The two non-key readings differ where $v_c$ is high, which P3 tests (\cref{sec:p3}).
\Cref{eq:model} organizes the predictions; it is not a fitted model (a fit is in \cref{app:composition}).

\section{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Tasks.}
Our own tasks come from five constraint families (remainder systems, subset sums, Goldbach pairs, divisor counts, digit sums); each stores its full set of correct answers and one member, drawn at random, as the reference.
The \emph{replication pool} (120 tasks) was built to replicate an exploratory observation on \textsc{RewardBench-2} Ties (\cref{app:ties}), and the \emph{$k$-pool} has 75 tasks with exactly $k \in \{1,2,4,8,16\}$ correct answers.
\textsc{MACE} contributes factual questions with 1, 2, 4, or 6 correct answers from its River and Language domains, whose answer lists miss 2.3\% and 8.1\% of correct answers; the reference is drawn at random from each list, and answers are matched to it by normalized string containment (\cref{app:mace}). MATH-500~\citep{hendrycks2021math} supplies unique answers, and two smaller studies use MathConstraint's solver-verified puzzles (\cref{app:mathconstraint}) and MBPP programs checked by their tests (\cref{app:code}).

\paragraph{Candidates and judges.}
Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct writes 64 samples per task at temperature 1 (Qwen2.5-7B for MATH-500). Each sample is judged \emph{blind}, from the question and the candidate, and \emph{referenced}, with the reference under the heading ``Reference answer (trusted)''.
The core judges are four open models that output only a score, Qwen2.5-7B, phi-4, Qwen2.5-14B, and gemma-2-9b, whose score is the expected value over the score tokens (\cref{app:setup}); Claude Sonnet~5 and Claude Opus~5 through the Anthropic API with thinking disabled, on 16-candidate subsamples of each pool that include wrong answers; and GPT-4.1, whose scores tie too often to rank candidates.
To study reasoning, Qwen3-8B and Qwen3-14B write before a final ``Score: N'' line with the thinking mode off (\emph{explanation first}) or on, and Sonnet~5 also runs with thinking through the API and its command-line interface (CLI).

\paragraph{Reading answers.}
The prompt asks for the final answer inside \texttt{\textbackslash boxed\{\}}, and our registered checkers read it only there, so a correct answer written in prose usually counted as wrong; 38\% to 43\% of replies ignore the format.
On the constructed pools the main text therefore uses \emph{any-format labels}, which credit the final answer a reply states in any format (registered for the API judges, post hoc for the open judges). In a registered audit of 200 unboxed replies, Claude Opus~5's reading agrees with these labels on 91\% but confirms only 86\% of the rule's credits (we had predicted 90\%); most of the rest list several numbers or commit to no answer. A Qwen2.5-14B reading agrees with Opus~5 on 95\%; because the two readers share an extraction prompt, this agreement validates neither labeling (\cref{app:grid}). For the four open judges and GPT-4.1, registered, any-format, and model-read labels and boxed replies alone agree on the sign of every grading AUROC change, and boxed replies give the open judges' smallest falls; selection-accuracy losses are less robust to the labeling (\cref{app:grid}).

\paragraph{Measurements and conventions.}
The \emph{swap change} is the change in score of identical text when the reference moves. The \emph{false-rejection rate} is the share of correct answers that differ from the reference scored 3 or below, and the \emph{grading AUROC} is the per-task AUROC for separating those answers from wrong answers (with one correct answer, all correct answers against wrong ones). \emph{Best-of-64 accuracy} is the correctness of the highest-scored candidate, averaged over ties. All changes are referenced minus blind, per task, with task-level bootstrap intervals, in points out of 10 for scores and percentage points (pp) for rates and accuracies. Unless stated otherwise, constructed pools use any-format labels and open judges use an exact expected score (\cref{app:estimator}). We do not correct for multiplicity.
Marks apply to effect estimates: unmarked estimates were pre-specified in a timestamped commit or on OSF, \ph{} marks post hoc estimates, and \reana{} marks pre-specified re-analyses of an effect whose direction was already known. \Cref{app:ledger} is the complete prediction ledger, with registration provenance, failed, split, and untestable predictions, and exploratory analyses.

\section{Prediction 1: the score follows the reference}
\label{sec:p1}

We re-judged the replication pool's byte-identical samples with the reference moved to another correct answer.
Text the reference moves onto gains 5.25 points under Qwen2.5-7B,\reana{} 4.09 under phi-4, and 3.04 under Sonnet~5; text it moves off loses 5.41,\reana{} 3.92, and 2.66; correct text that matches neither reference barely moves (\cref{fig:concept}c). In the terms of \cref{eq:model}, $\lambda$ is 3 to 5 points for these judges (\cref{app:rotation}).
All twelve registered cells (two judges, two rubric wordings, three readouts) went as predicted; a registered ordering of the swap by numeric distance from the reference failed (\cref{app:composition}). On 9 MathConstraint puzzles, swapping the reference between two valid solutions moves a fixed candidate by 3 to 5 points (\cref{app:mathconstraint}); on MBPP, where correct programs have few distinct outputs, a canonical program as the reference added no measurable effect (\cref{app:code}).
The pull survives a reference written in words but mostly vanishes when it is written as an expression or masked inside a worked solution (arithmetic only; \cref{app:mechanism,app:frontier}).

\section{Prediction 2: correct answers that differ from the reference are graded worse}
\label{sec:p2}

\label{fig:onevsseveral}

\paragraph{Several correct answers.}
Correct answers that differ from the reference are rejected more often: Qwen2.5-7B scores 28\% of them 3 or below with the reference on the replication pool, against 0.4\% blind (registered labels). Their grading AUROC against wrong answers falls by 0.06 to 0.16 for the four judges we ran on \textsc{MACE},\ph{} most for Sonnet~5 and Opus~5, and by 0.17 to 0.27 for the four open judges on the constructed pools\ph{} (0.16 to 0.25 with model-read labels, 0.07 to 0.14 on boxed replies only; \cref{fig:onevsseveral}b): the direction holds under every labeling, and only the magnitude changes. We did not run GPT-4.1 on \textsc{MACE} (\cref{app:frontier,app:acceptance}).

\paragraph{One correct answer.}
With one correct answer the reference helps, as every reading allows. Showing only the final answer raises best-of-64 accuracy on MATH-500 by 28.6 and 30.6~pp for Qwen2.5-7B and 14B,\reana{} as the full solution does, and on \textsc{MACE}'s single-answer questions by 29.4\ph{} and 26.5~pp for Qwen2.5-7B and phi-4 (\cref{fig:onevsseveral}).

\paragraph{Verdict.} P2 holds for every judge we ran on factual questions (Sonnet~5, Opus~5, Qwen2.5-7B, phi-4) and for the four open judges on the constructed pools. On the constructed pools the API judges fall less: Sonnet~5 and Opus~5 by 0.06 to 0.08 on the $k$-pool and by 0.04 and 0.02 on the replication pool (Opus~5's interval includes zero), and GPT-4.1 by at most 0.04 on either pool. With one correct answer the reference helps. Selection among sampled answers is less exposed: on \textsc{MACE}'s multi-answer questions best-of-64 accuracy did not fall ($+2.0$ and $+6.1$~pp, intervals including zero; our registered loss was not confirmed), and $k$-pool losses concentrate where no correct candidate matches the reference\ph{} (\cref{app:estimator}).

\section{Prediction 3: the effect persists after blind acceptance}
\label{sec:p3}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig3_acceptance.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{The reference rejects correct answers the judge already accepts, and on factual questions thinking does not remove this.}
\textbf{(a)}~Correct answers the judge accepts blind (a blind 10 for Sonnet~5 and Opus~5; for open judges, the blind score that at most 3\% of wrong answers reach). Arithmetic rows pool the replication and $k$-pool data ($k$-pool only for Qwen2.5-14B and gemma-2-9b); bins in \cref{app:acceptance}.

Pre-specified: the second blind calls, Opus~5's shares in (a), and (b); the rest is post hoc. 95\% intervals.}
\label{fig:reasoning}

\paragraph{Answers the judge accepts without a reference.}
The API judges rarely give a wrong answer a blind 10, so we take a blind 10 as acceptance; the open judges' expected scores rarely reach 10, so for them we take the blind score that at most 3\% of wrong answers reach, about Sonnet~5's rate for a blind 10. Of the correct factual answers the four judges accept that differ from the reference, between 19\% and 83\% score 3 or below once it is shown, and none of those that match it do (\cref{fig:reasoning}a).\ph{} The drop is not score noise: a second blind call through the same interface gives 3 or below to 2 of the 282 accepted differing factual answers of the two API judges, against 78 once the reference is shown.
Binned by blind score,\ph{} rejection of differing answers falls (not monotonically for phi-4) as phi-4, Sonnet~5, and Opus~5 grow surer, but stays far above zero in each judge's top bin (19\% to 46\%), where no matching answer is rejected, while Qwen2.5-7B rejects at least 83\% at every blind score (\cref{app:acceptance}). For three of the four judges verification difficulty may explain part of the effect, but the remainder, among answers the judge already accepts blind, is consistent with an agreement component. On arithmetic the remainder is small for Opus~5 (5\% or less on both pools) and for phi-4 on the replication pool (5\%), but not on the $k$-pool (15\%).\ph

\paragraph{Not only the prompt.}
Our referenced prompts call the reference trusted and tell the judge to use it. Under the verbatim MT-Bench template, which drops ``trusted'' and asks the judge to explain first, Qwen2.5-7B still loses 3.59 points on the swap. Appending a sentence that any correct answer deserves full credit even if it differs from the reference reduces the effect without removing it: on \textsc{MACE}, the rise in false rejections falls from 36 to 18~pp for Sonnet~5 and to 8~pp for Opus~5, but grading AUROC stays below blind for both (\cref{tab:protocols}). On a synthetic arithmetic grid the same instruction did little or nothing for Qwen2.5 judges up to 7B (\cref{app:mitigation}).

\paragraph{A thinking mode.}
On arithmetic, thinking shrinks the swap loss to near zero, from 2.85 to 0.32 points for Sonnet~5 and from 1.98 to 0.12 for Qwen3-14B, and a score ceiling does not explain this (\cref{app:reasoning}). On factual questions it only about halves the rise in false rejections for Sonnet~5 and both Qwen3 judges (\cref{fig:reasoning}b).

\paragraph{Verdict.} P3 holds for all six judges with a usable acceptance test, and on factual questions for four from three developers: two with a registered same-interface noise control, two at a post hoc threshold. The effect therefore cannot be explained solely by verification failure, although blind acceptance is a proxy for verification, not a direct test of it. Where thinking removes the effect, P3 cannot be tested.

\section{Consequences for benchmark scores, and what helps}
\label{sec:cost}

\paragraph{Benchmark scores.}
We asked Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, Claude Haiku~4.5, Sonnet~5, and Opus~5 for one answer to each of 149 \textsc{MACE} questions with several correct answers, and graded every answer with Sonnet~5, Opus~5, and Haiku~4.5, blind and with a reference drawn from each answer list (\cref{fig:benchmark}). With the reference, every grader puts every model below its answer-list accuracy (16 to 22 points pooled). Blind, Sonnet~5 and Opus~5 stay within 3 points, so their shortfall comes from the reference; Haiku~4.5 already under-counts by 9 points blind. The aggregate shortfall does not hinge on the draw, but the draw decides which correct answers get credit: a second reference drawn from the same lists (hollow markers) moves each pooled shortfall by about 3 points at most, but answers that stop matching lose 25 to 38~pp of credit and answers that start matching gain 23 to 35~pp, while re-grading with the first reference moves the same answers by about 3~pp. Whether the draw also reorders models is unresolved: under Sonnet~5 and Opus~5 a same-reference regrade leaves the Haiku~4.5 minus Llama-3.1-8B gap within 1 point while the redraw moves it by 7 to 9, but under Haiku~4.5 the regrade moves it as much as the redraw (10.7 points each), so we make no ranking claim (\cref{app:acceptance}).

\paragraph{What helps.}
The full-credit sentence halves the factual harm or more, but grading stays below blind (\cref{tab:protocols}). The registered blind-then-reveal protocol left grading below blind for most open judges (\cref{app:mitigation}). An exploratory safeguard chosen post hoc from five zero-cost score rules, keeping the higher of the blind and referenced scores (the \emph{max rule}), keeps grading AUROC within 0.03 of blind on the $k$-pool and \textsc{MACE},\ph{} and pooled benchmark accuracy within 2 points of the answer lists for all three graders\ph{} (5 points for one model under Haiku~4.5; \cref{fig:benchmark}). For Opus~5 and Haiku~4.5 we had registered that it would stay within 5 points; for Haiku~4.5, which under-counts blind, it was also closer to the lists than blind grading. Because the maximum never scores an answer below its blind score, its grading result is partly by construction; the informative questions are what it costs and whether it generalizes. With a unique answer it gives back part of the reference's benefit, up to 6.7~pp of accuracy and 0.15 of AUROC (paired)\ph{} for Qwen2.5-7B with a full-solution reference (\cref{app:mitigation}).

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Reference-guided judging.}
References are the standard remedy for grading math and reasoning with LLM judges~\citep{zheng2023judging,refguided2024,chen2026rethinking}, and judges without them over-credit wrong answers~\citep{generous2026}.
Judge models such as JudgeLM and Prometheus take a reference as a gold answer~\citep{zhu2023judgelm,kim2024prometheus}.
Judges depend on the reference being correct~\citep{judgingref2026,krumdick2025nofreelabels}, diverge from humans under reference-based grading~\citep{thakur2024judging}, and favor the reference's solution method~\citep{fixation2025}.

\paragraph{Single references and many correct answers.}
Reference-based metrics in translation reward outputs that resemble the particular reference~\citep{freitag2020bleu,fomicheva2016reference}. Open-domain question answering under-credits correct answers that are missing from the gold list or phrased differently~\citep{min2020ambigqa,si2021whatsinaname,bulian2022tomayto,kamalloo2023evaluating}; in our setting the list is complete and the judge still favors the one answer it is shown.

\paragraph{Judge biases and optimization.}
LLM judges favor the first response~\citep{wang2024fair}, longer responses~\citep{dubois2024lengthcontrolled}, their own outputs~\citep{panickssery2024llm}, and scores shown to them~\citep{kapetanovic2026anchoring}; broader catalogues of judge biases~\citep{koo2024benchmarking,ye2025justice} do not vary which correct answer the judge is shown. Optimizing against an imperfect evaluator inflates its scores~\citep{gao2022scaling,rafailov2024scaling}.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Labels.} The any-format labels on the constructed pools were checked by a frontier model's reading, not by humans; that reading rejects 14\% of the unboxed replies the rule credits, mostly lists of several numbers or replies that commit to no answer. Boxed replies alone give the conservative grading estimates. \textsc{MACE}'s answer lists miss 2.3\% to 8.1\% of correct answers (\cref{app:mace}).
\item \textbf{Tasks.} The constructed pools come from five arithmetic families with one generator, and the natural data from two \textsc{MACE} domains. The benchmark study uses one answer per model on multi-answer questions only, graded by three models from one provider, each of which also grades its own answers; Opus~5's readouts drop questions whose referenced replies ran past the token limit (141, 136, and 135 of 149 remain for the two draws and the regrade), and both reference draws come from the same answer lists. Benchmark intervals resample questions, not grader calls; a same-reference regrade moved one model's referenced accuracy by 6.7 points. Open-domain question answering such as NQ-open is untested, and whether the same reference dependence appears in open-ended tasks whose validity is subjective or only partly enumerable (summarization, writing, agent trajectories) is unknown.
\item \textbf{Judges and prompts.} All open judges have at most 14B parameters, and GPT-4.1 was run only on constructed pools. P3 on factual questions has a same-interface noise control only for the two API judges; the open judges' acceptance test uses a post hoc threshold. The MT-Bench template, which drops ``trusted'', and the full-credit instruction show that the effect survives two changes to our wording, but they do not exhaust reference prompts.
\item \textbf{Mechanism and protocols.} No experiment manipulates verification directly. The max rule was chosen post hoc from five zero-cost score rules; only its benchmark result for two graders was registered in advance.
\item \textbf{Registration.} Most registrations are timestamped commits, and the verdict rule was applied retroactively to earlier predictions (\cref{app:ledger}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

On the unique-answer tasks we study, a reference answer is a useful check.
When a question has several correct answers, LLM judges favor the one they are shown: swapping it moves the score of unchanged text by 2.7 to 5.4 points, and correct answers that differ from it are rejected far more often, even ones the judge accepts without a reference. On factual questions, frontier graders shown a reference scored four models 7 to 40 points below their answer-list accuracy; redrawing the reference left that shortfall about the same but moved credit for individual answers by 23 to 38 points, against about 3 on a regrade.
A thinking mode nearly removes the effect on arithmetic; on factual questions, thinking and an instruction that any correct answer counts reduce it without removing it.
Evaluations of such questions should use the full answer set when it exists and otherwise inspect blind and referenced scores separately.

\subsection*{AI use statement}
Generative AI models were the experimental subjects: open and frontier LLMs generated candidate answers and served as the judges under study (\cref{sec:setup}, \cref{app:setup}); three Claude models answered and graded the benchmark questions of \cref{sec:cost}, and Claude Opus~5 read replies for the label audit. One pilot (\cref{app:mathconstraint}) used a Claude model to generate candidate solutions, each verified by exact checks, and Qwen2.5-14B read unboxed replies for one labeling. We used generative AI tools to help write analysis code, draft and edit text, produce figures, search and verify related work, audit the preregistration ledger, and label a sample of dataset questions for the prevalence audit; both labeling passes came from one model family, were adjudicated against each other, and were not checked by human annotators. Every number in the paper is produced by committed analysis scripts from stored model outputs, and we checked every quoted number against those outputs. We take responsibility for the final content of this work.

\subsection*{Ethics statement}
This work studies a failure of automated evaluation that can make models look worse than they are on questions with several correct answers. We release no new model and no attack on a deployed system. The findings raise a concern for pipelines that use reference-guided judges as reward signals on such questions, which may reward matching the reference rather than being correct; our three training runs were inconclusive and did not show a policy learning to copy the reference (\cref{app:dpo}). We pair the failure with measurements of when it matters. No human subjects were involved.

\subsection*{Reproducibility statement}
\Cref{app:setup} specifies every pool, judge, prompt, and scorer, and \cref{app:ledger} lists each prediction with its registration type and outcome. The anonymized supplementary material contains task generators and checkers, candidate pools, the answer labelings, all judge score files, the analysis scripts that produce every number, table, and figure, and the registration documents with commit hashes replaced by stable tokens.
