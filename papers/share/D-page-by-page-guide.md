# Paper D — Page-by-Page Reading Guide

*A plain-language walkthrough of "When the Answer Key Backfires: Reference Anchoring Turns LLM Judges into Answer Matchers" (Draft v20, 26 pages — `D-when-the-answer-key-backfires.pdf`).*

*Shortcut for skimmers: pages 1–2 are the summary, page 14 is the figure to show someone, pages 16–17 are what a practitioner should read.*

---

**Page 1 — Title, abstract, and the story in miniature.**
The title says it all: "When the Answer Key Backfires." The abstract makes the full argument in one block: people put a trusted reference answer in an AI judge's prompt believing it prevents cheating, but on questions with more than one correct answer it does the opposite — the judge becomes an answer-matcher, and when you use it to pick the best of many attempts, scores get inflated instead of protected. It previews the evidence: a publicly pre-registered experiment (predictions locked before any data existed) found the reference makes inflation clearly worse; swapping the reference for a different correct answer moves the identical text's score by up to 9.5 points out of 10; the harm begins the moment a question has two valid answers; and the fix is to let the judge score blind first and only then see the reference. The page closes with the opening of the introduction: the divisor-of-91 example, where 7 and 13 are both correct but the judge gives ~10/10 to whichever matches the key it was shown and ~5/10 to the other.

**Page 2 — The whole paper at a glance.**
This page is deliberately all visuals. The top table answers the paper's four questions in four numbers: does the reference inflate scores? (yes, by +1.19 "inflation units" — think of it as how far the scoreboard drifts above reality); is the cause the answer key itself? (yes — up to +9.5 points from swapping it); when does the harm start? (as soon as there are 2 valid answers); is there a fix? (yes — score blind first, which drops the inflation to essentially zero). Below it sits the paper's key bar chart: at four judge sizes, the same answer's score leaps when the reference is rotated to match it, honest "blind" judges show almost no such preference, and the whole effect is visibly about what the judge is *shown*, not what the answer *is*.

**Page 3 — Why anyone should care, and the four promises.**
The intro continues: giving judges a reference is standard practice with real benefits on single-answer questions, and the paper doesn't dispute that — it points out the hidden assumption that the answer is unique. Many real tasks ("name any 7-digit prime," "give an example of...") have many correct answers, and there the reference is just one sample, not the truth. Prior work already showed judges statically penalize non-matching correct answers; the open question was what happens *dynamically*, when a model is being selected to please the judge. The page lists the four contributions — the inversion (pre-registered), the causal mechanism (anchoring), the boundary (harm starts at two valid answers), and the fix (ordering, not instructions) — then begins the Setup section by describing the first task pool: 51 short-answer questions, each with several enumerated correct answers, one of which is randomly designated as "the reference."

**Page 4 — The machinery of the experiments.**
This page defines how everything is measured, in plain terms. A model writes 64 answers per question; an automatic checker decides which are genuinely correct ("correct" always means *any* valid answer, never "matches the reference"); judges of four sizes score every answer twice, once blind and once shown the reference. The key metric, "inflation," captures a specific failure: when you pick the highest-scored answer out of 64, how much higher does its *score* look than its *actual quality* justifies? A judge whose scores track real correctness shows zero; a judge that hands out 10s for matching the key shows a lot. A second metric, "lost accuracy," asks how much correctness the selection leaves on the table, and the "margin" measures the bonus a key-matching answer gets over an equally correct non-matching one. The page also notes that every prediction and every deviation from plan is logged in a ledger at the back, then opens the main results section.

**Page 5 — The headline experiment's results table.**
A full page for the paper's most important table. This is the pre-registered replication: 120 brand-new math-style questions with airtight automatic checking, answers written by one model family and judged by two completely different ones, with the analysis code and predictions publicly frozen *before any data was generated* — meaning the authors couldn't massage anything afterward. All three locked-in predictions came true: the reference adds about +1.19 units of inflation over blind judging for both judges; on nearly every eligible question the referenced judge picked a key-matching answer where the blind judge didn't (lopsided counts like 76-to-0); and key-matching answers got a 4-to-5-point bonus. The fine print discloses everything, including a conservative re-reading (+0.90) on the cleanest-to-grade third of questions.

**Page 6 — More pressure means more harm, then the swap experiment begins.**
The top table shows the harm isn't an artifact of choosing from 64 samples: even picking the better of just *2* samples, the referenced judge is already measurably worse than blind, and the gap grows about twelve-fold as the pool grows to 64. The text then wraps up the replication (its one gap: it didn't test single-answer questions) and starts the mechanism section — the reference-swap experiment. The logic is simple: take the exact same questions and the exact same 64 answers, and re-judge everything with the reference switched to a *different* correct answer. If the judge grades quality, nothing should change; if it anchors on the key, scores must follow the key. The page ends mid-story with the three tell-tale signs appearing: newly-matching answers gain, formerly-matching answers lose, and neutral answers stay flat.

**Page 7 — The swap results, in full.**
A dense table quantifying the swap experiment. The star rows: the identical answer text gains up to +9.5 out of 10 points at the smallest judge (which scores almost like a pass/fail switch) and still +3 points at the largest; answers that used to match the old key lose a mirror-image amount; blind judges on the same comparison show almost nothing. The bottom rows show what this does to selection: whenever a key-matching correct answer exists anywhere in the pool of 64, the referenced judge picks it 93–100% of the time, versus roughly 40–50% for blind judges — near-total collapse onto the key.

**Page 8 — What the swap proves, what it doesn't, and a new experiment.**
First, the honest limits of the causal story: knowing which questions show the biggest key-matching bonus does *not* predict which questions inflate most, and the authors show this isn't just noisy measurement — if the bonus alone drove inflation, the correlation would have been strong, and it isn't. So anchoring is definitely the cause of score movement, but exactly how it adds up to inflation question-by-question stays unresolved (they say so plainly, and note an earlier draft got this analysis wrong). Then the next experiment starts: since neither previous pool controlled *how many* correct answers each question has, they build questions with exactly 1, 2, 4, 8, or 16 valid answers and test six judges from three different model families, under four setups — blind, one reference, reference-plus-instruction ("any correct answer counts"), and multiple references.

**Page 9 — The controlled-multiplicity grid.**
The results table for that experiment. Reading across each row: with exactly one valid answer, showing the reference is harmless or actually helpful; as the number of valid answers grows, referenced inflation climbs while blind judging stays roughly flat. The instruction sentence helps the strongest judge (phi-4) and does nothing for the mid-size one; showing multiple references helps partially. The bottom panel repeats the test with a different model writing the answers, where the harmful pattern reproduces but the single-answer benefit doesn't.

**Page 10 — The universal finding, distilled into two tables.**
The first table shows the paper's cleanest general claim: for *every one* of the six judges, crossing from one valid answer to two-or-more shifts the judge from "reference helps" to "reference inflates" — a step ranging from +0.46 to +2.55 depending on the judge. Interestingly, for weaker judges the harm arrives at full strength at just two valid answers and doesn't grow further (a switch, not a dial). The second table dissects the "just tell the judge any correct answer counts" idea: the instruction's benefit is ordered exactly by judge capability — it fully cures the strongest judge, partially helps the second-strongest, and does nothing (or slightly backfires) for the weak ones, and the same ordering holds under a differently-worded instruction, so it's not about phrasing.

**Page 11 — The picture, and the fix experiment begins.**
A full-width figure plots inflation against the number of valid answers for the two main judges under all four setups — you can see the referenced line lift off from the blind line as answers multiply, and the instruction line rescue only the stronger judge. Then the paper turns from diagnosis to treatment: rather than changing what the judge is *told*, change the *order of operations*. Two protocols are introduced, tested on a new pre-registered pool of 75 questions with exactly controlled answer counts: M1 has the judge score with no reference first, then shows the reference alongside that blind score with instructions to change it only for genuine errors; M2 has the judge score blind, then separately asks "is this answer valid?" and scales the score by that answer.

**Page 12 — The fix results table.**
The numbers for the mitigation experiment, pre-registered like the replication. A plain single reference adds +1.50 and +1.07 units of inflation for the two judges on multi-answer questions. M1 (blind-then-reveal) drops that to −0.05 and +0.16 — statistically nothing — while costing nothing on single-answer questions. M2 splits: it works perfectly for phi-4 but is *worse than a plain reference* for the weaker judge. The extensive notes disclose everything, including that M2 was implemented slightly differently from the registered plan (softly scaling scores instead of hard pass/fail), with a public amendment on record.

**Page 13 — Reading the fixes honestly, then the origin story.**
Three careful verdicts. The boundary prediction: confirmed — harm appears the instant uniqueness is lost. M1: it removes the *measured* inflation but only halves the judge's underlying bias toward key-matching answers, and that leftover bias didn't turn into inflation at any selection strength they could test — though stronger optimizers remain untested; also, "costs nothing on single-answer questions" is not the same as "preserves the reference's benefit there," and they say so. M2's failure is explained mechanically: the weaker judge's validity check gives key-matching answers a 0.998 multiplier and equally-correct non-matching ones 0.422 — the bias just moved into the new call. The page ends by introducing the exploratory study that started it all, flagged clearly as the rough draft whose analysis choices were partly made after seeing data.

**Page 14 — The paper's most persuasive picture.**
A full-page figure contrasting two worlds with the same protocol, the same judges, the same measurement. Left: unique-answer math, where adding the reference *collapses* inflation 4-to-13-fold at every judge size — the safeguard working as advertised. Right: multi-answer questions, where the same reference *raises* inflation at every judge size. One tool, opposite effects, and the only thing that changed is whether the question has one right answer or several.

**Page 15 — The exploratory numbers, and the big-picture section begins.**
The table for the original study: across all four judge sizes, adding the reference never helped and directionally always hurt, with the pooled effect statistically real, alongside the math contrast panel. The text is candid about its weaknesses — small pool, post-hoc analysis choices, four judges that share a model family so they aren't independent witnesses — which is precisely why the locked-in replication of page 5 carries the paper's headline (and found the effect 2.5× larger). Then the "boundary" section begins with the paper's unifying idea: it's *one* mechanism with two faces. When there's one right answer, matching the key *is* checking correctness, so anchoring is verification. When there are many, matching and correctness come apart, and the judge keeps matching anyway.

**Page 16 — The decision chart.**
A one-page practical table: for each thing you could do with a reference, what happens when answers are multiple, what happens when the answer is unique, whether it depends on the judge, and what it costs. Blind judging: safe but forfeits the reference's help. Checking membership in a known answer list: safe, no judge needed. A single reference: harmful under multiplicity. The instruction: works only for strong judges. Multiple references: unreliable. M1 (blind-then-reveal): the bolded row — removes the inflation, costs nothing, behaved the same for both judges, price is one extra judge call. M2: powerful but judge-dependent and can backfire.

**Page 17 — The rule to remember, then the confessions begin.**
The advice, stated as a procedure: *score blind first, then reveal the reference and let it correct only demonstrated errors.* Two honest qualifications ride along: about a third to three-fifths of the anchoring bias survives beneath the surface (just not as measurable inflation), and the fix wasn't shown to preserve the reference's single-answer benefit — only to not cost anything. Instructions don't substitute, and multiple references only spread the anchoring around. Then the Limitations section opens with the frankest items: the exploratory pool was small, its per-judge results individually inconclusive, and its automatic checker imperfect (though provably imperfect in the direction that *understates* the paper's effect).

**Page 18 — Limitations, the middle stretch.**
More boundaries drawn around the claims: the replication used only short constrained-answer problems (not essays or open-ended generation), one answer-writing model, and two judges. The four-judge ladder all comes from one model family, so the size-trend should be read cautiously. The controlled grid trades realism for precision — its tasks are narrow arithmetic families with few questions per cell — and the failure of the single-answer benefit on differently-generated text has a suspected but unproven cause (the answer-extraction step struggles with that model's formatting). The fix experiment covers only two protocols, two judges, one generator, and hasn't been tested against stronger optimization, long or unreliable references, or free-form tasks.

**Page 19 — Last limitations, the scholarly context, and the closing argument.**
The section ends with two disclosure paragraphs: which analyses were added after the fact (they carry no pre-registration weight and are labeled), and the standing puzzle that anchoring is proven causal for *scores* but doesn't fully explain inflation item-by-item. Related Work then situates the paper: others documented judges penalizing non-matching correct answers on static tests; this paper adds the dynamic result under selection pressure, the causal swap experiment, the exact boundary, and a working fix — and notes a popular technique (having the judge write its own reference first) is contraindicated on multi-answer tasks. The Conclusion begins: the reference is not a safety default but a conditional instrument with a sign.

**Page 20 — Conclusion wrap-up and the bibliography.**
The final paragraphs restate the takeaway hierarchy: with a unique answer and a capable judge, the reference collapses gaming; with multiple valid answers it becomes the attack surface, because the judge's trust in the key is exactly what gets exploited; the cure is ordering the judge's calls, not wording the prompt; and if you can enumerate the valid answers, skip the judge entirely and check membership. The rest of the page is the reference list.

**Page 21 — Appendix A opens: the ledger.**
The receipts drawer begins. The ledger table lists every prediction the authors committed to, when it was locked in (git commits and public OSF registrations with third-party timestamps), and how it fared — confirmed, partial, or falsified. The early rows cover the exploratory study's three predictions (all held directionally) and, importantly, its two admitted deviations, logged as deviations rather than buried.

**Pages 22–23 — The ledger, continued.**
The remaining rows: the grid predictions (including the one that was *falsified* — margins were predicted to grow with the number of valid answers and didn't for weaker judges), the replication row with all three confirmations, the mitigation predictions with their partial verdicts and the M2 implementation deviation, and a series of clearly-labeled post-hoc analyses that were added in response to review rounds — each marked as carrying no pre-registration weight. Reading these two pages, you can reconstruct exactly which claims were called in advance and which emerged from the data.

**Page 24 — Provenance, then the fine-print appendix begins.**
The provenance discussion concludes: every number traces to committed analysis scripts, seeds are fixed, and three earlier-draft errors are named and corrected (a wrong statistical test replaced, a script bug fixed, a mistaken "this test is uninformative" conclusion retracted). Appendix B then starts with two disclosures about the replication: how task difficulty was calibrated in a pilot (with a sensitivity check showing the two accidentally-reused pilot items change nothing), and the extraction-friction analysis showing the headline effect survives on the cleanest-to-grade questions at +0.90 — with the remaining gradient explained by question difficulty, which is the phenomenon itself, not a measurement bug.

**Page 25 — Two candid disclosures, then the checker audits.**
First, the swap experiment's registration note: the new references were deliberately chosen from answers the models actually produced (so the paired comparison would have data), which means that registration adds little beyond its timestamp — the within-sample comparison is what carries the causal weight. Second, the M2 deviation in full: registered as a hard pass/fail gate, implemented as a soft probability multiplier, publicly amended, with the silver lining that the soft version exposes its own failure mechanism. Appendix C then begins auditing the exploratory study's automatic checker: extraction worked on 100% of samples there, and an independent model adjudicated the checker's verdicts — finding no false positives but many false *negatives* (90% of rejected answers were actually valid).

**Page 26 — The audits' punchline, and one worked example.**
The audits conclude in the paper's favor: because the checker's mistakes fall only on *non-matching* answers — the ones blind judges pick — the errors made the paper's effect look *smaller* than it is, and re-running everything with corrected labels widened the result from +0.41 to +0.50, exactly as predicted. A saturation note explains why one secondary metric was always going to be flat. Finally, Appendix D walks through a single real question by hand: 20 of 64 answers are correct, both judges happen to pick correct answers, yet the referenced judge still "inflates" — because its scores are so polarized around key-matching that its winning pick sits much further above what the answer's true quality earns. It's the arithmetic that makes the paper's core unit tangible: inflation isn't about picking wrong answers, it's about the scoreboard drifting away from reality.
