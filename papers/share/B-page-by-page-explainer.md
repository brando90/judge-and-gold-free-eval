# Paper B — "Grading the Answer Key, Not the Student" — page-by-page in plain language

A simple-language walkthrough of `B-grading-the-answer-key.pdf` (24 pages, draft of Aug 22, 2026).

## TLDR

If a grading metric works by measuring how much a model's solution *resembles* the reference solution, it's really measuring how predictable the reference text is — a property of the benchmark, not the student. Such metrics can't grade individual solutions at all, and can rank models only on hard benchmarks with detailed references. The only cheap signal that genuinely grades is checking whether the final answer is correct — which is free — and nothing tested, including fancy completion-based verification, adds anything beyond it.

---

**Page 1 — Title, abstract, and intro.** Sets up the problem: many benchmarks ship a worked reference solution, and the cheapest way to grade a model's answer is to score how similar it is to that reference. The abstract gives away the whole story: across 17 models, 8 benchmarks, and human-graded solutions, no similarity-based metric can grade an individual solution. Similarity scores mostly measure how *predictable the reference text is*, not how good the student's solution is (hence the title — you're grading the answer key, not the student). The one cheap check that works is the one everyone already has for free: comparing final answers.

**Page 2 — The big summary table and contribution list.** Table 1 lines up every signal against blind human grades of reasoning soundness. Similarity metrics (TFA, TF-IDF, embeddings, unigram-F1, etc.) all land near zero, between −0.33 and +0.14 — useless. The free "is the final answer correct?" bit scores +0.92, beating even expensive LLM judges (+0.88 to +0.91). The bullet list names the four contributions: a null result (matching can't grade), a mechanism (scores are reference properties), a boundary (when matching can still rank models), and an unbeatable free baseline.

**Page 3 — What TFA is and the four research questions.** Explains teacher-forced accuracy: feed the model the gold solution token by token and count how many tokens it would have predicted. Argues the usual "cheap-to-expensive spectrum" framing is wrong — the real divide is *mechanism*: metrics that score resemblance to the reference text ("matching") versus signals that check whether the solution reaches a verifiable endpoint ("verification"). Previews the four answers: matching can't grade individuals (RQ1); because most of its variance belongs to the reference (RQ2); it can rank models only on hard benchmarks with rich references (RQ3); and nothing cheap beats the free answer-correct bit (RQ4).

**Page 4 — Setup.** The nuts and bolts: 17 models from 4 families (Qwen, Phi, OLMo, Mistral), 8 math/code benchmarks plus SWE-bench. Defines the five matching metrics and the three verification signals — executed tests, a "completion curve" (cut a solution partway, let a fixed model finish it, check if it reaches the right answer), and an answer-swap check — plus the free bit they all must beat.

**Page 5 — Human grading and statistics.** Humans graded 60 solutions on two separate 0–10 scales: soundness (is the reasoning valid?) and style (is it clear?), blind to model names and scores. A second independent rater agreed at 0.83, so a perfect metric could reach ~0.91 at most. Six predictions were registered in advance (committed to the repo before data was generated), and everything is reproducible from released scripts.

**Page 6 — RQ1: Can matching grade individual solutions? No.** All five matching metrics land in that near-zero band against human grades. For TFA and gold-token logprob the null is *structural* — they never even read the model's solution, so they couldn't grade it in principle. On code, similarity metrics are near zero against executed tests too. They even tried much stronger baselines (modern retrieval embeddings, an NLI entailment model, code AST similarity) — same null. The one exception, symbolic answer equivalence at +0.59, isn't a similarity signal at all — it's just the free answer-check in disguise.

**Page 7 — Figure 1 and RQ2 begins.** The figure shows the whole field visually: orange dots (matching metrics) clustered around zero, blue dots (verification signals) up high, with the free bit at the top. RQ2 explains *why*: decomposing TFA scores across models shows 83–92% of the per-solution variance is an "item effect" — a component identical across model families. In other words, the score is a property of the *problem's reference text*, the same for every model, not of the solution being graded.

**Page 8 — The mechanism, named and tested.** That item effect is "reference perplexity" — how hard the reference text is to predict. Then the killer intervention: replace each worked reference with just the line "The final answer is [answer]" and matching's ability to rank models collapses (+0.35 → +0.02). A control with content-free padding text of the same length stays collapsed, proving the collapse is about lost information, not shorter text.

**Page 9 — Figure 2 and RQ3.** The scatter plot: TFA vs. reference compressibility, correlation −0.895 — reference predictability alone explains 80% of TFA's variance. RQ3: matching *can* rank models, but only under three conditions: (1) averaged over a whole benchmark, never per item; (2) the reference must be a detailed worked solution worth predicting; (3) the benchmark must be hard enough that models differ. On easy, saturated benchmarks the ranking actually inverts.

**Page 10 — The boundary map (Table 2).** Per-benchmark ranking quality: strong on olympiad math (+0.86 on Omni-MATH), weak-to-negative on easy grade-school benchmarks (−0.25 on GSM8K). On saturated data every model predicts the reference about equally well, and what's left is writing style — which is why cross-family comparisons break while within-family ladders (same style) still work. Section 3.4 starts: every attempted "repair" of a matching metric failed.

**Page 11 — The prediction ledger and RQ4.** Table 3 lists the six pre-registered predictions: four hit, one sign flip (up-weighting late tokens made ranking *negative*, not just null), one falsification (completer strength turned out not to matter). RQ4: on these solutions nothing beats the free answer-correct bit at +0.92. The best verification signal, the completion curve, reaches +0.79 — significantly below the free bit — and adds no measurable grading information beyond it.

**Page 12 — Figure 3 and the two sweeps.** Left panel: the intuitive "repair" (weight later reference tokens more) makes ranking decay to −0.37. Right panel: the completion curve rises with truncation depth but never reaches the free bit's dashed line. Also shows the curve isn't an artifact of the completer model family (a phi-4 completer replicates it), and that completer *strength* barely matters — which falsified their registered prediction.

**Page 13 — Scoping the "beyond correctness" question.** Among solutions whose final answer is already correct, can any cheap signal tell sound reasoning from lucky reasoning? Answer: no detectable effect, and effects above ~0.4 are statistically ruled out. The unifying observation: every signal that works treats the reference only as a *checkable endpoint* (run the tests, compare the answer); every signal that treats it as a *similarity target* inherits the reference-predictability noise.

**Page 14 — Practical guidance and limitations begin.** Three rules: (1) check the final answer before buying any fancier signal; (2) never use a reference as a similarity target — only as context for readers or an endpoint for verifiers; (3) before trusting matching on a leaderboard, check that references are information-dense worked solutions and the benchmark isn't saturated — and report reference perplexity so readers can audit that. Limitations start: the human anchor is only 60 items graded by one annotator (who is an author), though an external blind rater agreed at 0.83.

**Page 15 — More limitations.** Honest caveats: the length-matched control tested only one padding template; the variance decomposition uses one statistical procedure; the completion-curve completers share a family with some graded traces (mitigated by the phi-4 replication); the "beyond correctness" question stays unresolved because intervals are wide at n=60; and trained similarity metrics (fine-tuned to score soundness) were never tested.

**Page 16 — More limitations, then related work.** Notes that all numbers now come from one canonical script (earlier drafts differed slightly), pending registrations have third-party OSF timestamps, and the SWE-bench result is preliminary (4 models). Related work: TFA came from Putnam-AXIOM (this paper characterizes it rather than inventing it); "compression tracks ability" at the model level is known prior art — the new part is the item-level decomposition; judge-audit papers called for exactly this kind of variance analysis.

**Page 17 — Conclusion and references begin.** The closing argument: reference-based evaluation isn't a spectrum from cheap-noisy to expensive-good — it's two different mechanisms. Matching measures the reference; verification measures the solution, but everything it captures here is already in the free answer bit. The open gap: problems where no final answer can be extracted (free-form proofs), where completion-based verification is the natural candidate but untested.

**Pages 18–19 — References, then Appendix A.1–A.3.** Citation list, then the exact model grid (all 17 models named), per-benchmark item counts (e.g., 500 MATH-500 items, 60-item human anchor, 492 executed code solutions), and the ladder-cell definitions.

**Page 20 — Appendix A.3–A.5.** The two small tables behind the ranking boundary: all twelve hard-benchmark family ladders rank correctly (+0.50 to +1.00), while on easy benchmarks five of twelve invert (Phi and OLMo flip). Also the verbatim human grading rubric and blind protocol, and completion-curve details (32 completions per solution).

**Page 21 — Appendix A.5–A.8.** Fine print on the phi-4 completer replication (rules out the possibility that excluded traces were harder), the two independent inter-rater reliability estimates (both agree, ceiling ~0.89–0.91), and exactly which model subsets each analysis used.

**Page 22 — Table 4 and Appendix A.9.** The twin of Table 1 with full 95% confidence intervals and per-row caveats — every matching metric's interval includes or nearly includes zero. A.9 details the four stronger post-hoc baselines reviewers requested (retrieval embedder, NLI entailment, code AST, symbolic equivalence) — all confirming the null except symbolic equivalence, which is just the free bit reimplemented.

**Page 23 — Appendix A.11.** One short paragraph: all scores, labels, per-token vectors, and pre-registered predictions are released in the repository.

**Page 24 — Table 5.** The master numbers table backing every single-number claim in the prose: completion-curve depth profiles, the completer sweep, the answer-line intervention endpoints, reliability cells, and the decomposition shares with their bootstrap intervals.
