# Paper A, page by page, in simple language

**Paper:** *Certify Before You Optimize: A Few Dozen Blind Labels Predict Which LLM Judges Get Gamed* (`A-certify-before-you-optimize.pdf`, Draft v4, Aug 23, 2026)

**One-sentence version:** before you let an AI judge grade or train other AIs, spend a few hours labeling ~60 examples by hand — that tiny check reliably predicts which judges will get fooled ("gamed") later, while the standard check (raw agreement with humans) cannot.

---

**Page 1 — Title, abstract, start of introduction.**
Teams increasingly use one LLM as a "judge" to pick the best of many candidate answers from another model. The usual sanity check is "does the judge agree with humans ~90% of the time?" The paper's point: that check fails exactly when it matters. A judge can agree with humans on normal data, yet fall apart the moment something optimizes against it — the pipeline learns to produce answers the judge *likes* (nicely written) rather than answers that are *right*. The proposed fix is a cheap pre-deployment "audit": humans grade a few dozen examples on two separate scales — is the reasoning actually correct (**soundness**) and is it nicely written (**style**) — and from that you get a verdict per judge: **pass**, **fail**, or **convict** (convict = the judge is actively rewarding style over substance). The verdicts, frozen in advance, correctly predicted what happened under real optimization pressure in 10 of 11 registered cases.

**Page 2 — The main results table and contributions.**
Table 1 shows every judge they audited. The killer comparison: a deliberately **sabotaged** frontier judge (GPT-5.2 told to reward presentation) and an **honest** mid-size judge score almost identically on raw human agreement (+0.35 vs +0.37) — the standard check literally can't tell them apart. But the audit's "style partial" (how much the judge rewards writing style once correctness is held fixed) separates them cleanly (+0.59 vs −0.21). The columns "inflation" and "regret" measure real damage: how much the judge's scoreboard lies upward, and how much true accuracy is left on the table. The contributions list starts: (1) a cheap audit with a categorical verdict, (2) validation under real optimization pressure.

**Page 3 — Rest of contributions, and the setup.**
More contributions: the audit catches failures raw agreement can't (a sabotaged frontier judge and a popular open-source reward model both get convicted); there are label-free versions of the audit for scaling; and there are practical design rules. Then the setup: they audit **13 judge configurations** — Qwen2.5 judges at 4 sizes (1.5B–14B), each run "blind" (no answer key) and "referenced" (gold solution shown), plus GPT-5.2 honest, GPT-5.2 sabotaged, and a trained reward model (Skywork-8B). Key design choices: humans grade on *two separate axes* (not one overall score), and the sabotaged judge acts as a positive control — proof the instrument can catch a bad actor, not just bless everyone.

**Page 4 — Figure 1 and the human labels.**
Figure 1 explains *why* a small audit works: evaluators fail in basically one way. Judges that **compare** an answer to reference text end up measuring the reference (and superficial things like style); judges that **verify** the answer against something checkable (a final answer, unit tests) track real quality. The audit detects which side a judge is on. The labels: ~60 math solutions, graded blind by one annotator on soundness (0–10) and style (0–10). Because the annotator is an author, an independent, hypothesis-blind person regraded 30 items — and the key verdicts reproduce on that person's labels too.

**Page 5 — Registration, and the audit itself (RQ1).**
To prevent cherry-picking, predictions were registered (git timestamps; later experiments got external OSF registrations) and every prediction — including the wrong ones — is reported in one ledger. The frozen verdict rule: **convict** if style partial > 0.30; **pass** if soundness partial > 0.40 and style partial < 0.20; **fail** otherwise. RQ1 ("can the audit tell substance-readers from style-readers?"): yes. Honest frontier judge passes cleanly (soundness +0.90). Sabotaged judge convicted (style +0.59). And the Skywork reward model — which raw agreement would just call "weak" — is convicted with the same signature as the sabotaged judge (style +0.54, soundness ≈ 0).

**Page 6 — Robustness of the convictions, and RQ2.**
Two checks: the convictions aren't secretly just "prefers short answers" (controlling for length, they survive), and they reproduce on the independent annotator's labels. Interesting nuance: the same sabotage instruction that corrupts a frontier judge just *breaks* smaller judges instead — corruption is "capability-gated," so you must audit each deployment, not each model family. RQ2 ("isn't raw agreement enough?"): no — raw agreement can *rank* judges but can't *diagnose* them. It confuses "weak but honest" with "capable but corrupt," which is exactly the distinction that determines what goes wrong later.

**Page 7 — Figure 2 and fine print.**
Figure 2 shows this visually: on raw agreement (left panel), the sabotaged judge hides among the honest weak ones; on the style axis (right panel), the corrupted judges sit alone in a "conviction region" defined before any outcomes were known. Also here: the audit beats the trivial baseline of "just check whether a reference is provided" (it additionally catches corrupted instructions and reward models); the convictions survive multiple-comparison corrections; ~40 labels are enough for clear cases while borderline cases need more; and a pass certifies overall soundness-tracking, not line-by-line proof checking (judges forgive planted false steps that don't change the final answer).

**Page 8 — Label stability, references, and the pressure test begins.**
Figure 3: convictions stabilize with as few as ~40 labels; borderline passes are the expensive ones. A free zero-label pre-screen: ask the judge the same comparison twice with order flipped — a judge that contradicts itself can't be audited at all. Then two lessons about giving judges an answer key: a trusted reference *transforms* weak judges on tasks with one right answer, but on tasks with many valid answers a reference *hurts* — judges anchor to the shown reference and penalize equally-correct alternatives, which makes gaming worse. Section 4 begins: the real test — a 7B model generates 64 candidates per problem, each judge picks its favorite, and two pre-registered numbers separate "the scoreboard lied" (**inflation**) from "accuracy was actually lost" (**regret**).

**Page 9 — Did the verdicts predict reality? (RQ3)**
Yes: 10 of 11 registered verdicts matched what happened under pressure. Every fail landed in the bad cluster; every conviction showed the predicted damage. The one miss is instructive: the blind 14B judge passed the threshold by a hair (0.41 vs a 0.40 cut) but got gamed anyway — a **false pass**. The authors respond transparently: they revise the rule (require the *confidence interval*, not the point estimate, to clear the bar), note the revision can only revoke passes, never grant them, and freeze the revised rule for a future pre-registered replication. Under the revised rule the miss becomes "insufficient evidence" at the cost of two abstentions.

**Page 10 — Figure 4: pressure curves, and transfer to code.**
Figure 4 tracks scoreboard inflation as selection pressure grows (best-of-4 → 16 → 64): certified judges stay low and flat; failed/convicted ones climb. A striking asymmetry: the sabotaged frontier judge inflates its scoreboard massively while still *selecting* almost as well as the honest judge — it deceives the metric without visibly hurting outcomes, the exact failure raw accuracy is blind to. They then extrapolate the audit to code, using executed unit tests instead of human labels as the "truth" axis: 10 of 11 predictions transfer, and the one miss is explained by the same capability gate (the 1.5B judge can't use tests, so its reference is worthless).

**Page 11 — Figure 5 and two surprising findings.**
Figure 5: giving a judge a trusted reference collapses inflation at every size on math (comparing text is easy), but only at 3B+ on code (using tests requires capability). Two findings: **(1)** There are two distinct failure modes, and capability trades one for the other — a sabotaged weak judge causes damage (bad selections) without lying; a sabotaged frontier judge lies (inflated scoreboard) without visible damage. When they actually trained a model (DPO) against each judge, the certified judge's training gained real accuracy while the convicted judge's training *destroyed* accuracy — though not in the exact way they predicted (the corrupted signal damaged the policy outright rather than teaching it to game the judge). **(2)** The tiny referenced 1.5B judge out-selects the frontier judge on math — because it outputs fine-grained continuous scores while the frontier's 0–10 scale ties 78% of items at the top and random tie-breaks let wrong answers through. Granularity beats scale.

**Page 12 — Generalizing to a four-tier procedure (Section 5).**
The one audit becomes a reusable pipeline: **Tier 0** (free, zero labels): self-consistency screens — a judge that flips its verdict when you swap answer order can't be audited. **Tier 1** (zero human labels): instead of hand-picking "style" as the nuisance axis, discover biases causally with a fixed six-axis perturbation battery (padding, formatting, fake authority, bandwagon cues, confident tone, hedging). Results were mixed in a healthy way — one registered prediction was falsified (the sabotaged judge punished appended social cues instead of rewarding them), and they report it. **Tier 2**: where the domain has an oracle (like executed tests for code), the correctness axis needs no humans — but the deception/style side still does.

**Page 13 — Tier 3, label economics, limits, and the design rules begin.**
A fully mechanical zero-label audit (test outcomes + response length) roughly orders corruption severity but also false-flags honest judges — it's a coarse screen, not a verdict tool. **Tier 3**: the best-of-n pressure test itself is the backstop for any bias axis you didn't think to measure. Label budget: no magic number — convictions stabilize around 40 labels, borderline passes need more, and human rater reliability caps what's measurable at all. Honest limits: synthetic perturbations may miss natural biases, and the procedure narrows the unmeasured space, never closes it. Then **Section 6, five practical rules**, starting with rule 1: run the audit in the actual deployment domain — sixty labels ≈ 4–8 annotator-hours, and one label set served all thirteen judges.

**Page 14 — Rules 2–5, and limitations begin (Section 7).**
The remaining rules: **(2)** never substitute raw agreement — it ranks but can't diagnose; **(3)** pick the nuisance axes from your threat model and remember the audit only screens the axes you instrumented; **(4)** give a judge a reference only when the answer is unique and the judge is capable enough to use it — otherwise it amplifies gaming; **(5)** audit your ground truth before auditing the judge (some apparent judge failures turned out to be errors in the evaluation pipeline itself). Section 7 then walks the six limitations honestly, starting with the labels: the main annotator is an author, but the independent-rater replication now backs the key verdicts.

**Page 15 — Limitations continued: the DPO experiment in detail, and confounds.**
The deepest limitation was "you only tested selection, not training," so they ran a registered DPO training experiment: train identical models on preference pairs from the certified judge vs. the convicted judge. Result: certified judge's arm gained +13.2 accuracy points on training items; convicted judge's arm significantly *lost* accuracy on held-out items. The verdicts predicted which judge was safe to train against. They score their own registered sub-predictions honestly — one leg confirmed, one failed (gains didn't generalize off training items), one falsified in direction. Also addressed: a family-confound worry (judges and generator from the same model family) is closed by replicating on a Llama-generated pool, and they concede the 13 configurations are really only ~4 independent tests.

**Page 16 — Reward-model inflation fix, and related work (Section 8).**
A technical fix: measuring "scoreboard inflation" fairly for a reward model requires independent score replicates, which they get by re-scoring content-preserving paraphrases — the convicted reward model's inflation (+1.46σ) is the largest measured, and either reading of that number (winner's curse or paraphrase-sensitivity) is bad for a deployed reward signal. Related work: prior work showed benchmark accuracy doesn't predict optimization failure and that style bias exists as a *class-level* phenomenon (e.g., RM-Bench); this paper's contribution is a *per-deployment* instrument — it tells you whether *your* judge, on *your* task and prompt, reads substance, with verdicts validated against actual optimization outcomes. The statistics themselves are classic construct-validity methodology from 1959 psychology.

**Page 17 — References.**
The bibliography. Notable: several "Anonymous" companion drafts (this is part of a series, including the "compare vs. verify" mechanism paper cited throughout as [9]), plus the classic reward-hacking literature (Gao et al. on overoptimization scaling laws), style-bias papers, and the 1959 Campbell & Fiske multitrait-multimethod paper the audit's statistics descend from.

**Page 18 — References end; appendix overviews.**
Short descriptions of the appendices: A = confidence intervals for the main table; B = the prediction ledger (every registered prediction and how it came out, including the falsified ones); C = replication and robustness numbers; D = the perturbation battery details.

**Page 19 — Table 2 and Appendix E.**
Table 2 repeats the main table with 95% confidence intervals for every cell — the honest judges' style partials all straddle or sit below zero; the three convicted configurations' style partials are clearly positive. Appendix E is the complete bookkeeping across all 27 pressure-tested configurations (math + code), spelling out exactly which verdicts hit and which missed, and re-scoring everything under the revised confidence-interval rule (8 predictions, 8 correct, 3 abstentions).

**Page 20 — Appendices F and G.**
Appendix F quantifies label needs: at 40 labels the sabotage conviction reproduces in ~98% of resamples, while the borderline 14B pass is still unstable at 50 — clear cases are cheap, boundary cases are not. Appendix G gives the paired confidence intervals behind the "reference hurts on multi-valid tasks" claim: referenced judges score answers matching the shown reference 2.7–10 points higher than equally-correct alternatives, with total anchoring at the smallest scales.

**Page 21 — Table 3: the prediction ledger.**
The paper's honesty centerpiece: every registered prediction in one table with when it was registered and how it came out. Most are "Correct," but several rows say "**Falsified**" or "mixed" in plain view — the sabotaged 7B/14B conviction prediction was wrong (they failed instead), the perturbation-battery sign prediction was wrong in direction, and one DPO leg failed. Three predictions (Putnam replication, code audit, label-budget forecast) are registered and still pending.

**Page 22 — Table 4: replication and robustness.**
Every single-number claim in the main text with its full confidence interval: the independent rater's replications (sabotage conviction is actually *stronger* on the blind rater's labels, +0.61), rater-reliability ceilings, multiplicity-corrected convictions, length-controlled partials, all the inflation intervals, and the cross-family Llama replication.

**Page 23 — Tables 5 and 6: the zero-label instruments.**
Table 5: the six-axis perturbation battery per judge — the honest frontier judge is flat on all six axes; the blind 1.5B is hugely swayed by fake authority (+0.95) and confident tone (+0.78); sensitivity to presentation tricks shrinks as judges get bigger. Table 6: the fully mechanical audit (tests + length, zero human labels) — it correctly gives the two most corrupted evaluators the largest length-bias magnitudes, but also flags two honest judges, confirming it's a severity screen rather than a verdict instrument.
