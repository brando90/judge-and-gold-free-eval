\title{Reference Answers Bias LLM Judges\\Against Other Correct Answers}

\author{Anonymous authors\\Paper under double-blind review}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
LLM judges are often given a reference answer, because checking a candidate against a known-correct answer is easier than judging it from scratch.
When a question has several correct answers, the reference is only one of them, and judges grade agreement with it.
Moving the reference to a different correct answer while keeping a candidate's text fixed changes its score by 2.7 to 5.4 points out of 10 for Qwen2.5-7B, phi-4, and Claude Sonnet~5, even for answers the judge had accepted without a reference.
With the reference shown, correct answers that differ from it are marked down: Qwen2.5-7B's per-task AUROC for separating them from wrong answers falls from 0.87 to 0.60 on constructed arithmetic tasks and from 0.74 to 0.66 on the natural \textsc{MACE} benchmark, and on factual questions the share of such answers scored 3/10 or below rises by 12.5 to 63.8 points for every judge we tested, including judges that think before scoring.
On arithmetic, judges that think before scoring barely anchor, but they already give nearly every correct answer full marks without a reference.
The cost to best-of-64 selection is narrower: on constructed tasks with several correct answers, once wrong answers that repeat the reference's value are set aside, accuracy falls by 15 to 21 points for three of four open judges, and we detect no change on \textsc{MACE} or for GPT-4.1.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Ask for any divisor of 91 strictly between 1 and 91.
Both 7 and 13 are correct.
Give an LLM judge the question, a candidate answer, and the reference answer 7, and it can do one of two things that look alike: check whether the candidate is right, or check whether it says 7.

We gave Claude Sonnet~5, with extended thinking off, correct answers to 93 questions that each have several correct answers.
Without a reference, it scored correct answers that happened to match the reference and correct answers that did not about the same, 9.19 and 9.09 out of 10.
With the reference in the prompt, matching answers rose to 9.99 and non-matching ones fell to 6.93 (\cref{fig:teaser}a), and the share of correct non-matching answers scored 3 or below rose from 4.9\% to 26.2\%.
With thinking on, the same judge still marks down correct answers that differ from the reference on factual questions it can verify without one (\cref{sec:fix}).

Reference-guided judging is standard practice.
MT-Bench's math and reasoning template tells the judge to begin ``by comparing the assistant's answer with the reference answer''~\citep{zheng2023judging}, references raise agreement with human labels~\citep{refguided2024}, judges without one are too generous~\citep{generous2026}, and a recent study of math grading found comparison against a reference to be the most reliable single-pass strategy~\citep{chen2026rethinking}.
The same judges grade benchmark answers, choose among sampled answers, and score rollouts for training, so what they reward is what gets optimized~\citep{gao2022scaling}.
Comparing against a reference is safe only when the reference is the only correct answer.
Curated math benchmarks mostly satisfy that, and open-domain questions often do not: in an LLM-labeled audit of 200 questions per dataset, 18.5\% of NQ-open questions admitted more than one correct answer (30.0\% counting answers that changed over time), compared with 2.5\% of GSM8K and DeepScaleR problems (\cref{app:prevalence}). Tasks that ask for an example, a construction, or a program admit many correct answers by design.

Prior work comes close to this failure without isolating it.
\citet{fixation2025} name it ``solution fixation'' but measure it by rewriting flawed solutions in the reference's style.
\citet{judgingref2026} and \citet{krumdick2025nofreelabels} replace the gold reference with an incorrect one, and work on verifier false negatives in reinforcement learning studies different surface forms of the same answer~\citep{xu2025tinyv,huang2025pitfalls}.
Human raters shown different reference translations favor candidates close to the one they see~\citep{fomicheva2016reference}, but no study of LLM judges that we know of varies which correct answer is the reference, or measures what that does to grading and selection.
We do this on tasks where correctness is decided by a program or an audited answer list, so we know every correct answer on our own tasks and nearly every one on the audited \textsc{MACE} domains:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{The score follows the reference} (\cref{sec:follows}). Moving the reference to a different correct answer, with the candidate's text unchanged, moves its score by 2.7 to 5.4 points, even for answers the judge had already accepted without a reference; correct answers that match neither reference barely move.
\item \textbf{The effect tracks an explicitly written answer} (\cref{sec:mechanism}). Writing the reference's answer in words kept most of the effect; an expression to evaluate or a worked solution with its answer masked removed most or all of it.
\item \textbf{Correct answers that differ from the reference are graded worse} (\cref{sec:cost}), on the replication pool and \textsc{MACE}, and on factual questions even by judges that think before scoring.
\item \textbf{The selection cost is narrower} (\cref{sec:cost}). It is 15 to 21 points on constructed multi-answer tasks for three of four open judges once look-alike wrong answers are set aside, and we detect none on \textsc{MACE} or for GPT-4.1.
\item \textbf{Mitigations are partial} (\cref{sec:fix}). Thinking removes the arithmetic effect but not the factual one, and blind-then-reveal and a zero-cost score rule each help in some pools and not others.
\end{itemize}

We mark results as preregistered on OSF\osf, pre-specified in a timestamped commit before the data existed\pre, pre-specified re-analyses of an effect whose direction was already known\reana, or post hoc\ph. \Cref{app:ledger} scores all 150 registered predictions: 86 confirmed, 25 not confirmed or falsified, 16 split across judges, and 23 not evaluable, not scored, or excluded.

\section{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Tasks.}
Our own tasks come from five constraint families (remainder systems, subset sums, Goldbach pairs, divisor counts, digit sums), for example ``name two primes that sum to 100.''
Each task stores its full set of correct answers and one member, drawn at random when the task is built, that serves as the reference.
The two main pools are a 120-task \emph{replication pool}\osf{} and a 75-task \emph{$k$-pool}\osf{} with exactly $k \in \{1,2,4,8,16\}$ correct answers per task, 15 tasks each.
A checker counts an answer by its \texttt{\textbackslash boxed\{\}} content, as the prompt requests, so replies that state a correct value in prose usually count as wrong (\cref{app:grid}).
We also use \textsc{MACE}~\citep{mace2026}, a benchmark of factual questions with 1, 2, 4, or 6 correct answers: its River and Language domains passed our completeness audit, and 133 of their tasks fall in the pass-rate band of our registered \textsc{MACE} analysis. MATH-500~\citep{hendrycks2021math} supplies unique answers (\cref{app:setup}).

\paragraph{Candidates, judges, and scores.}
Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct writes 64 samples per task at temperature 1 (Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct for MATH-500).
Each sample is judged \emph{blind}, from the question and the candidate, and \emph{referenced}, with the reference added under the heading ``Reference answer (trusted)''.
The main judges are Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct and phi-4; we also test Qwen2.5-14B, gemma-2-9b, Qwen3-8B and Qwen3-14B, Sonnet~5, and GPT-4.1, with more in the appendix.
Open judges answer with only an integer from 0 to 10, in at most four tokens and without reasoning, under rubric~$a$ (``using the reference to check it'') unless stated; their score is the expected value over the score tokens.
Our original scorer approximated the probability of ``10'' for tokenizers that write it as two tokens; a pre-specified rescoring computes it exactly, and we report the exact scorer except where stated (\cref{app:estimator}). phi-4 writes ``10'' as one token, so its scores are unchanged. We omit Mistral-7B from main-text comparisons because its tokenizer has no single-token digits, so its scores are parsed integers and a third of its blind replies contain none.

\paragraph{Measurements.}
The \emph{rotation change} is the change in the score of identical text when the reference moves.
The \emph{anchoring margin} is the mean score of correct answers that match the reference minus that of correct answers that do not.
For grading we report the \emph{false-rejection rate}, the share of correct answers that differ from the reference scored 3 or below, and the per-task AUROC of the score for separating correct from wrong answers; \emph{blind verification} is that AUROC without a reference.
For selection we report the accuracy of the best-of-64 pick, averaging over ties, on tasks whose pass rate lies between 0.2 and 0.8 (0.05 and 0.95 on \textsc{MACE}); on the $k$-pool this leaves 14 tasks at $k{=}1$ and 39 at $k{>}1$.
The preregistered endpoint of the OSF studies is best-of-64 \emph{inflation}: how many pool standard deviations the judge's score for its pick exceeds its scores for samples of the same correctness, which is what a policy optimized against the judge can exploit (\cref{app:setup}).
All contrasts are referenced minus blind, per task, with task-level bootstrap intervals.

\section{Moving the reference moves the score}
\label{sec:follows}

If the reference were an answer key, a candidate's score could not depend on which of two correct answers sits in the reference slot.
We kept the replication pool's samples byte-identical and re-judged them with the reference moved to another correct answer, the one that appears most often among each task's correct samples.

Text that the reference moves onto gains 5.25 points under Qwen2.5-7B\reana{} and 4.09 under phi-4, text it moves off loses 5.41 and 3.92, and correct text that matches neither barely moves (\cref{fig:teaser}b).\pre{}
Sonnet~5 shows the same pattern, gaining 3.04 and losing 2.66.\pre

Two readings would make this reasonable behavior.
In the first, the judge did not know the other answer was correct and the reference taught it; then answers the judge had already scored at 7 or above while blind should not move. They move just as much: by $+4.08$ for phi-4 and $+2.79$ for Sonnet~5,\pre{} and by $+5.27$ for Qwen2.5-7B under the exact scorer (\cref{tab:main-numbers}).\ph{}
In the second, the heading ``Reference answer (trusted)'' implies that the question has one answer. Telling the judge that other correct answers deserve full credit lowers Sonnet~5's anchoring margin by 0.93 points \ci{-1.22}{-0.65} from $+3.00$ but does not remove it.\pre{}
The effect is smaller for larger Qwen2.5 judges (\cref{app:rotation}) and nearly absent on arithmetic for judges that think before scoring (\cref{sec:fix}).

\section{The effect tracks an explicitly written answer}
\label{sec:mechanism}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.62\textwidth]{figures/fig3_mechanism.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{Anchoring shrinks when the reference does not state its answer outright.} Anchoring margin when the reference is written as a numeral, in words, or as an expression (replication pool, 93 tasks), and when a worked solution is shown with its final answer visible or masked (21 $k$-pool tasks with a single-number answer). Rubric~$a$, original scorer, 95\% intervals (\cref{tab:masking}).\pre}
\label{fig:mechanism}
\end{figure}

A cheap way to use a reference is to compare the candidate's final answer with the reference's.
Three pre-specified rewrites of the reference test whether judges do this (\cref{fig:mechanism}).
Writing 31 as ``thirty-one'' keeps most of the margin, although the reference then shares no characters with a numeric answer.
Writing it as ``30+1'' cuts the margin by 94\% for Qwen2.5-7B and 77\% for phi-4, although it still excludes zero, and masking the final answer in a worked solution removes it.\pre{}
Sonnet~5 behaves the same way (\cref{app:frontier}).
A control that encoded the reference with announced decoding steps also removed the margin, so we cannot say whether the property that matters is an answer that must be evaluated or an unusual framing (\cref{app:mechanism}).
Closeness to the reference matters little: a few tasks drive a registered negative slope with numeric distance, and the near, middle, and far thirds score alike (\cref{app:composition}).\pre

\paragraph{Correct answers that differ from the reference.}
A judge that grades by agreement should separate correct answers that differ from the reference from wrong answers less well once it sees the reference.
It does: for Qwen2.5-7B the per-task AUROC for these two groups falls from 0.87 to 0.60 on the replication pool and from 0.74 to 0.66 on \textsc{MACE} (\cref{fig:cost}a),\pre{} and phi-4 shows the same pattern (\cref{tab:r4-grading}).\ph{}
The judges mark such answers as failing: on factual questions the share scored 3 or below rises for every judge we tested, including Qwen3 and Sonnet~5 with thinking on (\cref{tab:false-reject}).

\paragraph{Grading overall.}
On \textsc{MACE}, overall AUROC rises by 0.07 \ci{+0.02}{+0.13}, as registered;\pre{} the rise comes from single-answer questions, where every correct answer matches the reference, and on multi-answer questions the change is 0.00 \ci{-0.05}{+0.05}.\ph{}
On the constructed pools overall AUROC falls, by 0.22 on the replication pool and by 0.14 to 0.23 at $k{>}1$ on the $k$-pool for four judges (\cref{tab:main-numbers}).\ph{}
Within \textsc{MACE}, tasks that the judge verifies well without a reference gain less from it (\cref{fig:cost}b), a post hoc lead confounded with room to improve.\ph

\paragraph{Selection.}
On the preregistered endpoint, the reference adds $+1.19$~\sd{} \ci{+1.03}{+1.35} of inflation on the replication pool.\osf{}
Accuracy, which is easier to interpret but post hoc on these pools, gives a narrower picture.
About a quarter of the wrong candidates on our constructed pools contain the reference's answer, most of them stating it without the required box, and the referenced judge favors them (\cref{app:grid}).
Setting these look-alikes aside, the reference lowers accuracy at $k{>}1$ on the $k$-pool by 15.4 points for Qwen2.5-7B and gemma-2-9b and by 20.5 for phi-4, with no loss at $k{=}1$, while Qwen2.5-14B's loss of 5.1 includes zero (\cref{fig:cost}c).\ph{}
With look-alikes included the losses are 15.4 to 35.9 points, and Qwen2.5-7B also loses 21.4 points at $k{=}1$.\reana{}
We detect no change on \textsc{MACE}'s multi-answer questions, where our registered prediction of a loss failed ($+2.0$ \ci{-5.1}{+10.1}),\pre{} or for GPT-4.1 on the $k$-pool, whose scores tie heavily ($-1.6$ \ci{-7.6}{+3.5}).\ph{}
Sonnet~5's selection accuracy cannot be computed, because only its scores for correct answers were collected.
On MATH-500, where correct answers rarely differ from the reference, the reference raises accuracy by 28.6 to 30.6 points for Qwen2.5-7B and 14B.\pre

\section{Mitigations are partial}
\label{sec:fix}

\label{fig:reasoning}

\paragraph{Thinking helps where judges already verify; explanations do not.}
Judges that think before scoring barely anchor on arithmetic: turning on Qwen3's thinking mode shrinks the rotation loss from $-0.69$ to $+0.01$ for Qwen3-8B and from $-1.98$ to $-0.12$ for Qwen3-14B, and asking Sonnet~5 to reason shrinks it from $-2.85$ to $-0.20$ (\cref{fig:reasoning}a).\pre{}
But these judges already give nearly every correct arithmetic answer full marks without a reference, and a written explanation under the MT-Bench template does not help: Qwen2.5-7B still loses 3.59 points.\pre{}
On factual questions, thinking leaves 44\% to 76\% of the increase in anchoring margin (\cref{fig:reasoning}b) and much of the increase in false rejections (\cref{tab:false-reject}).\pre

\paragraph{Instructions help only some judges.}
Telling the judge that other correct answers deserve full credit removes phi-4's margin on a synthetic grid at $k{=}16$ but does not help smaller Qwen2.5 judges, and it shrinks the margins of Sonnet~5 and GPT-4.1 without removing them (\cref{app:mitigation,app:frontier}).\pre

\paragraph{Blind, then reveal.}
Our preregistered mitigation scores the candidate blind, then reveals the reference together with that score.
It removes the preregistered inflation at $k{>}1$ for Qwen2.5-7B and phi-4,\osf{} and hiding the blind score brings the inflation back.\pre{}
In accuracy under the original scorer, it recovers two thirds of Qwen2.5-7B's loss at $k{>}1$ and all of Qwen2.5-14B's, but little of phi-4's or gemma-2-9b's (\cref{tab:acc-kpool});\ph{} it keeps nearly all of the reference's benefit on MATH-500;\pre{} and on \textsc{MACE} it did not beat the reference, as we had predicted it would.\pre{}
A zero-cost rule, taking the higher of the blind and referenced scores, does as well or better on the $k$-pool and \textsc{MACE} and worse on MATH-500 for Qwen2.5-7B, so no protocol is best in every pool (\cref{tab:score-rules}).\ph

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Reference-guided judging.}
References are the standard remedy for grading math and reasoning with LLM judges~\citep{zheng2023judging,refguided2024,chen2026rethinking}, and judges without them over-credit wrong answers~\citep{generous2026}.
Judges depend on the reference being correct: replacing it with a wrong answer collapses their accuracy~\citep{judgingref2026}, and without a correct reference they agree with experts mainly on questions they can answer themselves~\citep{krumdick2025nofreelabels}.
Even strong judges grading answers against references diverge from human judgments and are sensitive to prompt wording~\citep{thakur2024judging}.
\citet{fixation2025} describe a bias toward the reference's solution method, measured with flawed solutions rewritten in the reference's style.
We keep the reference correct and vary which correct answer it is.
Generating a reference before judging has been proposed as an improvement~\citep{dobefore2025}; our results predict that a self-generated reference anchors the same way on multi-answer tasks, which we have not tested.

\paragraph{Single references and many correct answers.}
Reference-based metrics in machine translation reward outputs that resemble the particular reference, which motivated paraphrased references~\citep{freitag2020bleu}; human raters shown a reference translation favor candidates close to it~\citep{fomicheva2016reference}; and execution-based evaluation of code replaced similarity to a single reference solution~\citep{chen2021codex}.
Open-domain question answering evaluation under-credits correct answers that are missing from the gold list or phrased differently from it~\citep{min2020ambigqa,si2021whatsinaname,bulian2022tomayto,kamalloo2023evaluating}; in our setting the gold list is correct and complete, and the judge still favors the one answer it is shown.
Rule-based and model-based verifiers in reinforcement learning miss correct answers written in a different form~\citep{xu2025tinyv,huang2025pitfalls,chen2025xverify}, and RL data pipelines often keep only problems that ``admit a single correct answer''~\citep{albalak2025bigmath}. \textsc{RewardBench-2} and \textsc{MACE} build benchmarks around questions with several answers~\citep{malik2025rewardbench2,mace2026}.

\paragraph{Judge biases and optimization.}
LLM judges favor the first response~\citep{wang2024fair}, longer responses~\citep{dubois2024lengthcontrolled}, and their own outputs~\citep{panickssery2024llm}, anchor on scores shown to them~\citep{kapetanovic2026anchoring}, and can be fooled by content-free responses~\citep{zhao2025onetoken}.
Optimizing against an imperfect evaluator inflates its scores~\citep{gao2022scaling,rafailov2024scaling}, and good average agreement does not guarantee good best-of-$n$ choices~\citep{landesberg2026bestofn}.
Closest to our protocol, a reference-free judge that commits to its own answer before seeing the candidate resists self-play reward hacking~\citep{zhou2026convincing}; blind-then-reveal applies the same idea to reference-guided judging.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Tasks and labels.} Our results come from constraint problems, two audited factual domains of \textsc{MACE}, and MATH-500, mostly with one generator, Llama-3.1-8B. Our constructed-pool checkers count unboxed answers as wrong, which enlarges the selection losses; we report them with look-alike answers set aside. \textsc{MACE}'s answer lists miss 2.3\% to 8.1\% of correct answers, and our prevalence labels come from LLM annotators of one model family. Our reference heading may itself suggest a unique answer.
\item \textbf{Judges.} The main open judges have at most 14B parameters and score in four tokens without reasoning, the regime where the arithmetic effect is largest. GPT-4.1's scores tie heavily, Sonnet~5's selection accuracy was not measured, and we did not test a reasoning judge from another frontier provider.
\item \textbf{Pools and scorers.} The selection cost rests on 39 constructed $k$-pool tasks and one natural pool. The blind-then-reveal comparisons and several appendix analyses use the original scorer.
\item \textbf{Registration.} Accuracy and false-rejection readouts on the OSF pools are post hoc, most registrations rely on timestamped commits, and we adopted the uniform verdict rule after most outcomes were known; it moved predictions in both directions (\cref{app:ledger}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

A reference answer is a good check when a question has one right answer.
When it has several, judges grade agreement with the one they are shown: moving the reference moves the score of unchanged text, and correct answers that differ from it are marked down, on factual questions even by judges that think before scoring.
How much this costs a best-of-$n$ pipeline depends on the pool and the judge. On our constructed tasks it cost three of four small open judges 15 to 21 points, and we found no cost on a natural factual benchmark or for GPT-4.1.
When correct answers can differ from the reference, audit the answers a reference-guided judge rejects before trusting its grades.

\subsection*{AI use statement}
In this work, generative AI models were the experimental subjects: open and frontier LLMs generated candidate answers and served as the judges under study (\cref{sec:setup}, \cref{app:setup}). One pilot (\cref{app:mathconstraint}) used a Claude model to generate candidate solutions, each verified by exact programmatic checks. We used generative AI tools to help write analysis code, draft and edit text, produce figures, search and verify related work, audit the preregistration ledger, and label a sample of dataset questions for the prevalence audit; both labeling passes came from one model family, were adjudicated against each other, and were not checked by human annotators. Every number in the paper is produced by committed analysis scripts from stored model outputs, and we checked every claim against those outputs. We take responsibility for the final content of this work, including text, claims, and artifacts produced with the aid of generative AI.

\subsection*{Ethics statement}
This work studies a failure of automated evaluation that can make optimized models look better or worse than they are. We release no new model and no attack on a deployed system. Anyone optimizing against a reference-guided judge on multi-answer tasks can exploit the failure, which is why we pair it with measurements of when it matters and recommend verifying judges on the tasks where they are used. No human subjects were involved.

\subsection*{Reproducibility statement}
\Cref{app:setup} specifies every pool, judge, prompt, and scorer, and \cref{app:ledger} lists each prediction with its registration type and outcome. The anonymized supplementary material contains task generators and correctness checkers, candidate pools, all judge score files, the analysis scripts that produce every number, table, and figure, and the registration documents with commit hashes replaced by stable tokens.
