\title{The Reference Is Not an Answer Key:\\LLM Judges Favor the Correct Answer They Are Shown}

\author{Anonymous authors\\Paper under double-blind review}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
LLM judges are routinely given a reference answer, as if it were an answer key.
We test whether it acts like one when a question has several correct answers.
On tasks whose correct answers are enumerated by a program or an audited list, moving the reference from one correct answer to another changes the score of identical text by 2.7 to 5.4 points out of 10 for Qwen2.5-7B, phi-4, and Claude Sonnet~5.
Correct answers that differ from the reference are rejected far more often (Qwen2.5-7B scores 28\% of them 3 or below, against 0.4\% without a reference) and are separated worse from wrong answers: grading AUROC falls by 0.07 to 0.27 for four open judges, depending on how answers are read, by 0.04 to 0.15 for Sonnet~5, and by at most 0.04 for GPT-4.1, whose scores tie heavily.
The effect is not only a failure to verify: of the factual answers Sonnet~5 scores 10 without a reference, it scores 19\% of those that differ from the reference 3 or below once the reference is shown.
Reasoning before scoring removes the effect on arithmetic and roughly halves it on factual questions.
A simple protocol removes the grading harm: scoring each answer with and without the reference and keeping the higher score leaves grading AUROC within 0.03 of blind judging, while keeping much of the reference's benefit when the answer is unique.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Ask for a two-digit number that is divisible by 5 and whose digits sum to 8.
Both 35 and 80 are correct.
Qwen2.5-7B, shown a candidate that reasons its way to 80, scores it 10.0 out of 10 with no reference, 2.9 when the reference answer is 35, and 9.3 when the reference is 80 (\cref{fig:concept}b).
The text never changed; only the correct answer the judge was shown did.
This example swaps more than most, but over 120 such tasks the same judge moves correct text by about 5 points (\cref{fig:concept}c).

Reference-guided judging is standard practice.
MT-Bench's math and reasoning template tells the judge to begin ``by comparing the assistant's answer with the reference answer''~\citep{zheng2023judging}, references raise agreement with human labels~\citep{refguided2024}, judges without one are too generous~\citep{generous2026}, and comparison against a reference is the most reliable single-pass strategy for grading math~\citep{chen2026rethinking}.
The same judges grade benchmarks, choose among sampled answers, and score rollouts for training~\citep{gao2022scaling}.
The practice is safe when a question has one correct answer, as on curated math benchmarks: our LLM-labeled audit of 200 questions per dataset (not checked by humans) found 0\% to 2.5\% with several on MATH-500, GSM8K, and DeepScaleR (\cref{app:prevalence}).
Whether it is safe where answers are not unique has not been tested, although such questions are common: 30\% of NQ-open questions in the same audit (18.5\% excluding answers that changed over time), benchmarks built around several valid answers such as \textsc{RewardBench-2} Ties and the factual benchmark \textsc{MACE}~\citep{malik2025rewardbench2,mace2026}, and tasks that ask for an example or a construction.

\paragraph{Three readings of a reference.}
If the reference is an \emph{answer key}, the judge checks a candidate against every correct answer, and a correct answer scores the same whichever one is shown.
Under \emph{verification failure}, the judge cannot tell on its own that a different answer is correct, so the reference is its only evidence.
Under \emph{agreement}, the judge favors the correct answer that matches the reference even when it can recognize the others, whether because it rewards agreement, because it assumes one intended answer, or because the prompt tells it to use the reference; we separate the last of these only partly (\cref{sec:p3}).
We test three predictions. P1 and P2 test the answer key; only P3 separates the other two readings.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=2pt]
\item[\textbf{P1}] \textbf{The score follows the reference.} Swapping which correct answer is the reference moves the score of unchanged text (\cref{sec:p1}).
\item[\textbf{P2}] \textbf{With several correct answers, the others are graded worse.} Correct answers that differ from the reference lose ground against wrong answers; with one correct answer, every reading allows the reference to help (\cref{sec:p2}).
\item[\textbf{P3}] \textbf{The effect survives verification.} It persists on correct answers the judge already accepts without a reference, which verification failure rules out (\cref{sec:p3}).
\end{itemize}
\Cref{tab:summary} gives what we observed for each. In short, all three hold for judges that score without reasoning first; reasoning before scoring removes the effect on arithmetic but not on factual questions; selection suffers mainly where no correct candidate matches the reference; and keeping the higher of the blind and referenced scores removes the grading harm (\cref{sec:cost}). Our evidence covers constructed arithmetic tasks, two domains of \textsc{MACE}, open judges up to 14B parameters, and two API judges.

Prior work comes close without isolating this failure.
\citet{fixation2025} name ``solution fixation'' but measure it with flawed solutions rewritten in the reference's style; \citet{judgingref2026} and \citet{krumdick2025nofreelabels} replace the reference with a wrong one; \citet{kapetanovic2026anchoring} show judges anchor on scores they are shown; and human raters shown different reference translations favor candidates close to the one they see~\citep{fomicheva2016reference}. Benchmarks with several valid answers score judges against all of them but do not vary which one the judge is shown, and no study of LLM judges that we know of does.

\section{A model of the three readings}
\label{sec:model}

Let $A^\star$ be the set of correct answers to a question and $a^\star \in A^\star$ the one shown as the reference. Let $b(c)$ be a judge's blind score for candidate $c$, $s(c; a^\star)$ its referenced score, and $v_c \in [0, 1]$ how reliably the judge recognizes $c$ as correct without a reference. We write
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
s(c; a^\star) \;\approx\;& \beta\, b(c) \;+\; \kappa\, \mathbb{I}[\,\mathrm{ans}(c) \in A^\star\,] \;+\; \lambda(v_c)\, \mathbb{I}[\,\mathrm{ans}(c) = a^\star\,] \\
&+\; \gamma\, \mathbb{I}[\,c \text{ is wrong and contains } a^\star\,] \;+\; \varepsilon .
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:model}
\end{equation}
An answer key checks candidates against the whole set: $\kappa \ge 0$ and $\lambda = \gamma = 0$. The other two readings reward matching the one answer shown; since only differences in score matter, the bonus $\lambda$ can equally be a penalty on correct answers that do not match. Under verification failure the bonus vanishes once the judge can recognize the answer itself ($\lambda(v) = \lambda_0(1-v)$); under agreement it does not ($\lambda(1) > 0$).
With one correct answer, $A^\star = \{a^\star\}$ and the $\kappa$ and $\lambda$ terms coincide, so every reading allows the reference to help (P2$'$).
With several, both non-key readings predict P1: swapping $a^\star$ moves fixed text by about $\lambda$. P2 needs more than P1, because a bonus for matching answers does not change how non-matching correct answers rank against wrong ones. It needs one of two further effects: the referenced score keeps only part of the judge's own assessment ($\beta < 1$), so noise weighs more, or wrong answers that contain $a^\star$ collect part of the bonus ($\gamma > 0$).
The two non-key readings differ only where $v_c$ is high, which P3 tests. We take a blind score of 10 as the judge accepting an answer, and per-task blind AUROC as a coarser measure of how well it verifies.
\Cref{eq:model} organizes the predictions; it is not a fitted model (a fit, with its caveats, is in \cref{app:composition}).

\section{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Tasks.}
Our own tasks come from five constraint families (remainder systems, subset sums, Goldbach pairs, divisor counts, digit sums); each stores its full set of correct answers and one member, drawn at random, as the reference.
The \emph{replication pool} (120 tasks) was built to replicate an exploratory observation on \textsc{RewardBench-2} Ties (\cref{app:ties}), and the \emph{$k$-pool} has 75 tasks with exactly $k \in \{1,2,4,8,16\}$ correct answers.
\textsc{MACE} contributes factual questions with 1, 2, 4, or 6 correct answers from its River and Language domains, which passed our completeness audit; the reference is drawn at random from each answer list, and answers are matched to it by normalized string containment (\cref{app:mace}). MATH-500~\citep{hendrycks2021math} supplies unique answers. Selection analyses use the tasks whose pass rate lies in $[0.2, 0.8]$, where selection can change correctness; for \textsc{MACE} these are 133 tasks.

\paragraph{Candidates and judges.}
Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct writes 64 samples per task at temperature 1 (Qwen2.5-7B for MATH-500).
Each sample is judged \emph{blind}, from the question and the candidate, and \emph{referenced}, with the reference under the heading ``Reference answer (trusted)'', under two rubric wordings.
\emph{Score-only} judges answer with an integer from 0 to 10 and nothing else: the open judges Qwen2.5 (1.5B to 14B), phi-4, and gemma-2-9b, whose score is the expected value over the score tokens; Mistral-7B, whose scores are parsed integers; and Claude Sonnet~5 and GPT-4.1 through their APIs. For tokenizers that write ``10'' as two tokens, the \emph{exact scorer} adds one decoding step to split that probability between 1 and 10. The main text uses it for every replication, $k$-pool, \textsc{MACE}, and MATH-500 result for Qwen2.5 and gemma-2; the exploratory Ties pool, the tests of how the reference is written, and the protocol comparisons of \cref{tab:protocols} use an earlier approximation that differs by 0.07 to 0.17 points per score (\cref{app:estimator}).
Other judges write before a final ``Score: N'' line, in one of two conditions: \emph{explanation first}, with no thinking mode (Qwen3-8B and Qwen3-14B with thinking off, Qwen2.5-7B under the verbatim MT-Bench template, and Sonnet~5 through its command-line interface (CLI) asked to explain first); and \emph{thinking on} (Qwen3 with thinking on, and Sonnet~5's CLI with extended thinking). Sonnet~5's CLI and API scores agree exactly on only 67\% of shared calls, so we compare Sonnet~5 results only within an interface.
Qwen2.5-7B, phi-4, Qwen2.5-14B, and gemma-2-9b passed a pre-specified score-resolution screen, which requires enough distinct score values and few candidates tied at the maximum score (\emph{screened} judges). Mistral-7B's and GPT-4.1's scores tie too often to rank candidates, so we leave them out of selection summaries but report their grading AUROC, which handles ties (\cref{app:mitigation}).

\paragraph{Reading answers.}
The prompt asks for the final answer inside \texttt{\textbackslash boxed\{\}}, and our registered checkers read it from there, so a correct answer written in prose usually counted as wrong; 38\% to 43\% of replies ignore the format.
On the constructed pools the main text therefore uses \emph{any-format labels}, which credit the final answer a reply states in any format.\ph{} The registered labels, a labeling in which Qwen2.5-14B reads each unboxed reply (it agrees with the any-format rule on 88\% of them), and boxed replies alone are compared in \cref{app:grid}; they give the same direction for every grading result of the screened judges and Sonnet~5.

\paragraph{Measurements and conventions.}
The \emph{swap change} is the change in score of identical text when the reference moves. The \emph{false-rejection rate} is the share of correct answers that differ from the reference scored 3 or below, and the \emph{grading AUROC} is the per-task AUROC for separating those answers from wrong answers. \emph{Best-of-64 accuracy} is the correctness of the highest-scored candidate, averaged over ties. The \emph{margin} is the mean score of correct answers that match the reference minus that of correct answers that do not. Contrasts are referenced minus blind, per task, with task-level bootstrap intervals. Changes in scores are in points out of 10, and changes in rates and accuracies in percentage points (pp). We do not correct for multiplicity.
Our OSF-preregistered primary endpoint was best-of-64 \emph{inflation}: how far above an average answer of the same correctness the chosen answer is scored, in standard deviations. It was confirmed ($+1.19$~\sd) and survives relabeling ($+1.14$~\sd{} with any-format labels; \cref{app:setup,app:grid}). We lead with the measures above because they are easier to interpret; inflation is a composite that also depends on how much of its own assessment the referenced judge keeps and on look-alike wrong answers (\cref{app:composition}).
Unmarked results were pre-specified in a timestamped commit or on OSF; results marked \ph{} are post hoc, and results marked \reana{} are pre-specified re-analyses of an effect whose direction was already known. Of 156 registered predictions, 92 were confirmed, 25 were not confirmed or were falsified, 16 held for some judges but not others, 18 could not be evaluated, 2 were not scored, and 3 came from a command-line check we exclude (\cref{app:ledger}).

\section{Prediction 1: the score follows the reference}
\label{sec:p1}

We re-judged the replication pool's byte-identical samples with the reference moved to another correct answer.
Text the reference moves onto gains 5.25 points under Qwen2.5-7B,\reana{} 4.09 under phi-4, and 3.04 under Sonnet~5; text it moves off loses 5.41,\reana{} 3.92, and 2.66; correct text that matches neither reference barely moves (\cref{fig:concept}c). These are the first rubric wording; the second gives changes of 2.5 to 6.6 points (\cref{app:rotation}).
All twelve registered cells (two judges, two rubric wordings, three readouts) went as predicted. On the exploratory Ties pool the swap is 8.7 to 9.8 points for the 1.5B and 3B Qwen2.5 judges and 2.8 to 3.8 for 14B (\cref{app:rotation}). A registered near, middle, and far ordering by numeric distance from the reference failed (\cref{app:composition}). We did not run the swap test on GPT-4.1.

The pull depends on the reference stating its answer outright. Writing 31 as ``30+1'' cuts the margin by 94\% for Qwen2.5-7B and 77\% for phi-4, and on 21 $k$-pool tasks hiding the final answer of a worked solution removes it, while spelling it out as ``thirty-one'' cuts Qwen2.5-7B's margin by 38\% and leaves phi-4's almost unchanged. Sonnet~5 goes from $+3.06$ with a numeral to $+3.14$ with words and $+0.47$ with an expression (\cref{app:mechanism,app:frontier}). These tests are arithmetic only.

\paragraph{Verdict.} P1 holds for every judge we swapped that does not reason before scoring, and for open judges that explain first. It fails on arithmetic with a thinking mode, and for Sonnet~5 already when it explains first (\cref{sec:p3}).

\section{Prediction 2: with several correct answers, the others are graded worse}
\label{sec:p2}

\paragraph{One correct answer.}
Showing only the final answer raises best-of-64 accuracy on MATH-500 by 28.6 and 30.6~pp for Qwen2.5-7B and 14B,\reana{} as the full solution does, and on \textsc{MACE}'s single-answer questions by 29.4\ph{} and 26.5~pp\ph{} for Qwen2.5-7B and phi-4, whose grading AUROC there rises by 0.29 and 0.23\ph{} (\cref{fig:onevsseveral}). Our constructed $k$-pool cannot test this: on its single-answer tasks, with answers read in any format, the reference does not change the accuracy of any screened judge's pick on any task.\ph

\paragraph{Several correct answers.}
Correct answers that differ from the reference are rejected more often. On the replication pool Qwen2.5-7B scores 28\% of them 3 or below with the reference and 0.4\% blind; with answers read in any format the rise is 27~pp,\ph{} and across the four screened judges and both constructed pools it ranges from 6.5 to 54~pp, varying by about 3~pp or less across the four labelings (\cref{app:grid}).\ph{}
Their grading AUROC against wrong answers falls by 0.27 and 0.24 on the replication pool and by 0.17 to 0.25 on the $k$-pool at $k{>}1$ for the four screened judges\ph{} (\cref{fig:onevsseveral}b). With boxed replies alone the fall is 0.07 to 0.14;\ph{} we do not separate how much of the difference comes from look-alike wrong answers and how much from which replies use the format.
On \textsc{MACE}'s multi-answer questions the AUROC falls by 0.08 for Qwen2.5-7B and 0.06\ph{} for phi-4.
Sonnet~5's falls by 0.04, 0.08, and 0.15 on the replication pool, $k$-pool, and \textsc{MACE}, where its false rejections rise by 36~pp. The second API judge shows much less: GPT-4.1's AUROC falls by 0.04 on the replication pool and does not change reliably on the $k$-pool,\ph{} although its scores tie so often that they rank candidates poorly. Mistral-7B's falls by 0.08 on the $k$-pool,\ph{} and not at all when only boxed replies are scored (\cref{app:frontier,app:grid}).

\paragraph{Verdict.} P2 holds for the four screened open judges and Sonnet~5 on constructed pools, and for Qwen2.5-7B, phi-4, and Sonnet~5 on \textsc{MACE}; for GPT-4.1 the effect is small. With one correct answer the reference helps, as every reading allows.

\section{Prediction 3: the effect survives verification}
\label{sec:p3}

\paragraph{Answers the judge accepts without a reference.}
On 40 factual \textsc{MACE} questions with one candidate of each kind (a correct answer that matches the reference, one that does not, and a wrong answer), Sonnet~5 (CLI) without a reference ranks both correct answers above the wrong one 94\% of the time with no thinking and 97\% with thinking on. The reference still widens the margin between the two correct answers by 4.0 and 3.0 points (\cref{app:reasoning}).
Sonnet~5's API subsamples give a more direct test, with matching answers as a comparison group. Of the 81 non-matching correct factual answers it scored a full 10 blind, it scores 19\% at 3 or below once shown a different correct answer; of the 38 matching correct answers it scored 10 blind, it scores none that low. On the constructed pools the rates are 9\% and 15\%, again against none of the matching answers (\cref{app:estimator}).\ph{} We did not measure how often Sonnet~5 would move a blind 10 that far on a second blind scoring.
For Qwen2.5-7B, the swap shrinks as its blind AUROC rises across replication tasks, but it is still 3.6 points in the third of tasks where that AUROC averages 0.98 (\cref{app:reasoning}).\ph

\paragraph{Not only the prompt.}
Every referenced prompt calls the reference trusted and tells the judge to use it. Two results show the effect is not only that instruction. Under the verbatim MT-Bench template, which does not call the reference trusted, Qwen2.5-7B still loses 3.59 points on the swap; and told that any correct answer deserves full credit even if it differs from the reference, Sonnet~5 keeps a margin of $+2.08$ \ci{+1.14}{+3.14} on the $k$-pool, against $+3.00$ without that sentence (\cref{app:frontier}). The same sentence closes phi-4's margin on one synthetic pool but failed its registered test for Qwen2.5-1.5B, 3B, and 7B (\cref{app:mitigation}).

\paragraph{Reasoning before scoring.}
On arithmetic, reasoning before scoring removes the effect (\cref{fig:reasoning}a). Sonnet~5's swap loss falls from 2.85 points when it scores only to 0.20 when it explains first and 0.32 with thinking on. Open judges need a thinking mode: Qwen3-14B loses 1.98 points with thinking off and 0.12 with it on (Qwen3-8B: 0.69 and none), while Qwen2.5-7B under the MT-Bench template still loses 3.59.
On factual questions, thinking roughly halves the effect for Qwen3: false rejections rise by 22 and 18~pp with it on against 44 and 42~pp with it off (\cref{fig:reasoning}b). For Sonnet~5 the reduction in margin, 0.95 points \ci{-2.45}{+0.63}, is not distinguishable from zero, and its false rejections rise by 17.5~pp without thinking and 12.5~pp with it.
On arithmetic, thinking leaves blind AUROC unchanged (0.85 to 0.86 for Qwen3-14B; 0.86 in both modes for Qwen3-8B\ph); on factual questions it raises Qwen3-14B's from 0.72 to 0.81 (Qwen3-8B: 0.78 to 0.80\ph), so better verification may contribute there (\cref{app:estimator}). Why reasoning removes the effect on arithmetic, our data do not say.

\paragraph{Verdict.} P3 holds: Sonnet~5 penalizes factual answers it already accepts, with and without thinking, and Qwen2.5-7B still moves arithmetic answers on tasks it verifies almost perfectly, so the effect is not only a failure to verify. Where reasoning removes the effect, P3 cannot be tested.

\section{Consequence: grading harm is robust, selection harm is conditional}
\label{sec:cost}

\begin{table}[t]
\centering
\footnotesize
\caption{\textbf{Keeping the higher score removes the grading harm and protects multi-answer selection, at a cost with a unique answer.} Change against blind judging. \emph{Max rule}: score each candidate blind and with the reference and keep the higher score. \emph{Blind, then reveal}: score blind, then show the reference and the blind score and ask the judge to revise only if the candidate is wrong. \paragraph{Natural data.}
On \textsc{MACE}'s multi-answer questions the reference did not lower best-of-64 accuracy ($+2.0$~pp for Qwen2.5-7B\ph{} and $+6.1$~pp for phi-4, both intervals including zero; \cref{fig:onevsseveral}a), and our registered prediction of a loss was not confirmed. The gains of 26 to 29~pp on the 34 single-answer questions outweigh the null on the 99 multi-answer ones, so over the natural mix the reference raises accuracy by 9 to 11~pp (\cref{tab:protocols}).

\paragraph{Constructed pools.}
With answers read in any format, the reference lowers $k$-pool accuracy at $k{>}1$ by 30.3~pp for Qwen2.5-7B (24.2 under the original scorer of \cref{tab:protocols}) and by 15.2~pp for phi-4, whose interval reaches zero, and leaves Qwen2.5-14B and gemma-2-9b within 3~pp\ph{} (\cref{fig:onevsseveral}a). The larger losses we first measured under the registered labels (15 to 36~pp for Qwen2.5-7B, Qwen2.5-14B, and gemma-2-9b\reana) were mostly prose answers counted as wrong (\cref{app:grid}).
The remaining losses sit where no correct candidate matches the reference. On the 11 $k$-pool tasks without one, Qwen2.5-7B loses 64~pp \ci{-91}{-36}, phi-4 45~pp \ci{-73}{-18}, Qwen2.5-14B 18~pp \ci{-46}{+18}, and gemma-2-9b 9~pp \ci{-27}{0}, against at most 14~pp on the 22 tasks with one; on the replication pool Qwen2.5-7B loses 31~pp without one (16 tasks) and 5~pp with one (\cref{app:estimator}).\ph

\paragraph{Protocols.}
Keeping the higher of the blind and referenced scores removes the grading harm (\cref{tab:protocols}): on the $k$-pool and \textsc{MACE} it leaves grading AUROC within 0.03 of blind judging for every judge, where showing the reference lowers it by 0.06 to 0.25.\ph{} It also recovers blind accuracy on the $k$-pool and keeps the reference's accuracy on \textsc{MACE}. With a unique answer it gives up part of the reference's benefit: 6.7~pp of accuracy \ci{-12.4}{-1.9} and half the AUROC gain on MATH-500 for Qwen2.5-7B, and little for Qwen2.5-14B.\ph{} Blind-then-reveal protects selection similarly but not grading, except for Qwen2.5-14B. For Sonnet~5, whose subsamples always contain a matching correct answer, the max rule's accuracy was 2.4~pp \ci{-5.0}{-0.5} below blind on the $k$-pool.\ph{} Showing several correct references removed the margin on one synthetic pool but largely failed for Qwen2.5-7B on another (\cref{app:mitigation}).

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Reference-guided judging.}
References are the standard remedy for grading math and reasoning with LLM judges~\citep{zheng2023judging,refguided2024,chen2026rethinking}, and judges without them over-credit wrong answers~\citep{generous2026}.
Judge models trained to take a reference as input assume it is a gold answer: JudgeLM studies how judges behave when a reference is added or removed~\citep{zhu2023judgelm}, and Prometheus conditions its rubric scores on one~\citep{kim2024prometheus}; the swap test asks what such judges do when the reference is one of several correct answers.
Judges depend on the reference being correct~\citep{judgingref2026,krumdick2025nofreelabels}, diverge from humans under reference-based grading~\citep{thakur2024judging}, and favor the reference's solution method~\citep{fixation2025}. Generating a reference before judging has been proposed as an improvement~\citep{dobefore2025}; we did not test self-generated references on multi-answer tasks.

\paragraph{Single references and many correct answers.}
Reference-based metrics in translation reward outputs that resemble the particular reference~\citep{freitag2020bleu,fomicheva2016reference}, and code evaluation moved from similarity to execution~\citep{chen2021codex}.
Open-domain question answering under-credits correct answers that are missing from the gold list or phrased differently~\citep{min2020ambigqa,si2021whatsinaname,bulian2022tomayto,kamalloo2023evaluating}; in our setting the list is complete and the judge still favors the one answer it is shown.
Verifiers in reinforcement learning miss correct answers written in a different form~\citep{xu2025tinyv,huang2025pitfalls,chen2025xverify}, as our own boxed-answer checker did.

\paragraph{Judge biases and optimization.}
LLM judges favor the first response~\citep{wang2024fair}, longer responses~\citep{dubois2024lengthcontrolled}, their own outputs~\citep{panickssery2024llm}, and scores shown to them~\citep{kapetanovic2026anchoring}. Optimizing against an imperfect evaluator inflates its scores~\citep{gao2022scaling,rafailov2024scaling}, and good average agreement does not guarantee good best-of-$n$ choices~\citep{landesberg2026bestofn}. A judge that commits to its own answer before seeing the candidate resists reward hacking~\citep{zhou2026convincing}.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Labels.} The registered checkers counted most prose answers as wrong. The any-format labels used in the main text are post hoc, agree with a model's reading on 88\% of unboxed replies, and were not checked against human labels; boxed replies alone give the conservative grading estimates. \textsc{MACE}'s answer lists miss 2.3\% to 8.1\% of correct answers, and matching by normalized string containment can miscount aliases (\cref{app:mace}).
\item \textbf{Tasks.} The constructed pools come from five arithmetic families with one generator, and the natural data from two \textsc{MACE} domains (133 questions); open-domain question answering such as NQ-open is untested.
\item \textbf{Judges and prompts.} All open judges have at most 14B parameters, and of our two frontier judges one, GPT-4.1, shows a much weaker effect, possibly because its scores tie. Sonnet~5's subsamples were drawn to contain every kind of answer, so its AUROCs are not directly comparable to the 64-candidate open-judge values, and its thinking and grading results come from different interfaces. Our own referenced prompts called the reference trusted and told the judge to use it; only the MT-Bench template and the full-credit instruction test this (\cref{sec:p3}).
\item \textbf{Mechanism.} No experiment manipulates verification directly, so we cannot say why reasoning removes the effect on arithmetic.
\item \textbf{Downstream.} Three DPO runs against the referenced judge did not show the policy copying the reference, and the protected arm failed its manipulation check, so we have not shown that training exploits the bias (\cref{app:dpo}).
\item \textbf{Registration.} Most registrations rely on timestamped commits, and the uniform verdict rule was applied retroactively to earlier predictions after most of their outcomes were known (\cref{app:ledger}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

A reference answer is a good check when a question has one right answer.
When it has several, judges favor the one they are shown: swapping it moves the score of unchanged text by 2.7 to 5.4 points, correct answers that differ from it are rejected far more often, and this happens even for answers the judge accepts without a reference.
Reasoning before scoring removes the effect on arithmetic and roughly halves it on factual questions, and best-of-64 selection loses accuracy mainly when no correct candidate matches the reference.
Where correct answers can differ, a low reference-guided score on an answer that differs from the reference is not evidence that the answer is wrong. On our pools, scoring every answer both with and without the reference and keeping the higher score removed the grading harm for the open judges and kept their multi-answer selection accuracy; its cost is on unique answers, where it gave up up to 6.7~pp of the reference's 30.5-pp accuracy gain and half of its grading gain for Qwen2.5-7B.

\subsection*{AI use statement}
Generative AI models were the experimental subjects: open and frontier LLMs generated candidate answers and served as the judges under study (\cref{sec:setup}, \cref{app:setup}). One pilot (\cref{app:mathconstraint}) used a Claude model to generate candidate solutions, each verified by exact checks, and Qwen2.5-14B read unboxed replies for one labeling. We used generative AI tools to help write analysis code, draft and edit text, produce figures, search and verify related work, audit the preregistration ledger, and label a sample of dataset questions for the prevalence audit; both labeling passes came from one model family, were adjudicated against each other, and were not checked by human annotators. Every number in the paper is produced by committed analysis scripts from stored model outputs, and we checked every claim against those outputs. We take responsibility for the final content of this work.

\subsection*{Ethics statement}
This work studies a failure of automated evaluation that can make models look better or worse than they are. We release no new model and no attack on a deployed system. A model optimized against a reference-guided judge on multi-answer tasks may be rewarded for matching the reference rather than for being correct, which is why we pair the failure with measurements of when it matters. No human subjects were involved.

\subsection*{Reproducibility statement}
\Cref{app:setup} specifies every pool, judge, prompt, and scorer, and \cref{app:ledger} lists each prediction with its registration type and outcome. The anonymized supplementary material contains task generators and checkers, candidate pools, the answer labelings, all judge score files, the analysis scripts that produce every number, table, and figure, and the registration documents with commit hashes replaced by stable tokens.
