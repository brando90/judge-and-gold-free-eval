\title{The Reference Is Not an Answer Key:\\LLM Judges Grade Agreement with One Correct Answer}

\author{Anonymous authors\\Paper under double-blind review}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
LLM judges are routinely shown a reference answer, on the premise that checking a candidate against a known-correct answer is more reliable than judging it alone.
That premise treats the reference as an answer key.
We propose a different reading for questions with several correct answers: the reference is one sample from the set of correct answers, and the judge grades agreement with that sample.
The reading makes three predictions, which we test on tasks whose correct answers are fixed by a program or an audited list.
First, swapping the reference between two correct answers should change the score of a candidate whose text never changes; it moves by 2.7 to 5.4 points out of 10 for Qwen2.5-7B, phi-4, and Claude Sonnet~5.
Second, the damage should begin exactly when a second correct answer exists; the reference helps on single-answer questions, but on multi-answer questions it makes correct answers that differ from it harder to tell from wrong ones for each of the seven judges we measured, including Sonnet~5, whose AUROC on factual questions falls from 0.94 to 0.78.
Third, the effect should survive what would cure ignorance; it persists for answers the judge had already accepted without a reference, at frontier scale, and, on factual questions, when the judge thinks before scoring.
Best-of-$n$ selection is more robust than grading, and scoring blind before revealing the reference removes the selection inflation we preregistered.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Ask for any divisor of 91 strictly between 1 and 91.
Both 7 and 13 are correct.
Give an LLM judge the question, a candidate that answers 13, and the reference answer 7, and it can do one of two things that look alike: check whether the candidate is right, or check whether it says 7.

Reference-guided judging assumes the first.
MT-Bench's math and reasoning template tells the judge to begin ``by comparing the assistant's answer with the reference answer''~\citep{zheng2023judging}; references raise agreement with human labels~\citep{refguided2024}; judges without one are too generous~\citep{generous2026}; and comparison against a reference is the most reliable single-pass strategy for grading math~\citep{chen2026rethinking}.
The same judges grade benchmark answers, choose among sampled answers, and score rollouts for training, so what they reward is what gets optimized~\citep{gao2022scaling}.
The assumption is safe when a question has one correct answer.
Many do not: in an LLM-labeled audit of 200 questions per dataset, 18.5\% of NQ-open questions admitted more than one correct answer, against 2.5\% of GSM8K problems (\cref{app:prevalence}), and tasks that ask for an example or a construction admit many by design.

\paragraph{An alternative explanation.}
We propose that on a question with several correct answers, the reference is not an answer key but one sample from the set of correct answers, and that judges grade agreement with that sample.
If this is right, three things must hold, and each would fail if the reference acted as an answer key:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=2pt]
\item[\textbf{P1}] \textbf{The score follows the reference.} Swapping which correct answer is the reference must move the score of unchanged text (\cref{sec:p1}).
\item[\textbf{P2}] \textbf{The damage starts at two correct answers.} With one correct answer, agreement and correctness coincide and the reference should help; once a second exists, correct answers that differ from the reference should be graded worse (\cref{sec:p2}).
\item[\textbf{P3}] \textbf{It survives what would cure ignorance.} If the judge merely did not know the other answer was correct, answers it had already accepted, a stronger judge, or time to reason should remove the effect (\cref{sec:p3}).
\end{itemize}
We test the three predictions on constraint problems whose correct answers a program enumerates, on factual questions from \textsc{MACE}~\citep{mace2026} whose answer lists we audited, and on unique-answer MATH-500, with nine judges from four model families. \Cref{tab:summary} lists each test and its result. \Cref{sec:cost} then asks what the effect costs a best-of-$n$ pipeline, where it stops, and what removes it.

Prior work comes close without isolating this failure.
\citet{fixation2025} name ``solution fixation'' but measure it with flawed solutions rewritten in the reference's style; \citet{judgingref2026} and \citet{krumdick2025nofreelabels} replace the reference with a wrong one; and human raters shown different reference translations favor candidates close to the one they see~\citep{fomicheva2016reference}. No study of LLM judges that we know of varies which correct answer is the reference.
We mark results as preregistered on OSF\osf, pre-specified in a timestamped commit before the analysis was run (for new model calls, before the data existed)\pre, or post hoc\ph; \cref{app:ledger} scores all 156 registered predictions, including the 25 that were not confirmed or were falsified.

\section{Two readings of a reference, and what each predicts}
\label{sec:model}

Let $b(c)$ be a judge's blind score for candidate $c$ and $s(c; a^\star)$ its score when the reference $a^\star$ is shown.
Under the answer-key reading, $s$ depends on $c$ only through whether $c$ is correct, so any two correct references give the same score.
Under the one-sample reading, agreement with $a^\star$ enters the score:
\begin{equation}
s(c; a^\star) \;\approx\; \beta\, b(c) \;+\; \lambda\, \mathbb{I}[\,\mathrm{ans}(c) = a^\star\,], \qquad \lambda > 0,\; \beta \le 1 .
\label{eq:model}
\end{equation}
The match bonus $\lambda$ is harmless when a question has one correct answer, because matching the reference and being correct are the same event, and the reference then pulls correct answers up (P2, $k{=}1$).
With several correct answers the bonus goes only to the correct answer that happens to be the reference: swapping $a^\star$ moves the score of fixed text by about $\lambda$ (P1), correct answers that differ from $a^\star$ lose ground to wrong answers, all the more when $\beta<1$ dilutes the judge's own assessment (P2), and nothing in \cref{eq:model} depends on whether the judge already knew $c$ was correct (P3).
Fit to blind and referenced scores on constructed sub-pools, the model gives $\lambda = 4.3$ and $3.5$ points and $\beta = 0.51$ and $0.69$ for Qwen2.5-7B and phi-4, and reproduces the pattern of inflation across nine pool compositions out of sample (\cref{app:composition}).\pre{} We use it to organize the predictions, not as a claim about how judges work inside.

\section{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Tasks.}
Our own tasks come from five constraint families (remainder systems, subset sums, Goldbach pairs, divisor counts, digit sums); each stores its full set of correct answers and one member, drawn at random, as the reference.
The \emph{replication pool}\osf{} has 120 tasks; the \emph{$k$-pool}\osf{} has 75 tasks with exactly $k \in \{1,2,4,8,16\}$ correct answers; a \emph{synthetic grid}\pre{} has 60 tasks over the same $k$.
\textsc{MACE} supplies factual questions with 1, 2, 4, or 6 correct answers; its River and Language domains passed our completeness audit (133 tasks in the registered pass-rate band). MATH-500~\citep{hendrycks2021math} and MBPP supply unique answers and programs (\cref{app:setup}).

\paragraph{Candidates and judges.}
Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct writes 64 samples per task at temperature 1 (Qwen2.5-7B for MATH-500 and MBPP).
Each sample is judged \emph{blind}, from the question and the candidate, and \emph{referenced}, with the reference under the heading ``Reference answer (trusted)''.
Open judges (Qwen2.5 1.5B--14B, phi-4, gemma-2-9b, Llama-3.1-8B, Mistral-7B, Qwen3-8B and 14B) answer with an integer from 0 to 10 in at most four tokens, and we take the expected value over the score tokens; Claude Sonnet~5 and GPT-4.1 run through their APIs, and Sonnet~5 also through its command-line interface (CLI) for experiments on reasoning.

\paragraph{Reading answers.}
The prompt asks for the final answer inside \texttt{\textbackslash boxed\{\}}, and our registered checkers read it from there, so a correct answer written in prose usually counted as wrong. Because 38\% of replies ignore the format, we also label every reply by the final answer it states in any format, and check that labeling against Qwen2.5-14B reading each unboxed reply; the two agree on 88\% of unboxed replies (\cref{app:grid}).\ph

\paragraph{Measurements.}
The \emph{swap change} is the change in score of identical text when the reference moves. The \emph{anchoring margin} is the mean score of correct answers that match the reference minus those that do not.
For grading we report the per-task AUROC for separating correct answers that differ from the reference from wrong answers, and the \emph{false-rejection rate}, the share of such correct answers scored 3 or below.
For selection, the preregistered endpoint is best-of-64 \emph{inflation}: the judge picks with one rubric, a second rubric scores the pick relative to samples of the same correctness, and a permutation null is subtracted, so inflation measures how far the pick is over-scored beyond its correctness (\cref{app:setup}). We also report the accuracy of the pick.
Contrasts are referenced minus blind, per task, with task-level bootstrap intervals; we do not correct for multiplicity, and main-text claims rest on intervals far from zero or on effects that repeat across judges and pools.

\section{Prediction 1: the score follows the reference}
\label{sec:p1}

\paragraph{Prediction.} If the reference were an answer key, a candidate's score could not depend on which of two correct answers sits in the reference slot.

\paragraph{Result.}
We re-judged the replication pool's byte-identical samples with the reference moved to another correct answer.
Text the reference moves onto gains 5.25 points under Qwen2.5-7B, 4.09 under phi-4, and 3.04 under Sonnet~5; text it moves off loses 5.41, 3.92, and 2.66; and correct text that matches neither reference barely moves (\cref{fig:concept}c).\pre{}
All twelve registered cells, two judges by two rubrics by three readouts, went as predicted. On an exploratory pool the swap moves scores by up to 9.5 points for the smallest judges and less for larger ones (\cref{app:rotation}).

\paragraph{What ``agreement'' means to the judge.}
Pre-specified rewrites of the reference show that judges compare against a stated answer, not against characters or a derivation (\cref{tab:masking}).\pre{}
Writing 31 as ``thirty-one'' keeps the margin almost unchanged for phi-4 ($+3.68$ against $+3.79$) and keeps two thirds of it for Qwen2.5-7B, though the reference then shares no character with a numeric answer.
Writing it as ``30+1'' cuts the margin by 94\% and 77\%, and showing a worked solution with its final answer masked removes it.
Sonnet~5 behaves the same way (\cref{app:frontier}). Closeness to the reference hardly matters: among correct answers that differ from it, the near, middle, and far thirds score alike, falsifying the gradient we registered (\cref{app:composition}).\pre

\section{Prediction 2: the damage starts at two correct answers}
\label{sec:p2}

\paragraph{Prediction.} With one correct answer, agreement is correctness and the reference should help. With two or more, the match bonus should go to one correct answer and not the others.

\paragraph{The switch.}
On tasks built with exactly $k$ correct answers, the inflation the reference adds rises from $k{=}1$ to $k{>}1$ for all six judges on the synthetic grid and for both judges on the preregistered $k$-pool (\cref{fig:boundary}).\osf\pre{}
The same switch appears on questions we did not construct: on \textsc{MACE}, the reference lowers inflation on single-answer questions ($-0.97$ and $-0.57$~\sd) and raises it on multi-answer questions ($+1.33$ and $+0.55$).\pre{}
On MATH-500, whose answers are unique, the reference raises best-of-64 accuracy by 28.6 to 30.5 points, even when it shows only the final answer.\pre

\paragraph{Correct answers that differ from the reference.}
A judge that grades agreement should separate these answers from wrong ones less well once it sees the reference.
For Qwen2.5-7B on the replication pool, the per-task AUROC falls from 0.87 to 0.60,\pre{} and the fall holds whether answers are read from the box, in any format, or by a model, for four open judges on two pools ($-0.17$ to $-0.28$ in any format; \cref{fig:cost}a).\ph{}
It is largest where it matters most in practice. On \textsc{MACE}'s multi-answer factual questions, Sonnet~5 separates correct answers that differ from the reference from wrong ones with AUROC 0.94 blind and 0.78 with the reference, and the share of them it scores 3 or below rises by 35.6 points \ci{+24.8}{+48.0}; its grading also worsens on both constructed pools (\cref{tab:sonnet-r8}).\pre{}
The reference does not make grading worse overall on natural data: on \textsc{MACE}, Qwen2.5-7B's AUROC over all answers rises by 0.07 as registered, all of it from single-answer questions.\pre

\section{Prediction 3: it survives what should cure ignorance}
\label{sec:p3}

\paragraph{Prediction.} If the judge simply did not know that the other answer was correct, three things should remove the effect: restricting to answers it had already accepted, a stronger judge, and time to reason. The one-sample reading predicts that none of them does.

\paragraph{Answers the judge already accepted.}
Keeping only samples each judge scored 7 or above while blind leaves the swap gain almost unchanged: $+5.27$ for Qwen2.5-7B, $+4.08$ for phi-4, and $+2.79$ for Sonnet~5 (\cref{tab:main-numbers}).\pre{} The judge re-scores answers it has already certified the moment the reference disagrees.

\paragraph{Frontier judges.}
Sonnet~5 anchors and grades worse with the reference (\cref{sec:p1,sec:p2}). GPT-4.1's scores tie too heavily to measure selection (15.5 of 64 candidates tie at its maximum), but its margin is $+1.19$ and its best-of-64 picks shift toward the reference's answer, 32 referenced-only against 1 blind-only (\cref{app:frontier}).\pre

\paragraph{Reasoning.}
Judges that reason before scoring barely anchor on arithmetic: thinking shrinks the swap loss from $-0.69$ to $+0.01$ for Qwen3-8B and from $-1.98$ to $-0.12$ for Qwen3-14B, and asking Sonnet~5 to reason shrinks it from $-2.85$ to $-0.20$ (\cref{fig:reasoning}a).\pre{}
A correct answer at 10 could still fall, so these judges check arithmetic and resist the pull; writing first is not enough by itself, since under the MT-Bench template Qwen2.5-7B still loses 3.59 points.\pre{}
On factual questions reasoning does not cure it. Thinking leaves 44\% to 76\% of the added margin (\cref{fig:reasoning}b), and false rejections rise for all five judges we ran on factual questions, with thinking on or off (\cref{tab:false-reject}), although Sonnet~5 ranks correct answers above wrong ones 94\% of the time without a reference.\pre

\paragraph{Instructions.}
Telling the judge that other correct answers deserve full credit closes phi-4's margin on the synthetic grid at $k{=}16$ ($+1.91 \to +0.03$), does not help smaller Qwen2.5 judges, and shrinks Sonnet~5's from $+3.00$ to $+2.08$ without removing it (\cref{app:mitigation,tab:frontier-api}).\pre

\section{What it costs, where it stops, and what removes it}
\label{sec:cost}

\paragraph{Selection.}
On the preregistered endpoint, the reference adds $+1.19$~\sd{} \ci{+1.03}{+1.35} of best-of-64 inflation on the replication pool; it is $+1.14$ over all 120 tasks and $+0.90$ on the third of tasks with the best answer formatting.\osf\ph{}
Accuracy tells a narrower story (\cref{fig:cost}b).
Under the registered checker the reference lowers accuracy at $k{>}1$ by 15 to 36 points for four open judges,\pre{} but the referenced judge favors unboxed replies that state the reference's value, which that checker counts as wrong.
Crediting answers in any format, the loss is 30.3 points \ci{-48.5}{-12.1} for Qwen2.5-7B and at most 15 points with intervals reaching zero for the others, and when a model reads the answers no loss excludes zero.\ph{}
Sonnet~5's best-of-16 accuracy does not change on any pool, and on \textsc{MACE} our registered prediction of a loss failed.\pre{} Grading, not selection, is where the reference does most of its damage.

\paragraph{Where it stops.}
We registered the prediction that a canonical program shown as the reference on MBPP would inflate like an answer key. It was falsified: the program lowered inflation relative to blind, as a test suite does ($-0.34$ and $-0.57$~\sd; \cref{tab:code}).\pre{}
A follow-up asked the same questions in four answer formats with fixed content; the reference's added inflation fell from integer to pair, JSON, and program ($+1.66 \to +0.13$ for Qwen2.5-7B, $+1.35 \to -0.37$ for phi-4), consistent with \cref{sec:p1}: the effect needs an answer the judge can read off and compare (\cref{app:code}).\pre

\paragraph{What removes it.}
Our preregistered mitigation scores the candidate blind, then shows the reference together with that score.
At $k{>}1$ it brings inflation to $-0.05$ and $+0.16$~\sd, against $+1.50$ and $+1.07$ with the reference alone.\osf{}
The displayed number does the work: removing it from the second call restores the inflation ($+1.42$ and $+0.36$), while wording it as someone else's score changes little (\cref{tab:m1variants}).\pre{}
Protection varies by judge (gemma-2-9b keeps $+1.23$), and on \textsc{MACE} the protocol did not beat the reference, contrary to our registered prediction.\osf\pre{}
With answers read in any format, both blind-then-reveal and the simpler rule of taking the higher of the blind and referenced scores stay within 6 points of blind accuracy at $k{>}1$; each needs two judge calls (\cref{tab:labelings-numbers}).\ph

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Reference-guided judging.}
References are the standard remedy for grading math and reasoning with LLM judges~\citep{zheng2023judging,refguided2024,chen2026rethinking}, and judges without them over-credit wrong answers~\citep{generous2026}.
Judges depend on the reference being correct~\citep{judgingref2026,krumdick2025nofreelabels}, diverge from humans and are sensitive to prompt wording~\citep{thakur2024judging}, and favor the reference's solution method~\citep{fixation2025}.
Generating a reference before judging has been proposed as an improvement~\citep{dobefore2025}; our results predict that a self-generated reference anchors the same way on multi-answer tasks.

\paragraph{Single references and many correct answers.}
Reference-based metrics in translation reward outputs that resemble the particular reference~\citep{freitag2020bleu,fomicheva2016reference}, and code evaluation moved from similarity to execution~\citep{chen2021codex}.
Open-domain question answering under-credits correct answers that are missing from the gold list or phrased differently~\citep{min2020ambigqa,si2021whatsinaname,bulian2022tomayto,kamalloo2023evaluating}; in our setting the list is complete and the judge still favors the one answer it is shown.
Verifiers in reinforcement learning miss correct answers written in a different form~\citep{xu2025tinyv,huang2025pitfalls,chen2025xverify}, a failure our own boxed-answer checker showed, and \textsc{RewardBench-2} and \textsc{MACE} build benchmarks around questions with several answers~\citep{malik2025rewardbench2,mace2026}.

\paragraph{Judge biases and optimization.}
LLM judges favor the first response~\citep{wang2024fair}, longer responses~\citep{dubois2024lengthcontrolled}, their own outputs~\citep{panickssery2024llm}, and scores shown to them~\citep{kapetanovic2026anchoring}. Optimizing against an imperfect evaluator inflates its scores~\citep{gao2022scaling,rafailov2024scaling}, and good average agreement does not guarantee good best-of-$n$ choices~\citep{landesberg2026bestofn}. A judge that commits to its own answer before seeing the candidate resists reward hacking~\citep{zhou2026convincing}; blind-then-reveal applies the same idea to references.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Tasks and labels.} Our tasks are constraint problems, two audited factual domains, MATH-500, and MBPP, mostly with one generator. The any-format and model-read labelings agree on 88\% of unboxed replies and were not checked against human labels; \textsc{MACE}'s answer lists miss 2.3\% to 8.1\% of correct answers.
\item \textbf{Judges.} Most open judges have at most 14B parameters and score in four tokens without reasoning, where the arithmetic effect is largest. Sonnet~5's grading and selection with wrong answers come from 16-candidate subsamples; GPT-4.1's scores tie too heavily for selection; we tested no reasoning judge from a third frontier provider.
\item \textbf{Downstream.} Three DPO runs against the referenced judge did not show the policy copying the reference, and the protected arm failed its manipulation check, so we have not shown that training exploits the bias (\cref{app:dpo}).
\item \textbf{Registration.} Accuracy and false-rejection readouts on the OSF pools are post hoc, most registrations rely on timestamped commits, and we adopted the uniform verdict rule after most outcomes were known (\cref{app:ledger}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

A reference answer is a good check when a question has one right answer.
When it has several, judges treat the one they are shown as the only one: swapping it moves the score of unchanged text, correct answers that differ from it are graded as failing, and neither prior certification, frontier scale, nor thinking on factual questions stops this.
We do not claim that references are harmful in general; on unique answers they help, and best-of-$n$ selection is more robust than grading.
When correct answers can differ from the reference, check them against the task's answer set where one exists, and otherwise score blind first and reveal the reference only afterwards.

\subsection*{AI use statement}
Generative AI models were the experimental subjects: open and frontier LLMs generated candidate answers and served as the judges under study (\cref{sec:setup}, \cref{app:setup}). One pilot (\cref{app:mathconstraint}) used a Claude model to generate candidate solutions, each verified by exact checks, and Qwen2.5-14B read unboxed replies for one labeling. We used generative AI tools to help write analysis code, draft and edit text, produce figures, search and verify related work, audit the preregistration ledger, and label a sample of dataset questions for the prevalence audit; both labeling passes came from one model family, were adjudicated against each other, and were not checked by human annotators. Every number in the paper is produced by committed analysis scripts from stored model outputs, and we checked every claim against those outputs. We take responsibility for the final content of this work.

\subsection*{Ethics statement}
This work studies a failure of automated evaluation that can make models look better or worse than they are. We release no new model and no attack on a deployed system. A model optimized against a reference-guided judge on multi-answer tasks may be rewarded for matching the reference rather than for being correct, which is why we pair the failure with measurements of when it matters and a protocol that removes it. No human subjects were involved.
