\title{Reference Answers Bias LLM Judges\\Against Other Correct Answers}

\author{Anonymous authors\\Paper under double-blind review}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
LLM judges are often given a reference answer, because checking a candidate against a known-correct answer is easier than judging it from scratch.
When a question has several correct answers, the reference is only one of them, and judges grade agreement with it.
Moving the reference to a different correct answer while keeping a candidate's text fixed changes its score by 2.7 to 5.4 points out of 10 for Qwen2.5-7B, phi-4, and Claude Sonnet~5.
With the reference shown, judges separate correct answers that differ from it from wrong answers less well, both on our constructed arithmetic tasks (per-task AUROC 0.87 to 0.60 for Qwen2.5-7B) and on the natural \textsc{MACE} benchmark (0.74 to 0.66).
On the constructed tasks overall grading gets worse too, and best-of-64 accuracy on tasks with several correct answers falls by 8.5 to 35.9 points across five open judges; on \textsc{MACE}, the reference improves overall grading only on single-answer questions.
The effect needs a final value stated explicitly in the reference.
In two Qwen3 models, extended thinking removes it on arithmetic tasks, where their scores sit near the ceiling, and roughly halves it on factual questions.
Scoring blind first and revealing the reference afterwards limits the worst-case selection loss for two of three judges.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Ask for any divisor of 91 strictly between 1 and 91.
Both 7 and 13 are correct.
Give an LLM judge the question, a candidate answer, and the reference answer 7, and it can do one of two things that look alike: check whether the candidate is right, or check whether it says 7.

We gave Claude Sonnet~5, with extended thinking off, correct answers to 93 questions that each have several correct answers.
Without a reference, it scored correct answers that happened to match the reference and correct answers that did not about the same, 9.19 and 9.09 out of 10.
With the reference in the prompt, matching answers rose to 9.99 and non-matching ones fell to 6.93 (\cref{fig:teaser}a), and the share of correct non-matching answers scored 3 or below rose from 4.9\% to 26.2\%.

Reference-guided judging is standard practice.
MT-Bench's math and reasoning template tells the judge to begin ``by comparing the assistant's answer with the reference answer''~\citep{zheng2023judging}, references raise agreement with human labels~\citep{refguided2024}, judges without one are too generous~\citep{generous2026}, and a recent study of math grading found comparison against a reference to be the most reliable single-pass strategy~\citep{chen2026rethinking}.
The same judges grade benchmark answers, choose among sampled answers, and score rollouts for training, so what they reward is what gets optimized~\citep{gao2022scaling}.
Comparing against a reference is safe only when the reference is the only correct answer.
Curated math benchmarks mostly satisfy that, and open-domain questions often do not: in an LLM-labeled audit of 200 questions per dataset, 18.5\% of NQ-open questions admitted more than one correct answer (30.0\% counting answers that changed over time), compared with 2.5\% of GSM8K and DeepScaleR problems (\cref{app:prevalence}). Tasks that ask for an example, a construction, or a program admit many correct answers by design.

Prior work comes close to this failure without isolating it.
\citet{fixation2025} name it ``solution fixation'' but measure it by rewriting flawed solutions in the reference's style.
\citet{judgingref2026} and \citet{krumdick2025nofreelabels} replace the gold reference with an incorrect one, and work on verifier false negatives in reinforcement learning studies different surface forms of the same answer~\citep{xu2025tinyv,huang2025pitfalls}.
None of it varies which correct answer is the reference or measures what that does to grading and selection.
We do this on tasks where correctness is decided by a program or an audited answer list, so we know every correct answer and, on our own tasks, can set how many there are:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{The score follows the reference} (\cref{sec:follows}). Moving the reference to a different correct answer, with the candidate's text unchanged, moves its score by 2.7 to 5.4 points, while correct answers that match neither reference barely move, including answers the judge had already accepted without a reference.
\item \textbf{The judge compares final values} (\cref{sec:mechanism}). The effect survives writing the reference in words and mostly disappears when the reference is an expression to evaluate or a worked solution with its final answer masked.
\item \textbf{Correct answers that differ from the reference are graded worse on every pool we tested} (\cref{sec:cost}). Overall grading and selection get worse on constructed arithmetic tasks; on \textsc{MACE}'s multi-answer questions, overall grading does not change.
\item \textbf{Thinking reduces the effect, and a two-step protocol hedges} (\cref{sec:fix}). Extended thinking removes it on arithmetic, where scores sit near the ceiling, and halves it on factual questions; scoring blind before revealing the reference bounds the worst-case loss for two of three judges.
\end{itemize}

We mark results as preregistered on OSF\osf, pre-specified in a timestamped commit before the data existed\pre, pre-specified re-analyses of an effect whose direction was already known\reana, or post hoc\ph. \Cref{app:ledger} scores all 147 registered predictions: 80 confirmed, 25 not confirmed or falsified, 16 split across judges, and 26 not evaluable, not scored, or excluded.

\section{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Tasks.}
Our own tasks come from five constraint families (remainder systems, subset sums, Goldbach pairs, divisor counts, digit sums), for example ``name two primes that sum to 100.''
Each task stores its full set of correct answers and one member, drawn at random when the task is built, that serves as the reference.
The two main pools are a 120-task \emph{replication pool}\osf{} and a 75-task \emph{$k$-pool}\osf{} with exactly $k \in \{1,2,4,8,16\}$ correct answers per task, 15 tasks each.
We also use the River and Language domains of \textsc{MACE}~\citep{mace2026}, a benchmark of factual questions with 1, 2, 4, or 6 correct answers, whose answer lists passed our completeness audit (133 tasks), MBPP~\citep{austin2021mbpp} for code, and MATH-500~\citep{hendrycks2021math} for unique answers (\cref{app:setup}).

\paragraph{Candidates, judges, and scores.}
Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct writes 64 samples per task at temperature 1 (Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct for MATH-500 and MBPP).
Each sample is judged \emph{blind}, from the question and the candidate, and \emph{referenced}, with the reference added under the heading ``Reference answer (trusted)''.
The main judges are Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct and phi-4; we also test the Qwen2.5 family from 1.5B to 14B, Llama-3.1-8B, gemma-2-9b, Mistral-7B, Qwen3-8B and Qwen3-14B, Claude Sonnet~5, and GPT-4.1.
The judge answers with an integer from 0 to 10; rubric~$a$, used in every figure, asks it to rate correctness ``using the reference to check it'', and a second wording, rubric~$b$, tells it to compare the candidate against the reference (\cref{app:setup}).
For open judges the score is the expected value over the score tokens at the first generated position. Our original scorer approximated the probability of ``10'' for tokenizers that write it as two tokens; a pre-specified rescoring computes it exactly and changes no conclusion (\cref{app:estimator}). We name the scorer wherever it matters.

\paragraph{Measurements.}
The \emph{rotation change} is the change in the score of identical text when the reference moves.
The \emph{anchoring margin} is the mean score of correct answers that match the reference minus that of correct answers that do not; the \emph{added margin} is the referenced margin minus the blind margin.
For grading we report the \emph{false-rejection rate}, the share of correct answers that differ from the reference scored 3 or below, and the per-task AUROC of the score for separating correct from wrong answers.
For selection we report the accuracy of the best-of-64 pick under rubric~$a$, averaging over ties, on tasks whose pass rate lies between 0.2 and 0.8 (between 0.05 and 0.95 on \textsc{MACE}), so that selection can change correctness.
The preregistered endpoint of the OSF studies is best-of-64 \emph{inflation} (\cref{app:setup}); we mention it only where it was registered.
All contrasts are referenced minus blind, per task, with task-level bootstrap intervals.

\section{Moving the reference moves the score}
\label{sec:follows}

If the reference were an answer key, a candidate's score could not depend on which of two correct answers sits in the reference slot.
We kept the replication pool's samples byte-identical and re-judged them with the reference moved to another correct answer, the one that appears most often among each task's correct samples.

Text that the reference moves onto gains 4.97 points under Qwen2.5-7B and 4.09 under phi-4, text it moves off loses 5.04 and 3.92, and correct text that matches neither moves by $+0.13$ and $+0.30$; all twelve registered cells hold (two judges, two rubrics, three predictions; \cref{app:rotation}).\pre{}
The exact scorer makes Qwen2.5-7B's changes slightly larger, $+5.25$ and $-5.41$, and its greedy integer scores larger still, $+5.65$ and $-5.78$ (\cref{tab:r4-rotation}).\reana{}
Sonnet~5 shows the same pattern through the API ($+3.04$ and $-2.66$) and through a second interface on 60 of these tasks ($+3.57$ and $-2.85$).\pre

Two readings would make this reasonable behavior.
In the first, the judge did not know the other answer was correct and the reference taught it; then answers the judge had already scored at 7 or above while blind should not move. They move just as much: $+4.99$ for Qwen2.5-7B (91\% of such answers kept), $+4.08$ for phi-4, and $+2.79$ for Sonnet~5.\pre{} Keeping only correct answers scored at least 9 blind leaves Qwen2.5-7B's gain at $+5.32$ and its loss at $-5.47$ (\cref{tab:main-numbers}).\ph{}
In the second, the heading ``reference answer (trusted)'' implies that the question has one answer. Telling the judge that other correct answers deserve full credit shrinks Sonnet~5's margin on the $k$-pool by 0.93 points \ci{-1.23}{-0.64} from $+3.00$ and GPT-4.1's by 0.53 \ci{-0.80}{-0.31} from $+0.99$, but removes it for neither, and does not help Qwen2.5 judges below 14B (\cref{sec:fix}).\pre{}
The effect is smaller for stronger judges (a rotation gain of 9.48 for Qwen2.5-1.5B and 3.07 for Qwen2.5-14B on an exploratory pool; \cref{tab:ladder}) but present in every judge we tested.

\section{What the judge is checking}
\label{sec:mechanism}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{figures/fig3_mechanism.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{The effect needs a final value stated in the reference.} Anchoring margin when the reference is written as a numeral, in words, or as an expression (replication pool, 93 tasks), and when a worked solution is shown with its final answer visible or masked (21 $k$-pool tasks with a single-number answer). Rubric~$a$, 95\% intervals (\cref{tab:masking}).\pre}
\label{fig:mechanism}
\end{figure}

A cheap way to use a reference is to compare the candidate's final answer with the reference's.
Three pre-specified rewrites of the reference test whether judges do this (\cref{fig:mechanism}).
Writing 31 as ``thirty-one'' keeps most of the margin, although the reference then shares no characters with a numeric answer.
Writing it as ``30+1'' cuts the margin by 94\% for Qwen2.5-7B and 77\% for phi-4, and masking the final answer in a worked solution removes it, although the same solution anchors strongly when its answer is visible.\pre{}
Judges behave as if they compare the candidate's final answer with a final value stated explicitly in the reference: the comparison survives a change of surface form, but not a reference that must first be evaluated or that states no final value.

Closeness to the reference matters little.
Our registered slope test found scores of non-matching correct answers falling with numeric distance from the reference for both judges, but a few tasks drive the average slope, and the near, middle, and far thirds score alike (\cref{app:composition}).\pre{}
Answers that are programs rather than values anchor less, but programs also have fewer distinct correct outputs, and the registered prediction held for phi-4 only (\cref{app:code}).\pre

\section{What the reference costs}
\label{sec:cost}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig3_cost.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{The reference damages grading of correct answers that differ from it everywhere; its overall cost depends on the pool.}
\textbf{(a)}~Per-task AUROC of Qwen2.5-7B's score (exact scorer) for separating correct from wrong answers, blind (orange) and with the reference (blue), for correct answers that differ from the reference (non-matching) and for all correct answers; \textsc{MACE} is split by the number of correct answers.\textsuperscript{\textsc{pre/ph}}
\textbf{(b)}~Within \textsc{MACE}, each task's blind AUROC, estimated on half of its samples, against the reference's change in overall AUROC on the other half (133 tasks).\ph{}
\textbf{(c)}~Change in the accuracy of the best-of-64 pick when the reference is shown, 95\% intervals.\textsuperscript{\textsc{re/ph}}}
\label{fig:cost}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Correct answers that differ from the reference.}
A judge that grades by agreement should separate correct answers that differ from the reference from wrong answers less well once it sees the reference.
It does, on both pools we tested. For Qwen2.5-7B the per-task AUROC for these two groups falls from 0.87 blind to 0.60 with the reference on the replication pool, and from 0.74 to 0.66 on \textsc{MACE} (\cref{fig:cost}a).\pre{} phi-4 shows the same pattern, from 0.90 to 0.65 and from 0.79 to 0.73.\ph{}
The share of these correct answers scored 3 or below rises from 0.4\% to 28.1\% for Qwen2.5-7B\pre{} and from 4.9\% to 26.2\% for Sonnet~5.\ph

\paragraph{Grading overall.}
Overall grading gets worse on the constructed pool and does not change on \textsc{MACE}'s multi-answer questions.
On the replication pool, overall AUROC falls by 0.22 for Qwen2.5-7B\pre{} and by 0.19 for phi-4.\ph{}
On \textsc{MACE} the reference raises it by 0.29 and 0.23 on single-answer questions, where every correct answer matches it, and changes it by $-0.00$ \ci{-0.05}{+0.05} and $+0.02$ \ci{-0.02}{+0.06} on questions with several correct answers (\cref{tab:main-numbers}).\ph{}
Blind verification may moderate this. Qwen2.5-7B's blind AUROC is 0.87 on both constructed pools and 0.73 on \textsc{MACE}, as we predicted,\pre{} and within \textsc{MACE}, tasks the judge verifies well without a reference gain less from it: with blind AUROC and the reference's effect estimated on separate halves of each task's samples, Spearman $\rho$ is $-0.54$ for Qwen2.5-7B and $-0.38$ for phi-4, and $-0.50$ and $-0.36$ on multi-answer questions alone (\cref{fig:cost}b). Within the constructed pools, where blind AUROC is uniformly high, there is no such relationship (\cref{tab:main-numbers}).\ph{} Tasks with low blind AUROC also have more room to improve, so we treat blind verification as a candidate moderator, not a rule.

\paragraph{Selection.}
Best-of-64 selection follows the grading results (\cref{fig:cost}c).
On the $k$-pool, pooled over $k{>}1$, the reference lowers accuracy by 35.9 points for Qwen2.5-7B, 23.1 for Qwen2.5-14B, 20.5 for phi-4, and 15.4 for gemma-2-9b, all with intervals excluding zero, and by 8.5 for Mistral-7B, whose scores are parsed integers and a third of whose blind replies contain no score.\reana{}
Part of this loss is not specific to multiple answers: Qwen2.5-7B also loses 21.4 points at $k{=}1$, and the step from $k{=}1$ to $k{>}1$ excludes zero only for phi-4 and gemma-2-9b (\cref{tab:main-numbers}).\ph{}
On the preregistered replication population the loss is 4.8 points for Qwen2.5-7B, an interval that includes zero, and 11.4 \ci{-19.1}{-4.8} for phi-4.\ph{}
On \textsc{MACE} the reference raises accuracy by 29.4 points on single-answer questions and changes it by $+2.0$ \ci{-5.1}{+10.1} on multi-answer ones.\ph{}
On MATH-500, where correct answers rarely differ from the reference and blind AUROC is 0.69, the reference raises accuracy by 28.6 to 30.6 points for Qwen2.5-7B and 14B, whether it is the full solution or only the final answer.\pre

\section{What to do about it}
\label{sec:fix}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig2_reasoning.pdf}

\label{fig:reasoning}

\paragraph{Verify when you can.}
When a program can check correctness, no judge is needed.
Extended thinking helps without removing the problem.
With the same weights, prompt, and sampling, turning on thinking removes the rotation effect on arithmetic tasks for Qwen3-8B ($-0.69$ to $+0.01$) and Qwen3-14B ($-1.98$ to $-0.12$), but with thinking on Qwen3-8B gives a score of 10 to 99.8\% of correct arithmetic answers when shown a reference and to 96.2\% blind, so the effect has little room to appear (\cref{fig:reasoning}a; \cref{app:rotation}).\pre{}
On factual \textsc{MACE} questions thinking roughly halves the added margin, from $+6.16$ to $+3.62$ for Qwen3-8B and from $+7.07$ to $+3.13$ for Qwen3-14B (\cref{fig:reasoning}b).\pre{}
Thinking does not make the reference safe for selection: shown the reference, Qwen3-8B with thinking still loses 5.8 points of best-of-16 accuracy at $k{=}1$ and 3.7 at $k{>}1$, and our prediction that it would not was falsified (\cref{tab:r2b-k}).\pre{}
Sonnet~5 behaves the same way on arithmetic: on 60 rotation tasks, asking it to reason before scoring shrinks its loss from $-2.85$ to $-0.20$ without extended thinking and to $-0.32$ with it, and its false rejections from 26.7\% to 0.4\%.\pre{} \pending{On factual questions (R5-A), ...}
A short explanation is not enough either: under the verbatim MT-Bench template, whose explanations average 259 tokens, Qwen2.5-7B's score still falls by 3.59 points when the reference moves off a correct answer.\pre

\paragraph{Prompts help only some judges.}
On a synthetic grid of 60 tasks whose correct sets enumerate exactly, adding ``the reference shown is one valid answer among possibly many \ldots\ any correct answer deserves full credit'' removes phi-4's margin at $k{=}16$ ($+1.91$ to $+0.03$) and shrinks Qwen2.5-14B's ($+3.34$ to $+1.21$), but does not help smaller Qwen2.5 judges.\pre{}
For Sonnet~5 and GPT-4.1 two such wordings shrink the margin by 0.40 to 0.93 points, each interval excluding zero, and leave it positive (\cref{app:frontier}).\pre{}
Showing up to three correct references instead of one removes the margin toward the original reference but does not restore blind-level selection (\cref{app:mitigation}).

\paragraph{Blind, then reveal.}
The protocol we preregistered as the main mitigation scores the candidate blind in a first call and, in a second, shows the reference with that blind score and states that a correct answer differing from the reference keeps its score.
In accuracy on the $k$-pool at $k{>}1$ (original scorer), it recovers most of the reference's loss for Qwen2.5-7B (a loss of 10.3 points instead of 30.8) and all of it for Qwen2.5-14B and Mistral-7B, but little for phi-4 and gemma-2-9b, which still lose 15.4 and 12.8 points against blind judging.\ph{}
On MATH-500 it keeps nearly all of the reference's benefit (95.2\% and 93.5\% accuracy, against 98.1\% and 95.2\% with the reference and 67.6\% blind).\pre{} On \textsc{MACE} our registered predictions that it would beat the reference failed for both judges: it was 1.5 to 3.0 points less accurate overall.\pre{}
Removing the blind score from the second call brings back the preregistered inflation, so the displayed score does the work, consistent with judges anchoring on scores shown to them~\citep{kapetanovic2026anchoring}.\pre{}
No protocol is best everywhere, and blind-then-reveal is a hedge (\cref{tab:regret}).
Its largest loss against the best protocol in each pool is 10.2 points for Qwen2.5-7B and 1.7 for Qwen2.5-14B, compared with 30.8 and 20.5 for the plain reference and 30.5 and 27.6 for blind judging; for phi-4, measured on two pools, blind judging has the smaller worst case.\ph{}

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Reference-guided judging.}
References are the standard remedy for grading math and reasoning with LLM judges~\citep{zheng2023judging,refguided2024,chen2026rethinking}, and judges without them over-credit wrong answers~\citep{generous2026}.
Judges depend on the reference being correct: replacing it with a wrong answer collapses their accuracy~\citep{judgingref2026}, and without a correct reference they agree with experts mainly on questions they can answer themselves~\citep{krumdick2025nofreelabels}.
Even strong judges grading answers against references diverge from human judgments and are sensitive to prompt wording~\citep{thakur2024judging}.
\citet{fixation2025} describe a bias toward the reference's solution method, measured with flawed solutions rewritten in the reference's style.
We keep the reference correct and vary which correct answer it is.
Generating a reference before judging has been proposed as an improvement~\citep{dobefore2025}; our results predict that a self-generated reference anchors the same way on multi-answer tasks, which we have not tested.

\paragraph{Single references and many correct answers.}
Reference-based metrics in machine translation reward outputs that resemble the particular reference, which motivated paraphrased references~\citep{freitag2020bleu}; human raters shown a reference translation favor candidates close to it~\citep{fomicheva2016reference}; execution-based evaluation of code replaced similarity to a single reference solution~\citep{chen2021codex}; and single-reference evaluation undercounts correct outputs when questions admit several~\citep{min2020ambigqa}.
Rule-based and model-based verifiers in reinforcement learning miss correct answers written in a different form~\citep{xu2025tinyv,huang2025pitfalls,chen2025xverify}, which concerns surface forms of one answer, and RL data pipelines often keep only problems that ``admit a single correct answer''~\citep{albalak2025bigmath}. \textsc{RewardBench-2} and \textsc{MACE} build benchmarks around questions with several answers~\citep{malik2025rewardbench2,mace2026}.

\paragraph{Judge biases and optimization.}
LLM judges favor the first response~\citep{wang2024fair}, longer responses~\citep{dubois2024lengthcontrolled}, and their own outputs~\citep{panickssery2024llm}, anchor on scores shown to them~\citep{kapetanovic2026anchoring}, and can be fooled by content-free responses~\citep{zhao2025onetoken}.
Optimizing against an imperfect evaluator inflates its scores~\citep{gao2022scaling,rafailov2024scaling}, and good average agreement does not guarantee good best-of-$n$ choices~\citep{landesberg2026bestofn}.
Closest to our protocol, a reference-free judge that commits to its own answer before seeing the candidate resists self-play reward hacking~\citep{zhou2026convincing}; blind-then-reveal applies the same idea to reference-guided judging.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Tasks and generators.} Our results come from constraint problems, two audited factual domains of \textsc{MACE}, MBPP, and MATH-500, and most pools use one generator, Llama-3.1-8B. We do not test free-form generation, long references, contested correctness, or step-level verifiers, and our prevalence labels come from LLM annotators without a human check.
\item \textbf{The overall cost.} Whether the reference hurts overall grading rests on one natural and two constructed pools, which also differ in domain, and the within-\textsc{MACE} relationship with blind verification is post hoc. \textsc{MACE}'s answer lists miss 2.3\% to 8.1\% of correct answers in our audit; an unlisted correct answer counts as wrong, which favors the reference.
\item \textbf{Judges and scorers.} Open judges are 14B parameters or smaller, and the effect shrinks with judge strength. The thinking contrast uses two models of one family. The blind-then-reveal comparisons, the phi-4 and Mistral-7B numbers, and several appendix analyses use the original scorer; Mistral-7B's tokenizer has no single-token digits, so its scores are parsed integers.
\item \textbf{Registration.} Accuracy and false-rejection readouts on the OSF pools are post hoc, and three experiments have third-party timestamps while the rest rely on git commits. The registered blind-then-reveal contrast for Mistral-7B used one bootstrap seed; across 20 seeds its interval excludes zero in 11 (\cref{app:ledger}).
\item \textbf{Wording and training.} Our reference heading may suggest a unique answer, which an explicit instruction only partly counters. A DPO policy trained against a referenced judge raised that judge's score while held-out accuracy fell, but every control arm failed its manipulation check (\cref{app:dpo}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

A reference answer is a good check when a question has one right answer.
When it has several, the reference is one of them, and judges grade agreement with it: moving the reference moves the score of unchanged text, and correct answers that differ from it are graded worse on every pool we measured.
Whether this makes grading and selection worse overall depends on the pool, and blind verification is a candidate moderator rather than a settled rule.
Extended thinking reduces the effect without removing it, and scoring blind before revealing the reference is a hedge, not a fix.
When a program can check correctness, no judge is needed; when none can, the share of correct answers that differ from the reference and the judge's blind verification can both be measured before deployment.

\subsection*{AI use statement}
In this work, generative AI models were the experimental subjects: open and frontier LLMs generated candidate answers and served as the judges under study (\cref{sec:setup}, \cref{app:setup}). One pilot (\cref{app:mathconstraint}) used a Claude model to generate candidate solutions, each verified by exact programmatic checks. We used generative AI tools to help write analysis code, draft and edit text, produce figures, search and verify related work, audit the preregistration ledger, and label a sample of dataset questions for the prevalence audit, whose labels were double-coded and adjudicated but not checked by human annotators. Every number in the paper is produced by committed analysis scripts from stored model outputs, and we checked every claim against those outputs. We take responsibility for the final content of this work, including text, claims, and artifacts produced with the aid of generative AI.

\subsection*{Ethics statement}
This work studies a failure of automated evaluation that can make optimized models look better or worse than they are. We release no new model and no attack on a deployed system. Anyone optimizing against a reference-guided judge on multi-answer tasks can exploit the failure, which is why we pair it with measurements of when it matters and recommend verifying judges on the tasks where they are used. No human subjects were involved.

\subsection*{Reproducibility statement}
\Cref{app:setup} specifies every pool, judge, prompt, and scorer, and \cref{app:ledger} lists each prediction with its registration type and outcome. The anonymized supplementary material contains task generators and correctness checkers, candidate pools, all judge score files, the analysis scripts that produce every number, table, and figure, and the registration documents with commit hashes replaced by stable tokens.
