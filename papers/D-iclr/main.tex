\title{The Reference Is Not an Answer Key:\\LLM Judges Favor the Correct Answer They Are Shown}

\author{Anonymous authors\\Paper under double-blind review}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
LLM judges are routinely given a reference answer, on the premise that it acts as an answer key.
We show that when a question has several correct answers, a judge's score follows which correct answer it is shown.
On tasks whose correct answers are enumerated by a program or an audited list, swapping the reference between two correct answers moves the score of byte-identical text by 2.7 to 5.4 points out of 10 for Qwen2.5-7B, phi-4, and Claude Sonnet~5.
A reference still helps when there is one correct answer, raising best-of-64 accuracy by about 30 points on MATH-500.
With several, correct answers that differ from the reference become harder to separate from wrong ones: AUROC falls by 0.07 to 0.33 for four open judges on constructed tasks, depending on how answers are read, and for Sonnet~5 by 0.04 to 0.08 on constructed tasks and from 0.94 to 0.78 on factual questions.
Verification failure explains part of this: a thinking mode cuts the effect by 89\% or more on arithmetic, where judges can check answers. It does not explain all of it: Sonnet~5 still penalizes factual answers it ranks above wrong ones 94\% of the time without a reference.
The harm falls on grading more than on selection: on natural multi-answer questions the reference did not lower best-of-64 accuracy.
Of 156 registered predictions, 92 were confirmed, 25 were not confirmed or were falsified, 16 held for some judges but not others, and 23 could not be evaluated or were excluded; all are reported.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Ask for a two-digit number that is divisible by 5 and whose digits sum to 8.
Both 35 and 80 are correct.
Qwen2.5-7B, shown a candidate that reasons its way to 80, scores it 10.0 out of 10 with no reference, 2.9 when the reference answer is 35, and 9.3 when the reference is 80 (\cref{fig:concept}b; a typical swap moves about 5 points).
The text never changed; only the correct answer the judge was shown did.

Reference-guided judging is standard practice.
MT-Bench's math and reasoning template tells the judge to begin ``by comparing the assistant's answer with the reference answer''~\citep{zheng2023judging}, references raise agreement with human labels~\citep{refguided2024}, judges without one are too generous~\citep{generous2026}, and comparison against a reference is the most reliable single-pass strategy for grading math~\citep{chen2026rethinking}.
The same judges grade benchmarks, choose among sampled answers, and score rollouts for training, so what they reward is what gets optimized~\citep{gao2022scaling}.
The practice is safe when a question has one correct answer, as on curated math benchmarks: our LLM-labeled audit of 200 questions per dataset (not checked by humans) found 0\% to 2.5\% with several on MATH-500, GSM8K, and DeepScaleR (\cref{app:prevalence}).
It is not safe where answers are not unique: 18.5\% of NQ-open questions in the same audit, benchmarks built around several valid answers such as \textsc{RewardBench-2} Ties and \textsc{MACE}~\citep{malik2025rewardbench2,mace2026}, and tasks that ask for an example or a construction.

\paragraph{Readings of a reference.}
If the reference is an \emph{answer key}, a correct answer scores the same whichever correct answer is shown.
Under \emph{verification failure}, the judge cannot tell that the other answer is correct, so the reference is its only evidence.
Under \emph{agreement}, the judge favors the correct answer that matches the reference even when it can check the others.
We test three predictions:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=2pt]
\item[\textbf{P1}] \textbf{The score follows the reference.} Swapping which correct answer is the reference moves the score of unchanged text (\cref{sec:p1}). This rules out the answer key.
\item[\textbf{P2}] \textbf{With several correct answers, the others are graded worse.} This is what P1 implies for grading; with one correct answer the reference should help (\cref{sec:p2}).
\item[\textbf{P3}] \textbf{The effect is not only a failure to verify.} It should persist on answers the judge demonstrably verifies without a reference (P3a), although it may shrink as verification improves (P3b; \cref{sec:p3}). Only P3a separates agreement from verification failure.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{What we find.}
P1 holds for every score-only judge we swapped (3 to 5 points) and not for thinking judges on arithmetic.
P2 holds: grading of correct answers that differ from the reference worsens for all four screened open judges and for Sonnet~5.
P3a splits: Sonnet~5 anchors on factual answers it verifies, while thinking judges on arithmetic do not, so both mechanisms operate; the effect also collapses when the reference must be computed, which points to matching a literal answer.
Selection loses accuracy mainly when no correct candidate matches the reference, and on natural multi-answer questions it did not lose accuracy at all.

Prior work comes close without isolating this failure.
\citet{fixation2025} name ``solution fixation'' but measure it with flawed solutions rewritten in the reference's style; \citet{judgingref2026} and \citet{krumdick2025nofreelabels} replace the reference with a wrong one; human raters shown different reference translations favor candidates close to the one they see~\citep{fomicheva2016reference}. Benchmarks with several valid answers score judges against all of them but do not vary which one the judge is shown, and no study of LLM judges that we know of does.

\section{A model of the three readings}
\label{sec:model}

Let $b(c)$ be a judge's blind score for candidate $c$, $s(c; a^\star)$ its score when the correct answer $a^\star$ is shown as the reference, and $v_c \in [0, 1]$ how reliably the judge can check $c$ without a reference. We write
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
s(c; a^\star) \;\approx\;& \beta\, b(c) \;+\; \lambda(v_c)\, \mathbb{I}[\,\mathrm{ans}(c) = a^\star\,] \\
&+\; \gamma\, \mathbb{I}[\,c \text{ is wrong and contains } a^\star\,] \;+\; \varepsilon, \qquad \beta \le 1 .
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:model}
\end{equation}
The answer key sets $\lambda \equiv \gamma = 0$ and $\beta = 1$. Verification failure sets $\lambda(v) = \lambda_0 (1 - v)$, a bonus that vanishes once the judge can check the answer. Agreement keeps $\lambda(1) > 0$. Both non-key readings predict P1 (swapping $a^\star$ moves fixed text by about $\lambda$) and P2 (correct answers that differ from $a^\star$ lose rank against wrong answers when $\beta < 1$ shrinks the judge's own signal relative to noise $\varepsilon$, or when wrong answers that contain $a^\star$ collect part of the bonus, $\gamma > 0$). They differ only at high $v$, which P3a tests.
We use \cref{eq:model} to organize the predictions, not as a fitted model of how judges work; a fit on the registered labels, with its caveats, is in \cref{app:composition}.

\section{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Tasks.}
Our own tasks come from five constraint families (remainder systems, subset sums, Goldbach pairs, divisor counts, digit sums); each stores its full set of correct answers and one member, drawn at random, as the reference.
The \emph{replication pool} has 120 tasks and the \emph{$k$-pool} 75 tasks with exactly $k \in \{1,2,4,8,16\}$ correct answers; a \emph{synthetic grid} has 60 tasks over the same $k$.
\textsc{MACE} contributes factual questions with 1, 2, 4, or 6 correct answers from its River and Language domains, which passed our completeness audit (133 tasks), with the reference drawn at random from each answer list. MATH-500~\citep{hendrycks2021math} supplies unique answers and MBPP supplies programs (\cref{app:setup}).

\paragraph{Candidates and judges.}
Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct writes 64 samples per task at temperature 1 (Qwen2.5-7B for MATH-500 and MBPP).
Each sample is judged \emph{blind}, from the question and the candidate, and \emph{referenced}, with the reference under the heading ``Reference answer (trusted)'', held fixed throughout.
\emph{Score-only} judges answer with an integer from 0 to 10 and nothing else: the open judges Qwen2.5 (1.5B to 14B), phi-4, gemma-2-9b, Llama-3.1-8B, and Mistral-7B, whose score is the expected value over the score tokens, computed exactly where ``10'' is two tokens (\cref{app:estimator}); and Claude Sonnet~5 and GPT-4.1 through their APIs.
\emph{Rationale-first} judges write before a final ``Score: N'' line: Qwen3-8B and Qwen3-14B with the thinking mode on or off, Qwen2.5-7B under the MT-Bench template, and Sonnet~5 through its command-line interface (CLI), asked for a written rationale or with extended thinking on. Sonnet~5's CLI and API scores agree exactly on 67\% of shared calls, so we do not compare the two interfaces directly.
Qwen2.5-7B, phi-4, Qwen2.5-14B, and gemma-2-9b passed a pre-specified score-resolution screen (\emph{screened} judges); Mistral-7B's and GPT-4.1's scores tie too often to rank candidates, so we report but do not count them in summaries (\cref{app:mitigation}).

\paragraph{Reading answers.}
The prompt asks for the final answer inside \texttt{\textbackslash boxed\{\}}, and our registered checkers read it from there, so a correct answer written in prose usually counted as wrong; 38\% to 43\% of replies ignore the format. We therefore also label every reply by the final answer it states in any format, and by the answer Qwen2.5-14B reads from each unboxed reply; the two agree on 88\% of unboxed replies (\cref{app:grid}).\ph

\paragraph{Measurements and conventions.}
The \emph{swap change} is the change in score of identical text when the reference moves. The \emph{margin} is the mean score of correct answers that match the reference minus that of correct answers that do not, and the \emph{added margin} is the referenced margin minus the blind one. The \emph{grading AUROC} is the per-task AUROC for separating correct answers that differ from the reference from wrong answers; the \emph{false-rejection rate} is the share of those correct answers scored 3 or below; changes in rates and accuracies are in percentage points (pp). Contrasts are referenced minus blind, per task, with task-level bootstrap intervals; we do not correct for multiplicity.
Unmarked claims in the main text were pre-specified in a timestamped commit or, for the replication, $k$-pool, and judge-coverage selection endpoints, preregistered on OSF; claims marked \ph{} are post hoc, and claims marked \reana{} are pre-specified re-analyses of an effect whose direction was already known.

\section{Prediction 1: the score follows the reference}
\label{sec:p1}

\paragraph{Prediction.} An answer key gives a correct answer the same score whichever correct answer is the reference.

\paragraph{Swapping the reference.}
We re-judged the replication pool's byte-identical samples with the reference moved to another correct answer.
Text the reference moves onto gains 5.25 points under Qwen2.5-7B\reana{} (4.97 under the registered scorer), 4.09 under phi-4, and 3.04 under Sonnet~5; text it moves off loses 5.41\reana, 3.92, and 2.66; and correct text that matches neither reference barely moves (\cref{fig:concept}c).
All twelve registered cells (two judges, two rubrics, three readouts) went as predicted. On an exploratory pool the swap moves scores by up to 9.8 points for the smallest Qwen2.5 judges (\cref{app:rotation}). We did not run the swap test on GPT-4.1.
Among correct answers that differ from the reference, the registered slope with numeric distance came out as predicted but is driven by a few tasks, and the registered near, middle, and far ordering failed, so we see no reliable distance gradient (\cref{app:composition}).

\paragraph{Verdict.} P1 holds for every score-only configuration we swapped (Qwen2.5 1.5B to 14B, phi-4, Sonnet~5) and for Qwen2.5-7B under the MT-Bench rationale template; it largely fails on arithmetic for judges with a thinking mode and for Sonnet~5 when asked for a written rationale (\cref{sec:p3}).

\section{Prediction 2: with several correct answers, the others are graded worse}
\label{sec:p2}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig2_onevsseveral.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{With one correct answer the reference raises accuracy; with several, it lowers the grading of correct answers that differ from it, without lowering accuracy on natural questions.} Blind (orange) and referenced (blue) values. \textbf{(a)}~Best-of-64 accuracy on MATH-500\reana{} and on \textsc{MACE} questions with one\ph{} and several correct answers. \textbf{(b)}~Grading AUROC; constructed pools read answers in any format.\ph{} Sonnet~5 (API) uses 16-candidate subsamples and is not directly comparable to the 64-candidate open-judge values.}
\label{fig:onevsseveral}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Prediction.} Given P1, a judge that favors the correct answer it is shown separates other correct answers from wrong ones less well; with one correct answer, matching and correctness coincide and the reference should help.

\paragraph{One correct answer.}
The reference raises best-of-64 accuracy by 28.6 to 30.6 points on unique-answer MATH-500, whether it shows a full solution or only the final answer,\reana{} and by 32.4 and 26.5 points on \textsc{MACE}'s single-answer questions for Qwen2.5-7B and phi-4 (\cref{fig:onevsseveral}a).\ph{}
On our constructed $k$-pool it did not help: blind accuracy at $k{=}1$ was already 79\% for Qwen2.5-7B, and its apparent 21-point loss under the registered checker disappears once prose answers are counted as correct.\ph

\paragraph{Several correct answers.}
For Qwen2.5-7B on the replication pool, the grading AUROC falls from 0.87 to 0.60 under the registered checker (\cref{fig:onevsseveral}b). Across ways of reading answers it falls by 0.16 to 0.33 for the four screened judges on the replication pool and the $k$-pool at $k{>}1$, by 0.07 to 0.14 when only boxed replies are kept, and by 0.24 and 0.20 on the replication pool when wrong answers that contain the reference's answer are removed, so both prose answers and look-alike wrong answers contribute (\cref{app:grid}).\ph{}
On \textsc{MACE}'s multi-answer questions the fall is 0.08 for Qwen2.5-7B and 0.06 for phi-4.\ph{}
For Sonnet~5 it is 0.04 and 0.08 on the constructed pools and 0.94 to 0.78 on \textsc{MACE}, where the share of those correct answers it scores 3 or below rises by 35.6~pp \ci{+24.8}{+48.0} (\cref{app:frontier}).
It is small and not robust for Mistral-7B and GPT-4.1, whose scores tie heavily.\ph

\paragraph{Verdict.} P2 holds for grading, as P1 implies: with several correct answers the reference degrades grading of the others for every screened judge and for Sonnet~5. With one correct answer it raises accuracy on MATH-500 and \textsc{MACE} and is neutral on the constructed $k$-pool.

\section{Prediction 3: the effect is not only a failure to verify}
\label{sec:p3}

\paragraph{Prediction.} Verification failure predicts that anchoring vanishes on answers the judge demonstrably verifies without a reference (P3a) and shrinks as verification improves (P3b). Agreement predicts that it persists on verified answers.

\paragraph{Judges that verify can still anchor.}
On 40 factual \textsc{MACE} questions (CLI, one sample per kind of answer), Sonnet~5 without a reference ranks both a matching and a non-matching correct answer above a wrong one 94\% of the time with a score only and 97\% with thinking on, yet the reference adds 4.0 and 3.0 points to its margin (\cref{app:reasoning}).
On its API subsamples (38 tasks), of the correct factual answers that differ from the reference and that it scored 7 or above blind, it scores 35\% at 3 or below once the reference is shown, and of the 81 it had scored a full 10, 19\% (9\% and 15\% on the constructed pools).\ph{}
Keeping answers scored 7 or above blind is a weaker test for the open judges, which score 24\% to 30\% of wrong answers that high;\ph{} with that filter the swap gain is unchanged, $+4.08$ for phi-4 and $+2.79$ for Sonnet~5, and $+5.27$ for Qwen2.5-7B under the exact scorer.\ph

\paragraph{A fourth reading: one intended answer.}
A judge might instead assume the question has one intended answer and treat a trusted reference that differs from the candidate as evidence against it. That reading also predicts P1, P2, and anchoring on verified factual answers, but it should survive rewriting the reference as a trivial computation, since the intended answer stays evident. It does not: writing 31 as ``thirty-one'' leaves phi-4's margin almost unchanged ($+3.68$ against $+3.79$), but writing it as ``30+1'' cuts the margin by 94\% for Qwen2.5-7B and 77\% for phi-4, and masking the final answer of a worked solution removes it; Sonnet~5 goes from $+3.06$ with a numeral to $+3.14$ with words and $+0.47$ with an expression (\cref{app:mechanism,app:frontier}). Judges match a literal answer. A control that encoded the reference with announced decoding steps also removed the margin, so the framing of a rewritten reference may contribute.

\paragraph{Verification reduces the effect.}
Across replication tasks, Qwen2.5-7B's swap size falls as its blind AUROC rises (Spearman $-0.40$ \ci{-0.53}{-0.25}): 5.2 and 5.4 points in the lower two thirds of tasks and 3.6 points in the third it verifies almost perfectly (blind AUROC 0.98).\ph

\paragraph{Reasoning before scoring.}
On arithmetic, a thinking mode nearly removes the effect: it shrinks the swap loss from $-0.69$ to $+0.01$ for Qwen3-8B (a small baseline) and from $-1.98$ to $-0.12$ for Qwen3-14B, and for Sonnet~5 from $-2.85$ to $-0.32$ \ci{-0.67}{-0.07}, as a written rationale without thinking also does ($-0.20$); under the MT-Bench template Qwen2.5-7B still loses 3.59 points (\cref{fig:reasoning}a). With thinking on, these judges score nearly every correct arithmetic answer 10 with or without the reference.\ph{}
On factual questions it does not: with thinking on, false rejections of correct answers that differ from the reference still rise by 18 to 22~pp for Qwen3 and by 12.5~pp for Sonnet~5, whose added margin with thinking on ($+3.0$) is not distinguishable from that without it ($+4.0$; \cref{fig:reasoning}b).

\paragraph{Instructions.}
Telling the judge that other correct answers deserve full credit closes phi-4's margin on the synthetic grid at $k{=}16$ ($+1.91 \to +0.03$) and shrinks Sonnet~5's from $+3.00$ to $+2.08$, but the registered prediction that it lowers the margin at every $k$ was falsified for Qwen2.5-1.5B, 3B, and 7B (\cref{app:mitigation,app:frontier}). Instructions do not separate the readings.

\paragraph{Verdict.} P3 partly holds. Anchoring shrinks where judges can check answers (the most verifiable tasks, thinking on arithmetic), so verification failure contributes. It does not account for Sonnet~5, which penalizes factual answers it ranked above a wrong answer 94\% of the time blind, or for the collapse of the effect when the reference must be computed rather than matched as a literal.

\section{Consequence: grading harm, not selection harm}
\label{sec:cost}

\paragraph{Natural data.}
On \textsc{MACE}'s multi-answer questions the reference did not lower best-of-64 accuracy ($+2.0$ \ci{-5.1}{+10.1} and $+6.1$~pp \ci{-1.0}{+13.1} for Qwen2.5-7B and phi-4), falsifying our registered prediction of a loss, and overall AUROC there was unchanged ($+0.00$ and $+0.02$).\ph{} The harm is specific to correct answers that differ from the reference.

\paragraph{Constructed pools.}
Under the registered checker the reference lowers accuracy at $k{>}1$ on the $k$-pool by 15.4 to 35.9~pp for the four screened judges.\reana{} Counting prose answers as correct, the loss survives only for Qwen2.5-7B ($-30.3$~pp \ci{-48.5}{-12.1} on the $k$-pool, $-9.3$ on the replication pool; \cref{app:grid}).\ph{} It sits where no correct candidate matches the reference: on $k$-pool tasks without one, Qwen2.5-7B loses 64~pp (11 tasks) and phi-4 45~pp, against $-14$ and $0$ where one exists, and on the replication pool Qwen2.5-7B loses 31~pp without one and 5~pp with one.\ph{}
Our OSF-registered selection endpoint, best-of-64 inflation, was confirmed ($+1.19$~\sd; $+1.14$ with answers read in any format); because it is scored by a second rubric that also sees the reference, it is implied by P1 and we do not treat it as separate evidence (\cref{app:setup,app:grid}).

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Reference-guided judging.}
References are the standard remedy for grading math and reasoning with LLM judges~\citep{zheng2023judging,refguided2024,chen2026rethinking}, and judges without them over-credit wrong answers~\citep{generous2026}.
Judge models trained to take a reference as input assume it is a gold answer: JudgeLM studies how judges behave when a reference is added or removed~\citep{zhu2023judgelm}, and Prometheus conditions its rubric scores on one~\citep{kim2024prometheus}; the swap test asks what such judges do when the reference is one of several correct answers.
Judges depend on the reference being correct~\citep{judgingref2026,krumdick2025nofreelabels}, diverge from humans under reference-based grading~\citep{thakur2024judging}, and favor the reference's solution method~\citep{fixation2025}. Generating a reference before judging has been proposed as an improvement~\citep{dobefore2025}; our results suggest that a self-generated reference could anchor the same way on multi-answer tasks.

\paragraph{Single references and many correct answers.}
Reference-based metrics in translation reward outputs that resemble the particular reference~\citep{freitag2020bleu,fomicheva2016reference}, and code evaluation moved from similarity to execution~\citep{chen2021codex}.
Open-domain question answering under-credits correct answers that are missing from the gold list or phrased differently~\citep{min2020ambigqa,si2021whatsinaname,bulian2022tomayto,kamalloo2023evaluating}; in our setting the list is complete and the judge still favors the one answer it is shown.
Verifiers in reinforcement learning miss correct answers written in a different form~\citep{xu2025tinyv,huang2025pitfalls,chen2025xverify}, as our own boxed-answer checker did.

\paragraph{Judge biases and optimization.}
LLM judges favor the first response~\citep{wang2024fair}, longer responses~\citep{dubois2024lengthcontrolled}, their own outputs~\citep{panickssery2024llm}, and scores shown to them~\citep{kapetanovic2026anchoring}. Optimizing against an imperfect evaluator inflates its scores~\citep{gao2022scaling,rafailov2024scaling}, and good average agreement does not guarantee good best-of-$n$ choices~\citep{landesberg2026bestofn}. A judge that commits to its own answer before seeing the candidate resists reward hacking~\citep{zhou2026convincing}.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Labels.} The registered accuracy and false-rejection readouts used a checker that counted most prose answers as wrong; all main-text accuracy claims on constructed pools use post hoc relabelings, which agree on 88\% of unboxed replies and were not checked against human labels. \textsc{MACE}'s answer lists miss 2.3\% to 8.1\% of correct answers.
\item \textbf{Tasks.} Most of our evidence is from constructed arithmetic tasks with one generator; our natural-data evidence is 133 \textsc{MACE} River and Language questions.
\item \textbf{Judges and prompts.} All open judges have at most 14B parameters. Sonnet~5's subsamples were drawn to contain every kind of answer, so its AUROCs are not directly comparable to the 64-candidate open-judge values, and its reasoning and grading results come from different interfaces. Every referenced prompt called the reference ``trusted'', which may strengthen deference. We tested no reasoning judge from a third frontier provider.
\item \textbf{Downstream.} Three DPO runs against the referenced judge did not show the policy copying the reference, and the protected arm failed its manipulation check, so we have not shown that training exploits the bias (\cref{app:dpo}).
\item \textbf{Registration.} Most registrations rely on timestamped commits, and the uniform verdict rule was applied retroactively to earlier predictions after most of their outcomes were known (\cref{app:ledger}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

A reference answer is a good check when a question has one right answer.
When it has several, judges favor the one they are shown: swapping it moves the score of unchanged text, and correct answers that differ from it are scored 3 or below up to 28\% of the time for Qwen2.5-7B and 36~pp more often for Sonnet~5 on factual questions, including answers the judge ranks correctly without a reference.
Judges with a thinking mode largely escape this on arithmetic, where they can check the answer, and selection suffers mainly when no candidate matches the reference.
The mitigations we tried are not a fix: scoring blind first and then revealing the reference trailed blind judging by 10 to 15~pp on the $k$-pool under the registered checker and did not beat the reference on \textsc{MACE}, and taking the higher of the blind and referenced scores lost up to 2.6~pp for the open judges and 2.4~pp for Sonnet~5 on the $k$-pool.\ph{}
When correct answers can differ from the reference, do not trust a low reference-guided score on an answer that differs from the reference: re-score it without the reference, or check it against the task's answer set.

\subsection*{AI use statement}
Generative AI models were the experimental subjects: open and frontier LLMs generated candidate answers and served as the judges under study (\cref{sec:setup}, \cref{app:setup}). One pilot (\cref{app:mathconstraint}) used a Claude model to generate candidate solutions, each verified by exact checks, and Qwen2.5-14B read unboxed replies for one labeling. We used generative AI tools to help write analysis code, draft and edit text, produce figures, search and verify related work, audit the preregistration ledger, and label a sample of dataset questions for the prevalence audit; both labeling passes came from one model family, were adjudicated against each other, and were not checked by human annotators. Every number in the paper is produced by committed analysis scripts from stored model outputs, and we checked every claim against those outputs. We take responsibility for the final content of this work.

\subsection*{Ethics statement}
This work studies a failure of automated evaluation that can make models look better or worse than they are. We release no new model and no attack on a deployed system. A model optimized against a reference-guided judge on multi-answer tasks may be rewarded for matching the reference rather than for being correct, which is why we pair the failure with measurements of when it matters. No human subjects were involved.

\subsection*{Reproducibility statement}
\Cref{app:setup} specifies every pool, judge, prompt, and scorer, and \cref{app:ledger} lists each prediction with its registration type and outcome. The anonymized supplementary material contains task generators and checkers, candidate pools, the answer labelings, all judge score files, the analysis scripts that produce every number, table, and figure, and the registration documents with commit hashes replaced by stable tokens.
