\title{Reference Answers Mislead LLM Judges\\When Many Answers Are Valid}

\author{Anonymous authors\\Paper under double-blind review}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
LLM judges are often given a reference answer, because checking a candidate against a known-correct answer is easier than judging it from scratch.
When a question has several correct answers, the reference is only one of them.
Holding a candidate's text fixed and moving the reference to a different correct answer changes its score by 2.7 to 5.4 points out of 10 for Qwen2.5-7B, phi-4, and Claude Sonnet~5, and by 3.6 points under the verbatim MT-Bench template.
The reference makes judges worse at telling correct answers that differ from it from wrong ones: the per-task AUROC falls from 0.87 to 0.60 on our constructed tasks and from 0.74 to 0.66 on the natural \textsc{MACE} benchmark.
Whether grading gets worse overall depends on how well the judge verifies without a reference.
Where it verifies well, overall AUROC falls by 0.22 and best-of-64 selection loses up to 35.9 points; on \textsc{MACE}, where it verifies poorly, the reference's help against wrong answers outweighs the damage.
The effect needs a final value stated explicitly in the reference, and extended thinking removes it on arithmetic tasks but not on factual questions in two Qwen3 models.
Scoring blind first and then revealing the reference limits the worst-case selection loss for two of the three judges we compared.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Ask for any divisor of 91 strictly between 1 and 91.
Both 7 and 13 are correct.
Give an LLM judge the question, a candidate answer, and the reference answer 7, and it can do one of two things that look alike: check whether the candidate is right, or check whether it says 7.

We gave Claude Sonnet~5, with extended thinking off, correct answers to 93 questions that each have several correct answers.
Without a reference, it scored correct answers that happened to match the reference and correct answers that did not about the same, 9.19 and 9.09 out of 10.
With the reference in the prompt, matching answers rose to 9.99 and non-matching ones fell to 6.93 (\cref{fig:teaser}a), and the share of correct non-matching answers scored 3 or below rose from 4.9\% to 26.2\%.

Reference-guided judging is standard practice.
MT-Bench's math and reasoning template tells the judge to begin ``by comparing the assistant's answer with the reference answer''~\citep{zheng2023judging}, references raise agreement with human labels~\citep{refguided2024}, judges without one are too generous~\citep{generous2026}, and a recent study of math grading found comparison against a reference to be the most reliable single-pass strategy~\citep{chen2026rethinking}.
The same judges grade benchmark answers, choose among sampled answers, and score rollouts for training, so what they reward is what gets optimized~\citep{gao2022scaling}.
Comparing against a reference is safe only when the reference is the only correct answer.
Curated math benchmarks mostly satisfy that; other data do not.
In an LLM-labeled audit of 200 questions per dataset, 18.5\% of NQ-open questions admitted more than one correct answer (30.0\% counting answers that changed over time), as did 2.5\% of GSM8K and DeepScaleR problems, mostly because they were underspecified (\cref{app:prevalence}). Tasks that ask for an example, a construction, or a program admit many correct answers by design.

Prior work comes close to this failure without isolating it.
\citet{fixation2025} name it ``solution fixation'' but measure it by rewriting flawed solutions in the reference's style.
\citet{judgingref2026} and \citet{krumdick2025nofreelabels} replace the gold reference with an incorrect one, and work on verifier false negatives in reinforcement learning studies different surface forms of the same answer~\citep{xu2025tinyv,huang2025pitfalls}.
None of it varies which correct answer is the reference or measures what that does to grading and selection.
We do this on tasks where correctness is decided by a program or an audited answer list, so we know every correct answer and, on our own tasks, can set how many there are:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{The score follows the reference} (\cref{sec:follows}). Moving the reference to a different correct answer, with the candidate's text unchanged, moves its score by 2.7 to 5.4 points, while correct answers that match neither reference barely move. This holds for answers the judge had already accepted without a reference.
\item \textbf{The judge needs a stated final value} (\cref{sec:mechanism}). The effect survives writing the reference in words, and largely disappears when the reference is an expression to evaluate or a worked solution with its final answer masked.
\item \textbf{Grading of correct non-matching answers gets worse everywhere; grading and selection overall get worse only where the judge verifies well without a reference} (\cref{sec:cost}).
\item \textbf{Thinking helps on checkable tasks, and a two-step protocol hedges} (\cref{sec:fix}). Extended thinking removes the effect on arithmetic tasks for Qwen3-8B and Qwen3-14B but not on factual questions; scoring blind before revealing the reference bounds the worst-case loss for two of three judges.
\end{itemize}

\section{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Tasks.}
Our own tasks come from five constraint families (remainder systems, subset sums, Goldbach pairs, divisor counts, digit sums), for example ``name two primes that sum to 100.''
Each task stores its full set of correct answers and one member, drawn at random when the task is built, that serves as the reference.
The two main pools are a 120-task \emph{replication pool}\osf{} and a 75-task \emph{$k$-pool}\osf{} with exactly $k \in \{1,2,4,8,16\}$ correct answers per task, 15 tasks each, of which 53 fall in the pass-rate band used for selection.
We also use the River and Language domains of \textsc{MACE}~\citep{mace2026}, factual questions with 1, 2, 4, or 6 correct answers whose answer lists passed a completeness audit (133 tasks), MBPP~\citep{austin2021mbpp} for code, and MATH-500~\citep{hendrycks2021math} for unique answers (\cref{app:setup}).

\paragraph{Candidates, judges, and scores.}
Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct writes 64 samples per task at temperature 1 (Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct for MATH-500 and MBPP).
Each sample is judged \emph{blind}, from the question and the candidate, and \emph{referenced}, with the reference added under the heading ``Reference answer (trusted)''.
The main judges are Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct and phi-4; we also test the Qwen2.5 family from 1.5B to 14B, Llama-3.1-8B, gemma-2-9b, Mistral-7B, Qwen3-8B and Qwen3-14B, Claude Sonnet~5, and GPT-4.1.
The judge answers with an integer from 0 to 10 under one of two rubric wordings; rubric~$a$, used in every figure, asks it to rate correctness ``using the reference to check it'', and rubric~$b$ tells it to compare the candidate against the reference (\cref{app:setup}).
For open judges the score is the expected value over the score tokens at the first generated position. When a tokenizer writes ``10'' as two tokens, we compute that value's probability exactly with one more forward step; an earlier scorer approximated it, and the pre-specified rescoring in \cref{app:estimator} shows that the approximation understated the effect slightly.\pre

\paragraph{Measurements.}
The \emph{rotation change} is the change in the score of identical text when the reference moves.
For \emph{grading}, we use the \emph{false-rejection rate}, the share of correct answers that differ from the reference scored 3 or below, and the per-task AUROC of the score for separating correct answers from wrong ones.
For \emph{selection}, we use the accuracy of the best-of-64 pick under rubric~$a$, averaging over ties.
The preregistered endpoint of the OSF studies is best-of-64 \emph{inflation}, how many pool standard deviations above an average answer of the same correctness the chosen answer is scored (\cref{app:setup}); we report it where it was registered.
All contrasts are referenced minus blind, per task, with task-level bootstrap intervals.

\section{Moving the reference moves the score}
\label{sec:follows}

If the reference were an answer key, a candidate's score could not depend on which of two correct answers sits in the reference slot.
We kept the replication pool's samples byte-identical and re-judged them with the reference moved to another correct answer, the one that appears most often among each task's correct samples.

Text that the reference moves onto gains 5.25 points under Qwen2.5-7B and 4.09 under phi-4, text it moves off loses 5.41 and 3.92, and correct text that matches neither moves by $+0.12$ and $+0.30$ (\cref{fig:teaser}b).
All twelve pre-specified cells hold (two judges, two rubrics, three predictions; \cref{app:rotation}), and Qwen2.5-7B's greedy integer scores move even more ($+5.65$ and $-5.78$), so the effect does not come from how we average over score tokens.\pre{}
Sonnet~5 shows the same pattern through the API ($+3.04$ and $-2.66$) and through the Claude command-line tool with its system prompt replaced by our rubric, on 60 of these tasks ($+3.57$ and $-2.85$).\pre

A charitable reading is that the judge did not know the other answer was correct and the reference taught it.
If so, answers the judge had already scored at 7 or above while blind should not move.
They move just as much: $+5.27$ for Qwen2.5-7B (96\% of samples kept), $+4.08$ for phi-4 (98\%), and $+2.79$ for Sonnet~5 (91\%).\pre

\section{What the judge is checking}
\label{sec:mechanism}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig3_mechanism.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{The effect needs a final value stated in the reference, and it shrinks from integer to program answers.}
\textbf{(a)}~Anchoring margin (mean score of correct answers that match the reference minus those that do not) when the reference is written as a numeral, in words, or as an expression (replication pool, 93 tasks), and when a worked solution is shown with its final answer visible or masked (21 $k$-pool tasks with a single-number answer). Rubric~$a$.\pre{}
\textbf{(b)}~Added best-of-64 inflation when the same content is requested as an integer, a pair, a JSON object, or a Python program whose output is checked (25 new tasks). Program outputs have few distinct correct answers, and phi-4's program value uses checker labels corrected after registration.\pre}
\label{fig:mechanism}
\end{figure}

A cheap way to use a reference is to compare the candidate's final answer with the reference's.
Three pre-specified rewrites of the reference test whether judges do this (\cref{fig:mechanism}a).
Writing 31 as ``thirty-one'' keeps most of the effect for both judges, although the reference then shares no characters with a numeric answer.
Writing it as ``30+1'' removes most of it, and so does masking the final answer in a worked solution that anchors strongly when the answer is visible.
Among correct answers that differ from the reference, being numerically close to it does not help (\cref{app:composition}).
Judges behave as if they compare the candidate's final answer with a final value stated explicitly in the reference: the comparison survives a change of surface form, but not a reference that must first be evaluated or that states no final value.\pre

The same account predicts a weaker effect when answers are artifacts rather than values.
Requesting the same content as an integer, a pair, a JSON object, or a program shrinks the added inflation from $+1.66$ to $+0.13$ for Qwen2.5-7B and from $+1.35$ to $-0.37$ for phi-4 (\cref{fig:mechanism}b), but programs also have fewer distinct correct outputs (a median of two per task), so the two explanations are confounded. A canonical MBPP solution shown as the reference lowered inflation rather than raising it, contrary to our prediction (\cref{app:code}).\pre

\section{What the reference costs}
\label{sec:cost}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig3_cost.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{The reference damages grading of correct answers that differ from it everywhere; overall, it hurts where the blind judge already verifies well.}
\textbf{(a)}~Per-task AUROC of Qwen2.5-7B's score for separating correct answers from wrong ones, blind (orange) and with the reference (blue): for correct answers that differ from the reference, and for all correct answers. Blind verification AUROC is 0.87 on the replication pool and 0.73 on \textsc{MACE}.\pre{}
\textbf{(b)}~Change in the accuracy of the best-of-64 pick when the reference is shown, by pool; filled circles Qwen2.5-7B (exact estimator), hollow squares phi-4. The replication pool is the preregistered population (105 tasks); the $k$-pool has 14 tasks at $k{=}1$ and 39 at $k{>}1$; \textsc{MACE} has 34 and 99; MATH-500 has 105, with the full reference solution or its final answer alone.\ph}
\label{fig:cost}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Grading.}
A judge that grades by agreement should become worse at separating correct answers that differ from the reference from wrong answers.
It does, in both pools we tested (\cref{fig:cost}a). For Qwen2.5-7B, the per-task AUROC for these two groups falls from 0.87 blind to 0.60 with the reference on the replication pool, and from 0.74 to 0.66 on \textsc{MACE}.\pre{} phi-4 shows the same pattern, from 0.90 to 0.65 and from 0.79 to 0.73.\ph{}
The share of these correct answers scored 3 or below rises from 0.4\% to 28.1\% (68.9\% at 5 or below), and for Sonnet~5 from 4.9\% to 26.2\%.\ph{}
Correct answers that match the reference move the other way, from 0.85 to 0.97 and from 0.69 to 0.98.

\paragraph{Grading overall depends on blind verification.}
Whether the judge becomes a worse grader of all answers depends on what the reference adds.
Blind, Qwen2.5-7B separates correct from wrong answers at an AUROC of 0.87 on the replication pool and on the $k$-pool, and 0.73 on \textsc{MACE}.
Where it already verifies well, the reference mostly redistributes credit toward matching answers, and overall AUROC falls by 0.22. On \textsc{MACE}, the reference's help against wrong answers outweighs the damage and overall AUROC rises by 0.07.\pre{} phi-4 repeats both results, at blind AUROC 0.90 and 0.79: overall AUROC falls by 0.19 on the replication pool and rises by 0.07 on \textsc{MACE}.\ph

\paragraph{Selection.}
Best-of-64 selection shows the same pattern with wider intervals (\cref{fig:cost}b).
On the preregistered replication pool the reference lowers the accuracy of the pick by 4.8 points for Qwen2.5-7B, an interval that includes zero, and by 11.4 points for phi-4 \ci{-19.1}{-4.8}.
On the $k$-pool, pooled over $k{>}1$, it lowers accuracy by 35.9 points for Qwen2.5-7B and by \pending{X to Y} for Qwen2.5-14B, gemma-2-9b, and Mistral-7B.\pre{}
The loss is largest at $k{=}16$, and Qwen2.5-7B also loses 21.4 points at $k{=}1$, where the reference is the only correct answer; wrong answers that contain the reference's text receive a bonus of 1.4 to 1.7 points (\cref{app:composition}), which may explain it.
On \textsc{MACE} the reference raises accuracy, almost entirely on single-answer questions ($+29.4$ points for Qwen2.5-7B at $k{=}1$, against $+2.0$ at $k{>}1$); on questions with four or six correct answers the blind judge already picks a correct answer 84.4\% to 93.8\% of the time (\cref{app:mace}).
On MATH-500, where correct answers rarely differ from the reference and blind verification is weakest (AUROC 0.69), the reference raises Qwen2.5-7B's accuracy by 30.5 points as a full solution and by 28.6 as a final answer alone (\cref{app:estimator}).\pre

\section{What to do about it}
\label{sec:fix}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig2_reasoning.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{Extended thinking removes the effect on checkable arithmetic tasks, not on factual questions.}
\textbf{(a)}~Score change of identical correct text when the reference moves off it, on the replication pool, grouped by how the score is read: expected scores over score tokens, or integers sampled from the reply (Sonnet~5's tool call, the MT-Bench template, and the Qwen3 models at temperature 0.6, which think before a final ``Score: N'' line; hollow markers: thinking on). Qwen3 rows and the MT-Bench row use a subsample of up to three samples per class per task.\pre{}
\textbf{(b)}~Referenced-minus-blind anchoring margin with thinking off (filled) and on (hollow), on arithmetic tasks (a 16-sample subsample of the $k$-pool, 19 tasks) and on factual \textsc{MACE} questions (81 tasks). 95\% intervals.\pre}
\label{fig:reasoning}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Verify when you can.}
When a program can check correctness, no judge is needed.
When a judge can check answers by reasoning, extended thinking helps.
With the same weights, prompt, and sampling, turning on thinking removes the rotation effect on arithmetic tasks for Qwen3-8B ($-0.69$ to $+0.01$) and Qwen3-14B ($-1.98$ to $-0.12$), at 569 to 1,092 generated tokens per candidate for Qwen3-8B (\cref{fig:reasoning}a).\pre{}
On factual \textsc{MACE} questions, which these models verify less well blind, the thinking judges still anchor: the margin increase is $+3.62$ with thinking on against $+6.16$ off for Qwen3-8B, and \pending{X} against $+7.07$ for Qwen3-14B (\cref{fig:reasoning}b).\pre{}
A shorter explanation is not enough: under the verbatim MT-Bench template, whose explanations average 259 tokens, Qwen2.5-7B's score still falls by 3.59 points when the reference moves off a correct answer.\pre

\paragraph{Prompts help only some judges.}
On a synthetic grid of 60 tasks whose correct sets enumerate exactly, adding ``the reference shown is one valid answer among possibly many \ldots\ any correct answer deserves full credit'' removes phi-4's margin at $k{=}16$ ($+1.91$ to $+0.03$) and shrinks Qwen2.5-14B's ($+3.34$ to $+1.21$), but does not help smaller judges.\pre{}
On Sonnet~5 two such wordings shrink the $k$-pool margin from $+3.00$ to $+2.08$ and $+2.60$, and on GPT-4.1 from $+0.99$ to $+0.46$ and $+0.59$.\pre{}
Showing up to three correct references instead of one removes the margin toward the original reference but does not restore blind-level selection (\cref{app:mitigation}).

\paragraph{Blind, then reveal.}
The protocol we preregistered as the main mitigation scores the candidate blind in a first call and, in a second, shows the reference together with that blind score and states that a correct answer differing from the reference keeps its score.
On the $k$-pool it removes the preregistered inflation at $k{>}1$ for Qwen2.5-7B and phi-4 ($-0.05$ and $+0.16$, against $+1.50$ and $+1.07$ with the reference alone), but gemma-2-9b keeps most of its inflation.\osf{}
On the 58 multi-answer tasks of the variant study, removing the blind score from the second call brings the inflation back ($+1.42$ and $+0.36$), so the displayed number does the work, consistent with judges anchoring on scores shown to them~\citep{kapetanovic2026anchoring}.\pre{}
No protocol wins everywhere: the plain reference is best on MATH-500 and \textsc{MACE}, and blind judging is best on the $k$-pool. Blind-then-reveal is a hedge (\cref{tab:regret}).
Its largest loss against the best protocol in each pool is 10.2 points for Qwen2.5-7B and 1.7 for Qwen2.5-14B, against 30.8 and 20.5 for the plain reference and 30.5 and 27.6 for blind judging. For phi-4, measured on two pools, blind judging has the smaller worst case.\ph

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Reference-guided judging.}
References are the standard remedy for grading math and reasoning with LLM judges~\citep{zheng2023judging,refguided2024,chen2026rethinking}, and judges without them over-credit wrong answers~\citep{generous2026}.
Judges depend on the reference being correct: replacing it with a wrong answer collapses their accuracy~\citep{judgingref2026}, and without a correct reference they agree with experts mainly on questions they can answer themselves~\citep{krumdick2025nofreelabels}.
\citet{fixation2025} describe a bias toward the reference's solution method, measured with flawed solutions rewritten in the reference's style.
We keep the reference correct and vary which correct answer it is.
Generating a reference before judging has been proposed as an improvement~\citep{dobefore2025}; our results predict that a self-generated reference anchors the same way on multi-answer tasks, which we have not tested.

\paragraph{Verifiers and many correct answers.}
Rule-based and model-based verifiers in reinforcement learning miss correct answers written in a different form~\citep{xu2025tinyv,huang2025pitfalls,chen2025xverify}, which concerns surface forms of one answer; we study distinct correct answers.
RL data pipelines often avoid them by keeping only problems that ``admit a single correct answer''~\citep{albalak2025bigmath}.
Human raters shown a reference translation favor candidates close to it~\citep{fomicheva2016reference}, execution-based evaluation of code replaced similarity to a single reference solution~\citep{chen2021codex}, and single-reference evaluation undercounts correct outputs when questions admit several~\citep{min2020ambigqa}; \textsc{RewardBench-2} and \textsc{MACE} build benchmarks around such questions~\citep{malik2025rewardbench2,mace2026}.

\paragraph{Judge biases and optimization.}
LLM judges favor the first response~\citep{wang2024fair}, longer responses~\citep{dubois2024lengthcontrolled}, and their own outputs~\citep{panickssery2024llm}, anchor on scores shown to them~\citep{kapetanovic2026anchoring}, and can be fooled by content-free responses~\citep{zhao2025onetoken}.
Optimizing against an imperfect evaluator inflates its scores~\citep{gao2022scaling,rafailov2024scaling}, and good average agreement does not guarantee good best-of-$n$ choices~\citep{landesberg2026bestofn}.
Closest to our protocol, a reference-free judge that commits to its own answer before seeing the candidate resists self-play reward hacking~\citep{zhou2026convincing}; blind-then-reveal applies the same idea of committing first to reference-guided judging.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Tasks.} Our results come from constraint problems, two audited factual domains of \textsc{MACE}, MBPP, and MATH-500. We do not test free-form generation, long references, contested correctness, or step-level verifiers.
\item \textbf{Which pools, which judges.} The rule that blind verification decides the overall cost rests on one natural and two constructed pools, measured with one judge. Open judges are 14B parameters or smaller; the thinking contrast uses two models of one family, and we did not test extended thinking on a frontier judge.
\item \textbf{Scores.} Several appendix analyses use the earlier scorer. Mistral-7B's tokenizer has no single-token digits, so its scores are parsed integers, and a third of its blind replies contained no score; GPT-4.1's scores tie heavily (\cref{app:frontier}).
\item \textbf{Registration.} Accuracy and false-rejection readouts on the OSF pools are post hoc; their registered endpoint is inflation. Three experiments have third-party timestamps and the rest rely on git commits. With a fixed seed, the registered blind-then-reveal contrast for Mistral-7B excludes zero in 11 of 20 seeds (\cref{app:ledger}).
\item \textbf{Training.} A DPO policy trained against a referenced judge raised that judge's score while held-out accuracy fell, but every control arm failed its manipulation check, so we cannot attribute this to the reference (\cref{app:dpo}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

A reference answer is a good check when a question has one right answer.
When it has several, the reference is one of them, and judges grade agreement with it: moving the reference moves the score of unchanged text, and correct answers that differ from it are often scored 3 out of 10 or below.
Judges compare the candidate with a final value stated in the reference, and extended thinking replaces that comparison with verification only where the answer can be checked by reasoning.
Whether the distortion makes grading and selection worse overall depends on how well the judge verifies without the reference and on how often correct answers differ from it. Both can be measured on a small labeled sample before a judge is deployed, and when a program can check correctness, that check is better than any judge.

\subsection*{AI use statement}
In this work, generative AI models were the experimental subjects: open and frontier LLMs generated candidate answers and served as the judges under study (\cref{sec:setup}, \cref{app:setup}). One pilot (\cref{app:mathconstraint}) used a Claude model to generate candidate solutions, each verified by exact programmatic checks. We used generative AI tools to help write analysis code, draft and edit text, produce figures, search and verify related work, and label a sample of dataset questions for the prevalence audit, whose labels were double-coded and adjudicated but not checked by human annotators. Every number in the paper is produced by committed analysis scripts from stored model outputs, and we checked every claim against those outputs. We take responsibility for the final content of this work, including text, claims, and artifacts produced with the aid of generative AI.

\subsection*{Ethics statement}
This work studies a failure of automated evaluation that can make optimized models look better or worse than they are. We release no new model and no attack on a deployed system. Anyone optimizing against a reference-guided judge on multi-answer tasks can exploit the failure, which is why we pair it with measurements of when it matters and recommend verifying judges on the tasks where they are used. No human subjects were involved.

The anonymized supplementary material contains task generators and correctness checkers, candidate pools, all judge score files, the analysis scripts that produce every number, table, and figure, and the registration documents with commit hashes replaced by stable tokens.
