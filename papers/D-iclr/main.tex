\title{The Reference Is Not an Answer Key:\\LLM Judges Favor the Correct Answer They Are Shown}

\author{Anonymous authors\\Paper under double-blind review}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
LLM judges are routinely given a reference answer, on the premise that it acts as an answer key.
We show that when a question has several correct answers, a judge's score follows which correct answer it is shown.
On tasks whose correct answers are enumerated by a program or an audited list, swapping the reference between two correct answers moves the score of byte-identical text by 2.7 to 5.4 points out of 10 for Qwen2.5-7B, phi-4, and Claude Sonnet~5.
Correct answers that differ from the reference become harder to separate from wrong ones: grading AUROC falls by 0.17 to 0.27 for four open judges on constructed tasks and by 0.04 to 0.15 for Sonnet~5, although a reference still raises best-of-64 accuracy by about 30 points when the answer is unique.
The effect is not only a failure to verify: on factual questions, Sonnet~5 ranks both correct answers above a wrong one 94\% of the time without a reference, yet the reference widens the lead of the answer that matches it by 4.0 points.
Reasoning before scoring removes the effect on arithmetic but only shrinks it on factual questions.
Best-of-64 selection is hurt less than grading: on natural multi-answer questions the reference did not lower accuracy.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Ask for a two-digit number that is divisible by 5 and whose digits sum to 8.
Both 35 and 80 are correct.
Qwen2.5-7B, shown a candidate that reasons its way to 80, scores it 10.0 out of 10 with no reference, 2.9 when the reference answer is 35, and 9.3 when the reference is 80 (\cref{fig:concept}b).
The text never changed; only the correct answer the judge was shown did.
This example swaps more than most, but over 120 such tasks the same judge moves correct text by about 5 points (\cref{fig:concept}c).

Reference-guided judging is standard practice.
MT-Bench's math and reasoning template tells the judge to begin ``by comparing the assistant's answer with the reference answer''~\citep{zheng2023judging}, references raise agreement with human labels~\citep{refguided2024}, judges without one are too generous~\citep{generous2026}, and comparison against a reference is the most reliable single-pass strategy for grading math~\citep{chen2026rethinking}.
The same judges grade benchmarks, choose among sampled answers, and score rollouts for training, so what they reward is what gets optimized~\citep{gao2022scaling}.
The practice is safe when a question has one correct answer, as on curated math benchmarks: our LLM-labeled audit of 200 questions per dataset (not checked by humans) found 0\% to 2.5\% with several on MATH-500, GSM8K, and DeepScaleR (\cref{app:prevalence}).
It is not safe where answers are not unique: 30\% of NQ-open questions in the same audit (18.5\% excluding answers that changed over time), benchmarks built around several valid answers such as \textsc{RewardBench-2} Ties and \textsc{MACE}~\citep{malik2025rewardbench2,mace2026}, and tasks that ask for an example or a construction.

\paragraph{Three readings of a reference.}
If the reference is an \emph{answer key}, the judge checks a candidate against every correct answer, and a correct answer scores the same whichever one is shown.
Under \emph{verification failure}, the judge cannot tell on its own that a different answer is correct, so the reference is its only evidence.
Under \emph{agreement}, the judge favors the correct answer that matches the reference even when it can recognize the others, whether because it rewards agreement or because it assumes the question has one intended answer; we do not separate these two.
We test three predictions:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=2pt]
\item[\textbf{P1}] \textbf{The score follows the reference.} Swapping which correct answer is the reference moves the score of unchanged text (\cref{sec:p1}). This rules out the answer key.
\item[\textbf{P2}] \textbf{With several correct answers, the others are graded worse.} Correct answers that differ from the reference lose ground against wrong answers. This does not follow from P1 alone (\cref{sec:model}). With one correct answer, every reading expects the reference to help (\cref{sec:p2}).
\item[\textbf{P3}] \textbf{The effect survives verification.} It persists on correct answers the judge already accepts without a reference. Verification failure predicts that it vanishes there; agreement predicts that it persists (\cref{sec:p3}).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{What we find.}
P1 holds for every judge we swapped that outputs only a score (2.7 to 5.4 points on the replication pool).
P2 holds for the four open judges that passed our score-resolution screen and for Sonnet~5, on constructed and on natural questions.
P3 holds for judges that score without reasoning first, most clearly Sonnet~5 on factual questions.
Reasoning before scoring is the one change that removes the effect, and only on arithmetic; it does not raise the judges' blind AUROC there, so why it helps remains open.
Selection loses accuracy mainly on constructed tasks where no candidate matches the reference.
We registered 156 predictions before their analyses; \cref{app:ledger} reports all of them, including the 25 that were not confirmed or were falsified.

Prior work comes close without isolating this failure.
\citet{fixation2025} name ``solution fixation'' but measure it with flawed solutions rewritten in the reference's style; \citet{judgingref2026} and \citet{krumdick2025nofreelabels} replace the reference with a wrong one; human raters shown different reference translations favor candidates close to the one they see~\citep{fomicheva2016reference}. Benchmarks with several valid answers score judges against all of them but do not vary which one the judge is shown, and no study of LLM judges that we know of does.

\section{A model of the three readings}
\label{sec:model}

Let $A^\star$ be the set of correct answers to a question and $a^\star \in A^\star$ the one shown as the reference. Let $b(c)$ be a judge's blind score for candidate $c$, $s(c; a^\star)$ its referenced score, and $v_c \in [0, 1]$ how reliably the judge recognizes $c$ as correct without a reference. We write
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
s(c; a^\star) \;\approx\;& \beta\, b(c) \;+\; \kappa\, \mathbb{I}[\,\mathrm{ans}(c) \in A^\star\,] \;+\; \lambda(v_c)\, \mathbb{I}[\,\mathrm{ans}(c) = a^\star\,] \\
&+\; \gamma\, \mathbb{I}[\,c \text{ is wrong and contains } a^\star\,] \;+\; \varepsilon, \qquad \beta \le 1 .
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:model}
\end{equation}
An answer key checks candidates against the whole set: $\kappa \ge 0$ and $\lambda = \gamma = 0$. The other two readings reward matching the one answer shown. Verification failure sets $\lambda(v) = \lambda_0 (1 - v)$, a bonus that vanishes once the judge can recognize the answer itself; agreement keeps $\lambda(1) > 0$.
With one correct answer, $A^\star = \{a^\star\}$ and the $\kappa$ and $\lambda$ terms coincide, so every reading expects the reference to help and the unique-answer case cannot separate them (P2$'$).
With several, both non-key readings predict P1: swapping $a^\star$ moves fixed text by about $\lambda$. P2 needs more than P1. A bonus for matching answers leaves correct non-matching answers ranked as before against wrong ones, unless the referenced score keeps only part of the judge's own signal ($\beta < 1$, relative to the noise $\varepsilon$) or wrong answers that contain $a^\star$ collect part of the bonus ($\gamma > 0$).
The two non-key readings differ only where $v_c$ is high, which P3 tests. We take a high blind score (7 or above, or a full 10) as the judge accepting an answer, and per-task blind AUROC as a coarser measure of how well it verifies.
\Cref{eq:model} organizes the predictions; it is not a fitted model (a fit, with its caveats, is in \cref{app:composition}).

\section{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Tasks.}
Our own tasks come from five constraint families (remainder systems, subset sums, Goldbach pairs, divisor counts, digit sums); each stores its full set of correct answers and one member, drawn at random, as the reference.
The \emph{replication pool} has 120 tasks and the \emph{$k$-pool} 75 tasks with exactly $k \in \{1,2,4,8,16\}$ correct answers; a \emph{synthetic grid} has 60 tasks over the same $k$.
\textsc{MACE} contributes factual questions with 1, 2, 4, or 6 correct answers from its River and Language domains, which passed our completeness audit; 133 of them fall in the pass-rate band where selection can change correctness. The reference is drawn at random from each answer list. MATH-500~\citep{hendrycks2021math} supplies unique answers (\cref{app:setup}).

\paragraph{Candidates and judges.}
Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct writes 64 samples per task at temperature 1 (Qwen2.5-7B for MATH-500).
Each sample is judged \emph{blind}, from the question and the candidate, and \emph{referenced}, with the reference under the heading ``Reference answer (trusted)''.
\emph{Score-only} judges answer with an integer from 0 to 10 and nothing else: the open judges Qwen2.5 (1.5B to 14B), phi-4, gemma-2-9b, Llama-3.1-8B, and Mistral-7B, whose score is the expected value over the score tokens, and Claude Sonnet~5 and GPT-4.1 through their APIs. For tokenizers that write ``10'' as two tokens, the \emph{exact scorer} adds one decoding step to split that probability between 1 and 10; the \emph{original scorer} approximated it and gives slightly smaller swap effects (\cref{app:estimator}). We use the exact scorer unless stated.
\emph{Rationale-first} judges write before a final ``Score: N'' line: Qwen3-8B and Qwen3-14B with the thinking mode on or off, Qwen2.5-7B under the MT-Bench template, and Sonnet~5 through its command-line interface (CLI), asked to explain first or with extended thinking on. Sonnet~5's CLI and API scores agree exactly on only 67\% of shared calls, so we compare Sonnet~5 results only within an interface.
Qwen2.5-7B, phi-4, Qwen2.5-14B, and gemma-2-9b passed a pre-specified score-resolution screen (\emph{screened} judges). Mistral-7B's and GPT-4.1's scores tie too often to rank candidates, so we leave them out of selection summaries but report their grading AUROC, which handles ties (\cref{app:mitigation}).

\paragraph{Reading answers.}
The prompt asks for the final answer inside \texttt{\textbackslash boxed\{\}}, and our registered checkers read it from there, so a correct answer written in prose usually counted as wrong; 38\% to 43\% of replies ignore the format.
On the constructed pools the main text therefore uses \emph{any-format labels}, which credit the final answer a reply states in any format.\ph{} The registered labels, a labeling in which Qwen2.5-14B reads each unboxed reply (it agrees with the any-format rule on 88\% of them), and boxed replies alone are compared in \cref{app:grid}. \textsc{MACE} and MATH-500 use their answer lists and unique answers.

\paragraph{Measurements and conventions.}
The \emph{swap change} is the change in score of identical text when the reference moves. The \emph{grading AUROC} is the per-task AUROC for separating correct answers that differ from the reference from wrong answers, and the \emph{false-rejection rate} is the share of those correct answers scored 3 or below. \emph{Best-of-64 accuracy} is the correctness of the highest-scored candidate, averaged over ties. The \emph{margin} is the mean score of correct answers that match the reference minus that of correct answers that do not. Contrasts are referenced minus blind, per task, with task-level bootstrap intervals; changes in rates are in percentage points (pp). We do not correct for multiplicity.
Unmarked results were pre-specified in a timestamped commit or on OSF; results marked \ph{} are post hoc, and results marked \reana{} are pre-specified re-analyses of an effect whose direction was already known.

\section{Prediction 1: the score follows the reference}
\label{sec:p1}

\paragraph{Prediction.} An answer key gives a correct answer the same score whichever correct answer is the reference.

\paragraph{Swapping the reference.}
We re-judged the replication pool's byte-identical samples with the reference moved to another correct answer.
Text the reference moves onto gains 5.25 points under Qwen2.5-7B,\reana{} 4.09 under phi-4, and 3.04 under Sonnet~5; text it moves off loses 5.41,\reana{} 3.92, and 2.66; correct text that matches neither reference barely moves (\cref{fig:concept}c).
All twelve registered cells (two judges, two rubrics, three readouts) went as predicted, and on an exploratory pool the swap reaches 9.8 points for the smallest Qwen2.5 judges (\cref{app:rotation}). A registered near, middle, and far ordering by numeric distance from the reference failed (\cref{app:composition}). We did not run the swap test on GPT-4.1.

\paragraph{Verdict.} P1 holds for every score-only judge we swapped (Qwen2.5 1.5B to 14B, phi-4, Sonnet~5) and for Qwen2.5-7B under the MT-Bench template; it fails on arithmetic for judges that reason before scoring (\cref{sec:p3}).

\section{Prediction 2: with several correct answers, the others are graded worse}
\label{sec:p2}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig2_onevsseveral.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{With one correct answer the reference helps selection; with several, it degrades grading of the correct answers that differ from it.} Change from showing the reference (referenced minus blind, per task, 95\% intervals). \textbf{(a)}~Best-of-64 accuracy; on MATH-500 the reference shows only the final answer. \textbf{(b)}~AUROC for separating correct answers that differ from the reference from wrong answers. Constructed pools read answers in any format; \textsc{MACE} uses its answer lists. Circles: open judges on 64 candidates. Squares: Sonnet~5 on 16-candidate subsamples that always include a correct answer matching the reference, so its selection is not shown. Triangles: GPT-4.1, whose scores tie heavily. Marks as in \cref{sec:setup}.}
\label{fig:onevsseveral}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Prediction.} Correct answers that differ from the reference lose ground against wrong answers when several answers are correct; with one correct answer, matching and correctness coincide and the reference should help.

\paragraph{One correct answer.}
Showing only the final answer raises best-of-64 accuracy on MATH-500 by 28.6 and 30.6 points for Qwen2.5-7B and 14B,\reana{} as the full solution does, and on \textsc{MACE}'s single-answer questions by 29.4 and 26.5 points for Qwen2.5-7B and phi-4\ph{} (\cref{fig:onevsseveral}a). Our constructed $k$-pool cannot test this: on its 14 single-answer tasks, with answers read in any format, the reference changes no screened judge's pick on any task.\ph

\paragraph{Several correct answers.}
With answers read in any format, the reference lowers the grading AUROC of correct answers that differ from it by 0.27 and 0.24 on the replication pool (Qwen2.5-7B from 0.91 to 0.63) and by 0.17 to 0.25 on the $k$-pool at $k{>}1$ for the four screened judges\ph{} (\cref{fig:onevsseveral}b). The fall holds under every labeling we tried; keeping only boxed replies shrinks it to 0.07 to 0.14 (\cref{app:grid}).\ph{}
On \textsc{MACE}'s multi-answer questions it is 0.08 for Qwen2.5-7B and 0.06 for phi-4.\ph{}
Frontier judges show it too. Sonnet~5 loses 0.04 on the replication pool, 0.08 on the $k$-pool, and 0.15 on \textsc{MACE}, where the share of those correct answers it scores 3 or below rises by 36~pp. GPT-4.1 loses 0.04 on the replication pool and shows no reliable change on the $k$-pool (\cref{app:frontier,app:grid}).\ph

\paragraph{Verdict.} P2 holds: with several correct answers, every screened open judge and Sonnet~5 grade the others worse, on constructed and on natural questions. With one correct answer the reference helps, as every reading expects.

\section{Prediction 3: the effect survives verification}
\label{sec:p3}

\paragraph{Prediction.} Verification failure predicts that the effect vanishes on correct answers the judge already accepts without a reference; agreement predicts that it persists.

\paragraph{Answers the judge accepts without a reference.}
On 40 factual \textsc{MACE} questions, each with one correct answer that matches the reference, one that does not, and one wrong answer, Sonnet~5 (CLI) without a reference ranks both correct answers above the wrong one 94\% of the time when scoring only and 97\% with thinking on. The reference still widens the matching answer's lead by 4.0 points, and by 3.0 with thinking on (\cref{app:reasoning}).
On its API subsamples, Sonnet~5 gave 81 non-matching correct factual answers a full 10 without a reference; shown a different correct answer as the reference, it scores 19\% of them 3 or below (9\% and 15\% on the two constructed pools; \cref{app:estimator}).\ph{}
A score of 7 or above is a weaker sign of verification for the open judges, which give it to 24\% to 30\% of wrong answers.\ph{} Restricted to answers scored that high blind, the swap is unchanged ($+4.99$ for Qwen2.5-7B under the original scorer, $+4.08$ for phi-4, $+2.79$ for Sonnet~5), and Qwen2.5-7B still moves correct answers it scored 9 or above blind by 5.3 points.\ph{}
Across replication tasks its swap shrinks as its blind AUROC rises, but it is still 3.6 points in the third of tasks where that AUROC is 0.98 (\cref{app:reasoning}).\ph

\paragraph{Reasoning before scoring.}
One change removes the effect on arithmetic (\cref{fig:reasoning}a). Qwen3-14B's swap loss is 1.98 points with thinking off and 0.12 with it on, and Sonnet~5's falls from 2.85 points when it scores only to 0.20 when it explains first and 0.32 with thinking on. Qwen2.5-7B, which explains first under the MT-Bench template, still loses 3.59 points, so writing an explanation is not enough by itself.
On factual questions reasoning only shrinks the effect (\cref{fig:reasoning}b): with thinking on, false rejections of correct answers that differ from the reference still rise by 18 and 22~pp for Qwen3-14B and Qwen3-8B (42 and 44~pp with it off) and by 12.5~pp for Sonnet~5 (17.5~pp with it off).
Thinking does not act through better measured verification: on arithmetic it leaves Qwen3's blind AUROC unchanged (0.85 to 0.86 for 14B, 0.86 for 8B in both modes), although it raises the share of correct answers Qwen3-8B scores a full 10 blind from 77\% to 96\% (\cref{app:reasoning,app:estimator}).\ph{} Whether reasoning judges check answers against the task rather than the reference, or simply attend less to the reference, our data do not say.

\paragraph{How the reference is written.}
The pull comes from the literal answer. On the replication pool, writing the reference 31 as ``thirty-one'' leaves phi-4's margin almost unchanged ($+3.68$ against $+3.79$) and cuts Qwen2.5-7B's by 38\%; writing it as ``30+1'' cuts them by 77\% and 94\%; and on 21 $k$-pool tasks, hiding the final answer of a worked solution removes the margin. Sonnet~5 follows the same pattern ($+3.06$ with a numeral, $+3.14$ with words, $+0.47$ with an expression; \cref{app:mechanism,app:frontier}). These tests are arithmetic only, and a score-only judge may simply fail to evaluate ``30+1'' in one step, so they show what the judges respond to rather than separating the readings.
Telling the judge that other correct answers deserve full credit narrows the margin for phi-4 and Sonnet~5 but failed its registered test for Qwen2.5-1.5B, 3B, and 7B (\cref{app:mitigation,app:frontier}).

\paragraph{Verdict.} P3 holds for judges that score without reasoning first: they move answers they already accept, most clearly Sonnet~5 on factual questions, so the effect is not only a failure to verify. Reasoning before scoring removes it on arithmetic and shrinks it on factual questions, by a mechanism our experiments do not identify.

\paragraph{Natural data.}
On \textsc{MACE}'s multi-answer questions the reference did not lower best-of-64 accuracy ($+2.0$ for Qwen2.5-7B and $+6.1$~pp for phi-4, both intervals including zero; \cref{fig:onevsseveral}a); our registered prediction of a loss was not confirmed. Over the benchmark's natural mix, the single-answer questions dominate and the reference raises accuracy by about 10 points (\cref{tab:protocols}).

\paragraph{Constructed pools.}
With answers read in any format, the reference lowers $k$-pool accuracy at $k{>}1$ by 30.3~pp for Qwen2.5-7B and 15.2~pp for phi-4 and leaves Qwen2.5-14B and gemma-2-9b within 3 points\ph{} (\cref{fig:onevsseveral}a). The larger losses we first measured under the registered labels (15 to 36~pp for Qwen2.5-7B, Qwen2.5-14B, and gemma-2-9b\reana) were mostly prose answers counted as wrong (\cref{app:grid}).
The remaining losses sit where no correct candidate matches the reference. On $k$-pool tasks without one, Qwen2.5-7B loses 64~pp \ci{-91}{-36} and phi-4 45~pp \ci{-73}{-18} (11 tasks), against $-14$ and $0$ on the 22 tasks with one; on the replication pool Qwen2.5-7B loses 31~pp without one (16 tasks) and 5~pp with one (\cref{app:estimator}).\ph

\paragraph{Protocols.}
No protocol we tried was best everywhere (\cref{tab:protocols}). Scoring blind as well and keeping the higher score recovers blind accuracy on the $k$-pool and keeps the reference's benefit on \textsc{MACE}, but gives up 6.7 of the reference's 30.5 points on MATH-500 for Qwen2.5-7B. Scoring blind first and then revealing the reference behaves similarly, trailing the reference by 3.0 points on \textsc{MACE} for Qwen2.5-7B and losing 6.1 points on the $k$-pool for phi-4. We did not measure how these protocols grade.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Reference-guided judging.}
References are the standard remedy for grading math and reasoning with LLM judges~\citep{zheng2023judging,refguided2024,chen2026rethinking}, and judges without them over-credit wrong answers~\citep{generous2026}.
Judge models trained to take a reference as input assume it is a gold answer: JudgeLM studies how judges behave when a reference is added or removed~\citep{zhu2023judgelm}, and Prometheus conditions its rubric scores on one~\citep{kim2024prometheus}; the swap test asks what such judges do when the reference is one of several correct answers.
Judges depend on the reference being correct~\citep{judgingref2026,krumdick2025nofreelabels}, diverge from humans under reference-based grading~\citep{thakur2024judging}, and favor the reference's solution method~\citep{fixation2025}. Generating a reference before judging has been proposed as an improvement~\citep{dobefore2025}; we did not test self-generated references on multi-answer tasks.

\paragraph{Single references and many correct answers.}
Reference-based metrics in translation reward outputs that resemble the particular reference~\citep{freitag2020bleu,fomicheva2016reference}, and code evaluation moved from similarity to execution~\citep{chen2021codex}.
Open-domain question answering under-credits correct answers that are missing from the gold list or phrased differently~\citep{min2020ambigqa,si2021whatsinaname,bulian2022tomayto,kamalloo2023evaluating}; in our setting the list is complete and the judge still favors the one answer it is shown.
Verifiers in reinforcement learning miss correct answers written in a different form~\citep{xu2025tinyv,huang2025pitfalls,chen2025xverify}, as our own boxed-answer checker did.

\paragraph{Judge biases and optimization.}
LLM judges favor the first response~\citep{wang2024fair}, longer responses~\citep{dubois2024lengthcontrolled}, their own outputs~\citep{panickssery2024llm}, and scores shown to them~\citep{kapetanovic2026anchoring}. Optimizing against an imperfect evaluator inflates its scores~\citep{gao2022scaling,rafailov2024scaling}, and good average agreement does not guarantee good best-of-$n$ choices~\citep{landesberg2026bestofn}. A judge that commits to its own answer before seeing the candidate resists reward hacking~\citep{zhou2026convincing}.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Labels.} The registered checkers counted most prose answers as wrong. The any-format labels used in the main text are post hoc, agree with a model's reading on 88\% of unboxed replies, and were not checked against human labels. \textsc{MACE}'s answer lists miss 2.3\% to 8.1\% of correct answers.
\item \textbf{Tasks.} Most of our evidence is from constructed arithmetic tasks with one generator; our natural-data evidence is 133 \textsc{MACE} River and Language questions.
\item \textbf{Judges and prompts.} All open judges have at most 14B parameters, and Sonnet~5 and GPT-4.1 are our only frontier judges. Sonnet~5's subsamples were drawn to contain every kind of answer, so its AUROCs are not directly comparable to the 64-candidate open-judge values, and its reasoning and grading results come from different interfaces. Every referenced prompt called the reference ``trusted'', which may strengthen deference.
\item \textbf{Mechanism.} No experiment manipulates verification directly, and the tests of how the reference is written are arithmetic only, so we cannot say why reasoning removes the effect on arithmetic.
\item \textbf{Downstream.} Three DPO runs against the referenced judge did not show the policy copying the reference, and the protected arm failed its manipulation check, so we have not shown that training exploits the bias (\cref{app:dpo}).
\item \textbf{Registration.} Most registrations rely on timestamped commits, and the uniform verdict rule was applied retroactively to earlier predictions after most of their outcomes were known (\cref{app:ledger}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

A reference answer is a good check when a question has one right answer.
When it has several, judges favor the one they are shown: swapping it moves the score of unchanged text by 3 to 5 points, and correct answers that differ from it become harder to tell from wrong ones, including answers the judge accepts without a reference.
Reasoning before scoring removes the effect on arithmetic but not on factual questions, and best-of-64 selection loses accuracy mainly when no candidate matches the reference.
Where correct answers can differ, a low reference-guided score on an answer that differs from the reference is not evidence that the answer is wrong. Score such answers without the reference as well: on our multi-answer pools, keeping the higher of the two scores lost no accuracy for the open judges relative to the better of blind and referenced judging, and on unique-answer math it cost up to 6.7 points (\cref{tab:protocols}).

\subsection*{AI use statement}
Generative AI models were the experimental subjects: open and frontier LLMs generated candidate answers and served as the judges under study (\cref{sec:setup}, \cref{app:setup}). One pilot (\cref{app:mathconstraint}) used a Claude model to generate candidate solutions, each verified by exact checks, and Qwen2.5-14B read unboxed replies for one labeling. We used generative AI tools to help write analysis code, draft and edit text, produce figures, search and verify related work, audit the preregistration ledger, and label a sample of dataset questions for the prevalence audit; both labeling passes came from one model family, were adjudicated against each other, and were not checked by human annotators. Every number in the paper is produced by committed analysis scripts from stored model outputs, and we checked every claim against those outputs. We take responsibility for the final content of this work.

\subsection*{Ethics statement}
This work studies a failure of automated evaluation that can make models look better or worse than they are. We release no new model and no attack on a deployed system. A model optimized against a reference-guided judge on multi-answer tasks may be rewarded for matching the reference rather than for being correct, which is why we pair the failure with measurements of when it matters. No human subjects were involved.

\subsection*{Reproducibility statement}
\Cref{app:setup} specifies every pool, judge, prompt, and scorer, and \cref{app:ledger} lists each prediction with its registration type and outcome. The anonymized supplementary material contains task generators and checkers, candidate pools, the answer labelings, all judge score files, the analysis scripts that produce every number, table, and figure, and the registration documents with commit hashes replaced by stable tokens.
