\title{The Reference Is Not an Answer Key:\\LLM Judges Favor the Correct Answer They Are Shown}

\author{Anonymous authors\\Paper under double-blind review}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
LLM judges grade benchmarks, pick among sampled answers, and score training rollouts, often guided by a reference answer on the premise that it acts as an answer key: a correct response should score the same whichever correct answer is shown.
Many questions have several correct answers, and on them the premise fails: judges favor the correct answer they are shown.
We distinguish three readings of a reference (an answer key, the only evidence for a judge that cannot verify, or an answer the judge rewards for matching), formalize them in a simple model, and test two predictions that rule out the answer key and one that separates the other two.
Swapping the reference between two correct answers moves the score of byte-identical text by 2.7 to 5.4 points out of 10, and correct answers that differ from the reference are rejected far more often (28\% against 0.4\% for Qwen2.5-7B), although a reference still helps when the answer is unique.
The effect is not only a failure to verify: Claude Sonnet~5 penalizes answers it scores 10 without a reference, a score it rarely gives to wrong answers.
It is small for GPT-4.1 and near zero on arithmetic when the judge thinks before scoring, but persists on factual questions.
We test on program-enumerated tasks and public factual, math, puzzle, and code benchmarks, with 156 registered predictions, all reported.
On such questions a low reference-guided score is weak evidence of error; in a post hoc analysis, scoring with and without the reference and keeping the higher score removed the grading harm.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Ask for a two-digit number that is divisible by 5 and whose digits sum to 8.
Both 35 and 80 are correct.
Qwen2.5-7B, shown a candidate that reasons its way to 80, scores it 10.0 out of 10 with no reference, 2.9 when the reference answer is 35, and 9.3 when the reference is 80 (\cref{fig:concept}b).
The text never changed; only the correct answer the judge was shown did.
This example swaps more than most, but over 120 such tasks the same judge moves correct text by about 5 points in either direction (\cref{fig:concept}c).

Reference-guided judging is standard practice.
MT-Bench's math and reasoning template tells the judge to begin ``by comparing the assistant's answer with the reference answer''~\citep{zheng2023judging}, references raise agreement with human labels~\citep{refguided2024}, judges without one are too generous~\citep{generous2026}, and comparison against a reference is the most reliable single-pass strategy for grading math~\citep{chen2026rethinking}.
The same judges grade benchmarks, choose among sampled answers, and serve as reward signals, which optimization exploits~\citep{gao2022scaling}.
The practice is safe when a question has one correct answer, as on curated math benchmarks: our LLM-labeled audit of 200 questions per dataset (not checked by humans) found 0\% to 2.5\% with several on MATH-500, GSM8K, and DeepScaleR (\cref{app:prevalence}).
Whether it is safe where answers are not unique has not been tested directly for LLM judges, although such questions are common: 30\% of NQ-open questions in the same audit (18.5\% excluding answers that changed over time), benchmarks built around several valid answers such as \textsc{RewardBench-2} Ties and the factual benchmark \textsc{MACE}~\citep{malik2025rewardbench2,mace2026}, and tasks that ask for an example or a construction.

\paragraph{Three readings of a reference.}
If the reference is an \emph{answer key}, the judge checks a candidate against every correct answer, and a correct answer scores the same whichever one is shown.
Under \emph{verification failure}, the judge cannot tell on its own that a different answer is correct, so the reference is its only evidence.
Under \emph{agreement}, the judge favors the correct answer that matches the reference even when it can recognize the others, whether because it rewards agreement, because it assumes one intended answer, or because the prompt tells it to use the reference; we separate the last of these only partly (\cref{sec:p3}).
We test three predictions. P1 and P2 rule out the answer key; only P3 separates the other two readings.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=2pt]
\item[\textbf{P1}] \textbf{The score follows the reference.} Swapping which correct answer is the reference moves the score of unchanged text (\cref{sec:p1}).
\item[\textbf{P2}] \textbf{With several correct answers, the others are graded worse.} Correct answers that differ from the reference lose ground against wrong answers; with one correct answer, every reading allows the reference to help (\cref{sec:p2}).
\item[\textbf{P3}] \textbf{The effect survives verification.} It persists on correct answers the judge already accepts without a reference, which verification failure rules out (\cref{sec:p3}).
\end{itemize}
\Cref{tab:summary} gives what we observed. P1 holds for every score-only judge we swapped and P2 for every score-only judge we tested, although the grading harm is small for GPT-4.1; P3 holds for Sonnet~5, the judge on which we could test acceptance directly. A thinking mode removes the effect on arithmetic but only reduces it on factual questions (\cref{sec:p3}). Keeping the higher of the blind and referenced scores kept grading at blind level on our multi-answer pools, at a cost when the answer is unique (\cref{sec:cost}).

Prior work comes close without isolating this failure.
\citet{fixation2025} name ``solution fixation'' but measure it with flawed solutions rewritten in the reference's style; \citet{judgingref2026} and \citet{krumdick2025nofreelabels} replace the reference with a wrong one; \citet{kapetanovic2026anchoring} show judges anchor on scores they are shown; and human raters shown different reference translations favor candidates close to the one they see~\citep{fomicheva2016reference}. Benchmarks with several valid answers score judges against all of them but do not vary which one the judge is shown, and no study of LLM judges that we know of does.

\section{A model of the three readings}
\label{sec:model}

Let $A^\star$ be the set of correct answers to a question and $a^\star \in A^\star$ the one shown as the reference. Let $b(c)$ be a judge's blind score for candidate $c$, $s(c; a^\star)$ its referenced score, and $v_c \in [0, 1]$ how reliably the judge recognizes $c$ as correct without a reference. We write
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
s(c; a^\star) \;\approx\;& \beta\, b(c) \;+\; \kappa\, \mathbb{I}[\,\mathrm{ans}(c) \in A^\star\,] \;+\; \lambda(v_c)\, \mathbb{I}[\,\mathrm{ans}(c) = a^\star\,] \\
&+\; \gamma\, \mathbb{I}[\,c \text{ is wrong and contains } a^\star\,] \;+\; \varepsilon .
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:model}
\end{equation}
An answer key checks candidates against the whole set: $\kappa \ge 0$ and $\lambda = \gamma = 0$. The other two readings reward matching the one answer shown; since only differences in score matter, the bonus $\lambda$ can equally be a penalty on correct answers that do not match. Under verification failure the bonus vanishes once the judge can recognize the answer itself ($\lambda(v) = \lambda_0(1-v)$); under agreement it does not ($\lambda(1) > 0$).
With one correct answer, $A^\star = \{a^\star\}$ and the $\kappa$ and $\lambda$ terms coincide, so every reading allows the reference to help (P2$'$).
With several, both non-key readings predict P1: swapping $a^\star$ moves fixed text by about $\lambda$. P2 needs more than P1, because a bonus for matching answers does not change how non-matching correct answers rank against wrong ones. It needs one of two further effects: the referenced score keeps only part of the judge's own assessment ($\beta < 1$), so noise weighs more, or wrong answers that contain $a^\star$ collect part of the bonus ($\gamma > 0$).
The two non-key readings differ only where $v_c$ is high, which P3 tests. We take a blind score of 10 as the judge accepting an answer, and per-task blind AUROC (\cref{sec:setup}) as a coarser measure of how well it verifies.
\Cref{eq:model} organizes the predictions; it is not a fitted model (a fit, with its caveats, is in \cref{app:composition}).

\section{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Tasks.}
Our own tasks come from five constraint families (remainder systems, subset sums, Goldbach pairs, divisor counts, digit sums); each stores its full set of correct answers and one member, drawn at random, as the reference.
The \emph{replication pool} (120 tasks) was built to replicate an exploratory observation on \textsc{RewardBench-2} Ties (\cref{app:ties}), and the \emph{$k$-pool} has 75 tasks with exactly $k \in \{1,2,4,8,16\}$ correct answers.
\textsc{MACE} contributes factual questions with 1, 2, 4, or 6 correct answers from its River and Language domains, whose answer lists miss 2.3\% and 8.1\% of correct answers (three other domains miss 31\% to 44\% and were excluded); the reference is drawn at random from each list, and answers are matched to it by normalized string containment (\cref{app:mace}). MATH-500~\citep{hendrycks2021math} supplies unique answers.
Two smaller studies use MathConstraint's solver-verified puzzles (\cref{app:mathconstraint}) and MBPP programs checked by their tests (\cref{app:code}).
Selection analyses use the tasks whose pass rate lies in $[0.2, 0.8]$, where selection can change correctness; for \textsc{MACE} we follow its registration and use $[0.05, 0.95]$ (133 tasks).

\paragraph{Candidates and judges.}
Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct writes 64 samples per task at temperature 1 (Qwen2.5-7B for MATH-500).
Each sample is judged \emph{blind}, from the question and the candidate, and \emph{referenced}, with the reference under the heading ``Reference answer (trusted)'', under two rubric wordings.
The core judges are four open models that output only a score, Qwen2.5-7B, phi-4, Qwen2.5-14B, and gemma-2-9b, whose score is the expected value over the score tokens (\cref{app:setup}); Claude Sonnet~5 through its API; and GPT-4.1, whose scores tie too often to rank candidates, so we report only its grading AUROC. The four open judges passed a pre-specified score-resolution screen that Mistral-7B failed (\cref{app:mitigation}).
To study reasoning, Qwen3-8B and Qwen3-14B write before a final ``Score: N'' line with the thinking mode off (\emph{explanation first}) or on, as does Qwen2.5-7B under the verbatim MT-Bench template; Sonnet~5 does so through its command-line interface (CLI). Sonnet~5's two interfaces agree exactly on only 67\% of shared calls, so we compare its results only within one.

\paragraph{Reading answers.}
The prompt asks for the final answer inside \texttt{\textbackslash boxed\{\}}, and our registered checkers read it from there, so a correct answer written in prose usually counted as wrong; 38\% to 43\% of replies ignore the format.
On the constructed pools the main text therefore uses \emph{any-format labels}, which credit the final answer a reply states in any format; these were registered for Sonnet~5 and are post hoc for the open judges. The registered labels, a labeling in which Qwen2.5-14B reads each unboxed reply (it agrees with the any-format rule on 88\% of them), and boxed replies alone give the same direction for every grading result of the core judges (\cref{app:grid}).

\paragraph{Measurements and conventions.}
The \emph{swap change} is the change in score of identical text when the reference moves. The \emph{false-rejection rate} is the share of correct answers that differ from the reference scored 3 or below, and the \emph{grading AUROC} is the per-task AUROC for separating those answers from wrong answers (with one correct answer, all correct answers against wrong ones). \emph{Best-of-64 accuracy} is the correctness of the highest-scored candidate, averaged over ties. The \emph{margin} is the mean score of correct answers that match the reference minus that of correct answers that do not. All changes are referenced minus blind, per task, with task-level bootstrap intervals, in points out of 10 for scores and percentage points (pp) for rates and accuracies. Unless stated otherwise, constructed pools use any-format labels, open judges use an exact expected score (\cref{app:estimator}), and Sonnet~5 results come from its API. We do not correct for multiplicity.
Our OSF-preregistered primary endpoint, best-of-64 inflation, was confirmed ($+1.19$~\sd) and survives relabeling ($+1.14$~\sd\ph; \cref{app:grid}); we lead with measures that are easier to interpret.
Marks apply to effect estimates: unmarked estimates were pre-specified in a timestamped commit or on OSF, \ph{} marks post hoc estimates, and \reana{} marks pre-specified re-analyses of an effect whose direction was already known; descriptive statistics such as format compliance and the prevalence audit are post hoc. \Cref{app:ledger} lists all 156 registered predictions: 92 confirmed, 10 not confirmed, 15 falsified, 16 split across judges, and 23 not evaluable, not scored, or excluded.

\section{Prediction 1: the score follows the reference}
\label{sec:p1}

We re-judged the replication pool's byte-identical samples with the reference moved to another correct answer.
Text the reference moves onto gains 5.25 points under Qwen2.5-7B,\reana{} 4.09 under phi-4, and 3.04 under Sonnet~5; text it moves off loses 5.41,\reana{} 3.92, and 2.66; correct text that matches neither reference barely moves (\cref{fig:concept}c). In the terms of \cref{eq:model}, $\lambda$ is 3 to 5 points for these judges; the second rubric wording gives 2.5 to 6.6 (\cref{app:rotation}).
All twelve registered cells (two judges, two rubric wordings, three readouts) went as predicted. A registered near, middle, and far ordering by numeric distance from the reference failed (\cref{app:composition}). We did not run the swap test on GPT-4.1.

\paragraph{Beyond arithmetic.}
On 9 MathConstraint all-interval-series puzzles with generated solutions, swapping the reference between two valid solutions moves a fixed candidate by 3.2 to 5.0 points for Qwen2.5-7B and phi-4 and 4.4 for Sonnet~5 (\cref{app:mathconstraint}). For MBPP code, a canonical program shown as the reference added no best-of-64 inflation, but correct programs have few distinct outputs, which leaves the effect little room (\cref{app:code}).

\paragraph{What the judge responds to.}
The pull depends on how explicitly the reference states its answer. Spelled out in words (``thirty-one''), it keeps most of its effect: Qwen2.5-7B's margin falls by 38\% and phi-4's barely. Written as an expression (``30+1''), it loses most of it, 94\% and 77\%, and hidden inside a worked solution it loses all of it (21 $k$-pool tasks). Sonnet~5 shows the same pattern ($+3.06$ with a numeral, $+3.14$ with words, $+0.47$ with an expression; \cref{app:mechanism,app:frontier}). These tests are arithmetic only.

\paragraph{Verdict.} P1 holds for every score-only judge we swapped and for open judges that explain first. On arithmetic it nearly vanishes with a thinking mode, and for Sonnet~5 already when it explains first (\cref{sec:p3}).

\section{Prediction 2: with several correct answers, the others are graded worse}
\label{sec:p2}

\paragraph{Several correct answers.}
Correct answers that differ from the reference are rejected more often. On the replication pool Qwen2.5-7B scores 28\% of them 3 or below with the reference and 0.4\% blind; across the four open judges (two run on both constructed pools, two on the $k$-pool only) the rise ranges from 7 to 53~pp and changes by about 3~pp or less across the four labelings (\cref{app:grid}).\ph{}
Their grading AUROC against wrong answers falls by 0.17 to 0.27, from 0.84--0.93 blind to 0.63--0.72 with the reference;\ph{} counting only boxed replies, the fall is 0.07 to 0.14.\ph{} On \textsc{MACE}'s multi-answer questions it falls by 0.08 for Qwen2.5-7B and 0.06\ph{} for phi-4, to 0.66 and 0.73 (\cref{fig:onevsseveral}b).
For Sonnet~5 it falls by 0.04, 0.08, and 0.15 on the replication pool, $k$-pool, and \textsc{MACE}, where its false rejections rise by 36~pp.
GPT-4.1's AUROC falls by only 0.04 on the replication pool and does not change reliably on the $k$-pool, possibly because its scores tie heavily, although its false rejections on the replication pool rise by 6~pp, as phi-4's do (\cref{app:frontier,app:grid}).\ph

\paragraph{One correct answer.}
With one correct answer the reference helps, as every reading allows. Showing only the final answer raises best-of-64 accuracy on MATH-500 by 28.6 and 30.6~pp for Qwen2.5-7B and 14B,\reana{} as the full solution does, and on \textsc{MACE}'s single-answer questions by 29.4 and 26.5~pp\ph{} for Qwen2.5-7B and phi-4, whose grading AUROC there rises by 0.29 and 0.23\ph{} (\cref{fig:onevsseveral}). Our $k$-pool cannot test this: on its single-answer tasks the reference changes the correctness of no open judge's pick under any-format labels, and an apparent 21-pp loss for Qwen2.5-7B under the registered labels came from prose answers counted as wrong.\ph

\paragraph{Verdict.} P2 holds for the four open judges and Sonnet~5 on constructed pools, and for Qwen2.5-7B, phi-4, and Sonnet~5 on \textsc{MACE}; for GPT-4.1 the fall in AUROC is small. With one correct answer the reference helps.

\section{Prediction 3: the effect survives verification}
\label{sec:p3}

\paragraph{Answers the judge accepts without a reference.}
On 40 factual \textsc{MACE} questions with one candidate of each kind (a correct answer that matches the reference, one that does not, and a wrong answer), Sonnet~5 (CLI) ranks both correct answers above the wrong one 94\% of the time without thinking and 97\% with it, yet the reference widens the margin between the two correct answers by 4.0 and 3.0 points (\cref{app:reasoning}).
This registered test measures ranking rather than acceptance, so we add a direct post hoc test on the API subsamples, which include wrong answers. Sonnet~5 gives a blind 10 to 2\% to 4\% of wrong answers there, so a blind 10 is close to verification. Of the 81 non-matching correct factual answers it scored 10 blind, it scores 19\% at 3 or below once shown a different correct answer, and none of the 38 matching answers it scored 10 blind; on the constructed pools the rates are 9\% and 15\%, again against none of the matching answers. The drop is not score noise: on the replication pool a second blind call scored none of 111 such answers 3 or below, against 12 once the reference was shown. That call went through the CLI, whose scores run 0.38 points lower, which if anything adds low scores; it covers the 111 of 150 answers the interrupted CLI run reached (\cref{app:estimator,app:frontier}).\ph{}
For Qwen2.5-7B, the swap shrinks as its blind AUROC rises across replication tasks but is still 3.6 points in the third of tasks where that AUROC averages 0.98;\ph{} this ranks answers rather than showing acceptance (\cref{app:reasoning}).

\paragraph{Not only the prompt.}
Our referenced prompts call the reference trusted and tell the judge to use it. The verbatim MT-Bench template drops ``trusted'' but still asks the judge to compare against the reference, and to explain first; under it Qwen2.5-7B still loses 3.59 points on the swap. Our only test of the instruction itself adds a sentence saying that any correct answer deserves full credit even if it differs from the reference. With it, Sonnet~5 keeps a margin of $+2.08$ \ci{+1.14}{+3.14} on the $k$-pool, against $+3.00$ without it; the sentence closes phi-4's margin on one synthetic pool but failed its registered test for Qwen2.5-1.5B, 3B, and 7B (\cref{app:frontier,app:mitigation}).

\paragraph{A thinking mode.}
On arithmetic, a thinking mode shrinks the effect to near zero (\cref{fig:reasoning}a): Qwen3-14B's swap loss is 1.98 points with thinking off and 0.12 with it on (Qwen3-8B: 0.69 and $+0.01$), and Sonnet~5's falls from 2.85 points when it scores only to 0.32 with thinking. Explaining first is enough for Sonnet~5 (0.20) but not for Qwen2.5-7B under the MT-Bench template (3.59) or for Qwen3-14B (1.98). With thinking on, Qwen3-8B gives 96\% of correct arithmetic answers a 10 blind and keeps that score when the reference moves off them, so a score ceiling does not explain the missing loss (\cref{app:reasoning}).
On factual questions, thinking roughly halves the effect for Qwen3: false rejections rise by 22 and 18~pp with it on against 44 and 42~pp with it off (\cref{fig:reasoning}b). For Sonnet~5 the change in margin is not distinguishable from zero.
Thinking does not change blind AUROC on arithmetic (0.85 to 0.86). On factual questions it raises it (Qwen3-14B: 0.72 to 0.81), so better verification may contribute there (\cref{app:estimator}). Why thinking shrinks the effect on arithmetic, our data do not say.

\paragraph{Verdict.} P3 holds for Sonnet~5 on factual questions: a registered ranking test, with and without thinking, and a post hoc acceptance test with a noise floor both show the penalty, so the effect is not only a failure to verify. For Qwen2.5-7B on arithmetic the evidence is consistent with agreement but is not an acceptance test. Where thinking removes the effect, P3 cannot be tested.

\paragraph{Natural data.}
On \textsc{MACE}'s multi-answer questions the reference did not lower best-of-64 accuracy (changes of $+2.0$~pp for Qwen2.5-7B\ph{} and $+6.1$~pp for phi-4, both intervals including zero; \cref{fig:onevsseveral}a), and our registered prediction of a loss was not confirmed. The gains of 26 to 29~pp on the 34 single-answer questions outweigh the null on the 99 multi-answer ones, so over the natural mix the reference raises accuracy by 9 to 11~pp (\cref{tab:protocols}).

\paragraph{Constructed pools.}
On the $k$-pool at $k{>}1$ the reference changes accuracy by $-30.3$~pp for Qwen2.5-7B and $-15.2$~pp for phi-4, whose interval reaches zero, and by no more than 3~pp for Qwen2.5-14B and gemma-2-9b\ph{} (\cref{tab:protocols}).
The losses sit where no correct candidate matches the reference. On the 11 $k$-pool tasks without one, accuracy changes by $-64$~pp \ci{-91}{-36} for Qwen2.5-7B, $-45$~pp \ci{-73}{-18} for phi-4, $-18$~pp \ci{-46}{+18} for Qwen2.5-14B, and $-9$~pp \ci{-27}{0} for gemma-2-9b, against at most $-14$~pp on the 22 tasks with one; on the replication pool Qwen2.5-7B's changes by $-31$~pp without one (16 tasks) and $-5$~pp with one (\cref{app:estimator}).\ph

\paragraph{A safeguard and its costs.}
Keeping the higher of the blind and referenced scores (the \emph{max rule}) keeps grading at blind level on both multi-answer pools: grading AUROC stays within 0.03 of blind for every open judge, where showing the reference lowers it by 0.06 to 0.25 (\cref{tab:protocols}).\ph{} This is partly by construction, since the reference lowers the scores of both groups that this AUROC compares, so the informative results are the costs. The max rule recovers blind accuracy on the $k$-pool and keeps the reference's accuracy on \textsc{MACE}. With a unique answer it gives back part of the reference's benefit: 4.8~pp of accuracy and 0.05 of AUROC for Qwen2.5-7B when the reference is a bare answer, 6.7~pp and 0.15 when it is a full solution, and 0.07 of AUROC on \textsc{MACE}'s single-answer questions.\ph{} For Sonnet~5 on its 16-candidate $k$-pool subsamples, the max rule's accuracy was 2.4~pp \ci{-5.0}{-0.5} below blind, about as much as the reference's own 2.6~pp \ci{-5.8}{+0.4} (\cref{app:frontier}).\ph{} The registered blind-then-reveal protocol protected selection but not grading, and showing several correct references largely failed for Qwen2.5-7B on one synthetic pool (\cref{app:mitigation}).

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Reference-guided judging.}
References are the standard remedy for grading math and reasoning with LLM judges~\citep{zheng2023judging,refguided2024,chen2026rethinking}, and judges without them over-credit wrong answers~\citep{generous2026}.
Judge models trained to take a reference as input assume it is a gold answer: JudgeLM studies how judges behave when a reference is added or removed~\citep{zhu2023judgelm}, and Prometheus conditions its rubric scores on one~\citep{kim2024prometheus}; the swap test asks what such judges do when the reference is one of several correct answers.
Judges depend on the reference being correct~\citep{judgingref2026,krumdick2025nofreelabels}, diverge from humans under reference-based grading~\citep{thakur2024judging}, and favor the reference's solution method~\citep{fixation2025}. Generating a reference before judging has been proposed as an improvement~\citep{dobefore2025}; we did not test self-generated references on multi-answer tasks.

\paragraph{Single references and many correct answers.}
Reference-based metrics in translation reward outputs that resemble the particular reference~\citep{freitag2020bleu,fomicheva2016reference}, and code evaluation moved from similarity to execution~\citep{chen2021codex}.
Open-domain question answering under-credits correct answers that are missing from the gold list or phrased differently~\citep{min2020ambigqa,si2021whatsinaname,bulian2022tomayto,kamalloo2023evaluating}; in our setting the list is complete and the judge still favors the one answer it is shown.
Verifiers in reinforcement learning miss correct answers written in a different form~\citep{xu2025tinyv,huang2025pitfalls,chen2025xverify}, as our own boxed-answer checker did.

\paragraph{Judge biases and optimization.}
LLM judges favor the first response~\citep{wang2024fair}, longer responses~\citep{dubois2024lengthcontrolled}, their own outputs~\citep{panickssery2024llm}, and scores shown to them~\citep{kapetanovic2026anchoring}; broader catalogues of judge biases~\citep{koo2024benchmarking,ye2025justice} do not vary which correct answer the judge is shown. Optimizing against an imperfect evaluator inflates its scores~\citep{gao2022scaling,rafailov2024scaling}, and good average agreement does not guarantee good best-of-$n$ choices~\citep{landesberg2026bestofn}. A judge that commits to its own answer before seeing the candidate resists reward hacking~\citep{zhou2026convincing}.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Labels.} The registered checkers counted most prose answers as wrong. The any-format labels used in the main text agree with a model's reading on 88\% of unboxed replies and were not checked against human labels; boxed replies alone give the conservative grading estimates. \textsc{MACE}'s answer lists miss 2.3\% to 8.1\% of correct answers, and matching by normalized string containment can miscount aliases (\cref{app:mace}).
\item \textbf{Tasks.} The constructed pools come from five arithmetic families with one generator, and the natural data from two \textsc{MACE} domains (133 questions); the puzzle and code studies are small, and open-domain question answering such as NQ-open is untested.
\item \textbf{Judges and prompts.} All open judges have at most 14B parameters, and GPT-4.1, one of our two frontier judges, shows a much smaller fall in grading AUROC. Our referenced prompts called the reference trusted and told the judge to use it; only the full-credit sentence tests that instruction directly (\cref{sec:p3}).
\item \textbf{Mechanism and protocols.} No experiment manipulates verification directly, so we cannot say why a thinking mode shrinks the effect on arithmetic. The max rule was chosen post hoc from five score combinations and evaluated only on stored scores: four open judges, and Sonnet~5 on 16-candidate subsamples.
\item \textbf{Downstream.} Three DPO runs did not show a policy learning to copy the reference, and the protected arm failed its manipulation check (\cref{app:dpo}).
\item \textbf{Registration.} Most registrations rely on timestamped commits, and the uniform verdict rule was applied retroactively to earlier predictions after most of their outcomes were known (\cref{app:ledger}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

A reference answer is a good check when a question has one right answer.
When it has several, judges that score without a thinking mode favor the one they are shown: swapping it moves the score of unchanged text by 2.7 to 5.4 points, correct answers that differ from it are rejected far more often, and for Sonnet~5 this happens even for answers it accepts without a reference.
A low reference-guided score on such an answer is therefore weak evidence of error: for the four open judges, grading AUROC for these answers falls from 0.74--0.93 blind to 0.63--0.73 with the reference.
Keeping the higher of the blind and referenced scores kept grading at blind level on our multi-answer pools, at a cost when the answer is unique. Selection suffers mainly when no correct candidate matches the reference, and a thinking mode removes the effect on arithmetic but only reduces it on factual questions.

\subsection*{AI use statement}
Generative AI models were the experimental subjects: open and frontier LLMs generated candidate answers and served as the judges under study (\cref{sec:setup}, \cref{app:setup}). One pilot (\cref{app:mathconstraint}) used a Claude model to generate candidate solutions, each verified by exact checks, and Qwen2.5-14B read unboxed replies for one labeling. We used generative AI tools to help write analysis code, draft and edit text, produce figures, search and verify related work, audit the preregistration ledger, and label a sample of dataset questions for the prevalence audit; both labeling passes came from one model family, were adjudicated against each other, and were not checked by human annotators. Every number in the paper is produced by committed analysis scripts from stored model outputs, and we checked every claim against those outputs. We take responsibility for the final content of this work.

\subsection*{Ethics statement}
This work studies a failure of automated evaluation that can make models look better or worse than they are. We release no new model and no attack on a deployed system. A model optimized against a reference-guided judge on multi-answer tasks may be rewarded for matching the reference rather than for being correct, although our three training runs did not show a policy learning to copy the reference (\cref{app:dpo}); we therefore pair the failure with measurements of when it matters. No human subjects were involved.

\subsection*{Reproducibility statement}
\Cref{app:setup} specifies every pool, judge, prompt, and scorer, and \cref{app:ledger} lists each prediction with its registration type and outcome. The anonymized supplementary material contains task generators and checkers, candidate pools, the answer labelings, all judge score files, the analysis scripts that produce every number, table, and figure, and the registration documents with commit hashes replaced by stable tokens.
