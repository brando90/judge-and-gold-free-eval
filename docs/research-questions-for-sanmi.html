Koyejo group · judge-free evaluation

Which question is worth going after?

Our study is essentially done. A cheap, automatic way of scoring answers ranks whole models well but cannot grade an individual answer, and we can prove why: 89 to 96 percent of the per-answer score reflects how predictable the reference text is, not how good the answer is. The signal tracks resemblance to the reference's style, not reasoning quality.

Five questions follow, in no particular order. Under each: the closest prior work and where it stops short, checked in a literature pass.

scaling law

As AI gets more capable than the methods we use to check it, does our ability to catch its mistakes break down in a predictable way?

If we can plot that breakdown as a curve, we can tell which checking methods will still hold up as models keep improving, instead of only comparing them on the models we have now.

Closest prior work. Engels et al. 2025 (arXiv:2504.18530) already plots oversight success against the capability gap, but on game Elo; Agent Score Difference (arXiv:2504.03731) is a single score at parity, not a curve. Our edge: the optimization-pressure-over-time axis, and real math, code, and agentic capability rather than games.

looks right vs is right

When we grade AI answers cheaply and automatically, are we measuring whether the answer looks right, or whether it is right?

Our result suggests cheap graders, including using one AI to judge another, mostly reward answers that resemble the reference, not answers that are actually better.

Closest prior work. JudgeBench (arXiv:2410.12784) shows judges failing at objective correctness; ODIN (arXiv:2402.07319) and Length-Controlled AlpacaEval (arXiv:2404.04475) each remove one bias. None reports the surface-nuisance fraction of the score as the headline, which our decomposition does.

checking vs doing

Is telling a good answer from a bad one really easier than producing a good answer in the first place?

We found that judging plain correctness is easy but judging quality is not, so the common assumption that checking is easier than doing may only hold for right-or-wrong tasks.

Closest prior work. Weaver (arXiv:2506.18203), Generator-Validator Consistency (arXiv:2310.01846), and "Can LLMs Judge Better Than They Generate?" (arXiv:2606.28050) all study the gap for correctness only. Restricting it to quality among already-correct answers appears open.

gaming the grader

If we train an AI to score well on a cheap evaluator, does that evaluator stop being trustworthy, and can we tell in advance which ones will break?

An evaluator that looks accurate can fall apart once a model is optimized to satisfy it (reward hacking). The open question is which signals survive that pressure.

Closest prior work. Scaling Laws for Reward Model Overoptimization (arXiv:2210.10760) predicts hacking magnitude from optimization, not from a static property; Skalse et al. (arXiv:2209.13085) prove unhackability is near-impossible. Predicting hackability in advance from a measurable surface property appears unclaimed.

no right answer

For open-ended work with no clear right answer, can anything cheap judge quality, or do we always need a human?

Essays, research, and long agent tasks have no gold answer to compare against, so it is unknown whether any low-cost signal tracks quality at all.

Closest prior work. BARTScore (arXiv:2106.11520), CCS (arXiv:2212.03827), and Self-Consistency (arXiv:2203.11171) give label-free signals, but for constrained or convergent tasks. Whether any survives into open-ended, answer-free work, or whether human judgment is the floor, is unposed.

The spine: why these hang together

Across all five, one measurable quantity keeps recurring: how much of an evaluator's score is surface nuisance rather than model-invariant quality. No prior work defines that fraction and shows it predicts both out-of-distribution collapse and hackability (closest: Inside the Unfair Judge, arXiv:2607.11871; Singhal et al. length correlations, arXiv:2310.03716; Eisenstein et al. Helping or Herding, arXiv:2312.09244). That unifying predictor is the open gap, and it is a mechanism rather than a trained metric, which makes it hard to game.
